# Chapter 0: Prologue: What the Ground Keeps

---

Before the story begins, someone is already ending it.

---

The woman's name was Yanu, and she had been awake since before the birds.

She sat at the water's edge in the dark before dawn, the copper pendant in both hands. It was the size of her palm, worked into a shape that was almost a face — two eyes, a mouth curved downward, the edge hammered thin enough to catch light that was not yet there. A Hopewell trader had brought it north along the river two seasons past, and she had given him three beaver pelts and a clay vessel she had made herself, and she had not regretted it once, which was unusual for her. She regretted most things eventually.

She had made it for Taya. Her daughter had lived long enough to hold it once, both small hands curled around the copper, eyes wide at the weight of it. That was the last good day. The fever came that night and did not leave for six days, and on the seventh day Taya did not wake, and the pendant had been Yanu's to carry ever since.

She had carried it for three years. She was done carrying it.

The birchbark was already cut and folded — she had done that part yesterday, while Ranu was telling the children the story of the first river, his voice rising and falling in the patterns she had heard so many times she heard them in her sleep. She had sat with the bark in her lap and said nothing and thought about the mound, which was two hundred paces from the water, which her people had been building for two generations, which was not finished and would not be finished in her lifetime and that was the point of it.

She wrapped the pendant in the bark. She pressed the fold flat with her thumb. She tied it with a strip of inner bark, three knots, the way her mother had taught her to tie things that needed to stay tied.

The river moved. The dark above it was beginning to separate into sky and tree. A heron passed low over the water, its wings making no sound, its reflection making no sound, the whole of it so quiet that Yanu felt she was watching something that was not meant to be watched.

She stood. Her knees were stiff from the cold ground. She walked the two hundred paces to the mound.

It was not tall yet — the height of two men, perhaps, the slope gentle, the top not yet flat. Her grandfather had laid the first courses of earth. Her mother had added to it. She had added to it herself, carrying basket after basket of the dark river soil up the slope on days when the work felt like prayer and days when it felt like nothing at all. Her children would add to it. Their children would add to it. The mound would outlast every name she knew, and that was the point.

She climbed to the top. The earth was cold and damp under her hands. She found the place she had chosen — the center of the northern slope, where the soil was deepest — and she dug with the antler pick she had brought, three hands deep, four, until she reached the layer that had not been disturbed since her grandfather's time.

She placed the wrapped pendant in the hole.

She covered it.

She packed the earth down with her palms, pressing firmly, the way you pressed a wound.

She stood up and looked out over the water, which was gray now, the sky beginning to go pink at its far edge. Somewhere behind her, she heard Ranu's voice — he was awake, telling a story to whoever would listen, because Ranu told stories the way other people breathed, continuously and without particular intention, and she loved him for it and was sometimes very tired of it.

She said her daughter's name once, quietly, into the air above the mound.

Then she went back down the slope to start the morning fire.

---

Two thousand years passed.

The mound grew and was added to and was not added to and grew again. The river flooded twice in the same generation and did not flood for forty years after. People were born on the slope's shadow and died there and were put into the earth alongside things they had made or been given or carried for reasons that did not survive them. A drought came and the community argued about its meaning and the argument was never fully resolved and the drought ended anyway. English ships appeared on the bay and then English settlements and then English law, and a woman named Saya wrapped a petition in oilskin and buried it at the mound's base when the petition failed, and her husband Okwi stood beside her and said nothing, which was the most serious thing he ever did. A man named Matta made pipes and kept two sets of accounts — one for the colonial record, one for the people who came to him at night. A girl named Tessa wore a shell gorget at her throat and argued with her brother about what it meant to survive and never reached a conclusion and survived anyway.

The highway came last. Route 301 through Charles County, running straight because someone had decided it would, the land learning to agree. The mound sat in the expansion corridor's secondary zone, which was the state's language for *we have not yet decided what to do with this.*

The woman who would decide was two hours north, in a university office, holding a permit she had spent two years earning.

Her name was Tayak Moyaone.

The only thread connecting her to Yanu was a copper pendant wrapped in birchbark, four hands deep in the northern slope, waiting with the patience of things that have been waiting so long they have forgotten they are waiting.

And a name. Spoken aloud, generation to generation, the sound of it changing the way all sounds change over time, the meaning of it constant: *the one who reads the ground.*

The woman who buried and the woman who digs are the same story, told from opposite ends.

---

The envelope had been sitting on her desk for twenty minutes, and Tayak had not touched it.

She had moved it once — from the center of the blotter to the left edge, next to her cold coffee — and that was all. *Maryland Department of Planning.* Her name typed in the little window. The return address she had been waiting two years to see.

Outside her office window, the quad was loud with the last week of November: someone's music, a skateboard on brick, two students arguing about something that wasn't going to matter in a month. Tayak watched a crow land on the lip of a concrete planter, cock its head at a dropped granola bar, and decide it was worth the trouble. She envied the clarity.

The permit application had cost her fourteen months of her life. Before that, the site survey. Before that, the tribal council hearings, the university review board, the environmental impact filing, the correspondence with the state historic preservation office that had grown, letter by letter, into something close to a legal record. She had a folder four inches thick and a second one starting. She had learned the name of every person in the Maryland SHPO chain of command, their administrative assistants, and in one case their administrative assistant's supervisor.

She had done everything right. That was the thing she kept coming back to. She had done everything right, and it still might not be enough.

The permit envelope was standard business size, cream-colored, with a small coffee-colored smear on one corner from the mail cart. Whatever was inside was thin — two pages, maybe three. Thin enough to be a denial. Thin enough to be an approval with conditions she couldn't meet.

She picked it up.

The paper was the kind of thick, slightly waxy stock that government offices used, and it crinkled when she pressed the fold. She slid her thumb under the flap.

*Two years,* she thought. *Two years and a site that won't exist in eight months.*

The highway expansion project — Route 301 through Charles County — had a construction start date of January 15. She had known about it for three years, had filed her first letter of concern before the environmental impact study was even published. The mound sat in the expansion corridor's secondary zone, which meant it was scheduled for "evaluation and possible relocation of cultural materials" — state language for a backhoe and a weekend. She had six months between a construction permit and the first machine cut, assuming the state moved on its usual schedule. Assuming she got approval today.

If she didn't —

She pulled out the letter.

*Dear Dr. Moyaone. The Maryland State Historic Preservation Office has reviewed your application for excavation permit (file no. 2024-SHPO-0441) pertaining to the site designated PG-CA-0117, Charles County. After review of the submitted documentation, this office is prepared to issue a limited excavation permit subject to the following conditions —*

She stopped. Read it again. *Prepared to issue.*

The relief came fast and physical, a loosening in her chest she hadn't known was clenched. She sat back in her chair and looked at the ceiling, where a water stain shaped like Florida had been there since she'd taken this office three years ago.

Then she read the conditions.

There were seven of them. She got through the first three quickly — standard stuff, site photography protocols, artifact cataloguing requirements, the university's liability framework. The fourth was a monitoring requirement: a state-appointed observer at the site for any excavation below thirty centimeters. She didn't like it, but she could work with it.

The fifth stopped her.

*5. All human remains and associated funerary objects recovered during excavation shall be subject to immediate transfer to state custody pending consultation under NAGPRA, notwithstanding any tribal affiliation claims currently under review. Transfer shall occur within 72 hours of recovery.*

She read it twice. Then she set the letter down on the desk and looked at it.

The Piscataway Nation's federal recognition petition had been under review for eleven years. *Currently under review* was the state's way of saying that the Piscataway's claim to their own dead could be set aside because a federal office hadn't finished its paperwork. Seventy-two hours. Whatever she found, whatever came out of that ground — bones, objects, things that had been in the earth for centuries — the state wanted it in their custody before she could file a tribal affiliation claim.

She picked up the letter again and read condition six.

*6. Excavation shall be limited to the footprint designated in the attached survey map (Exhibit A). Any expansion of the excavation area requires prior written approval from this office with a minimum review period of thirty days.*

She found Exhibit A. The footprint was smaller than she'd requested — significantly smaller. They had cut the northwest quadrant entirely, which was exactly where the deepest stratigraphy was most likely to run.

The crow was still at the planter. It had the granola bar now and was working on it with focused, methodical contempt.

Tayak looked at condition seven.

*7. This permit is valid for a period of ninety days from the date of issue. Extensions may be requested but are not guaranteed.*

She did the math. Ninety days from today put her at the end of February. The highway construction permit was January 15. That left a gap — a gap when the site would be unprotected, when anyone with a shovel and a weekend could —

She stopped herself. That was a different problem.

The immediate problem was condition five.

She could accept the permit as written. She could begin the excavation, document everything, and fight the custody condition through the courts while the dig was running. Marcus would know the legal angles. He had been talking about a challenge to the NAGPRA consultation framework for two years; maybe this was the case that forced the issue.

Or she could refuse the permit and file a challenge before she set foot on the site, which meant months of legal process she didn't have months for.

She pulled her legal pad from under the desk phone and wrote, at the top: *Condition 5. What does Marcus say.* Then she wrote: *Condition 6. NW quadrant — why cut?* Then: *90 days. Enough?*

She looked at the three lines. They looked back at her.

Two years of work, and she had a permit she couldn't fully trust, a site that was smaller than she needed, and a state that had written a condition specifically designed to separate the Piscataway from their own ancestors before the tribe could establish the legal standing to claim them.

She thought about what was in that ground. What might be in that ground. She had done enough survey work to know the mound was old — older than the colonial stratigraphy, older than the eighteenth century, possibly much older. She had held the ground-penetrating radar printout in her hands and traced the anomalies with her finger and felt something she didn't talk about in grant applications: the absolute certainty that whatever was down there had been put there by someone who knew what they were doing and why.

She thought about her grandmother, who had grown up two miles from that mound and had never once mentioned it.

She picked up her phone and called Marcus.

It rang four times. She was composing a voicemail when he picked up.

"You got it," he said. Not a question.

"I got it." She hesitated. "With conditions."

A pause. She heard the sound of a car door, then quiet. "How bad?"

"Condition five," she said. "Seventy-two-hour transfer of human remains to state custody. Pending tribal affiliation review."

The silence on his end had a particular quality — the kind that meant he was already thinking three moves ahead. "They're trying to get ahead of the recognition petition," he said.

"Yes."

"If they have custody before we file —"

"I know."

Another pause. "When do you want to talk?"

"Tonight," she said. "I'll come to you."

She hung up and looked at the letter again. The permit was real. The excavation was real. She could begin in a week, maybe less, if she moved fast on the equipment and got Wren out to the site for the preliminary grid work.

But condition five sat in the middle of the page like a trap with the door still open, and she had just agreed to walk toward it.

She folded the letter back into the envelope and put it in the four-inch folder. Then she opened her laptop and started drafting the acceptance letter, because the alternative was losing the site entirely, and she had not come this far to let the ground keep its silence by default.

Her coffee was cold. She drank it anyway.

---

Marcus called back before she finished the second paragraph of the acceptance letter.

"Don't send that yet," he said.

She pulled her hands off the keyboard. "I haven't."

"Good. I want to look at the language before you respond officially." She heard him moving — a door, then the low hum of a building's heating system. He was at the Howard Law library. He went there when he needed to think without his office around him. "Read me condition five again."

She read it. All of it, including the seventy-two hours and the phrase *pending tribal affiliation review.*

"And condition six," he said.

She read that too.

The silence lasted long enough that she checked her phone to make sure the call was still connected.

"The northwest quadrant," he said finally. "That's deliberate."

"I know."

"They've seen the survey data."

"They reviewed the same GPR report I submitted with the application." She kept her voice level. "The deepest anomalies are in the northwest quadrant. They cut it because that's where the oldest material is most likely to be."

"Which means if you find something significant —"

"It'll be in the footprint they approved." She picked up her pen and set it down. "And condition five will apply to it."

Another pause. She heard him exhale.

"Marcus," she said. "Tell me you have a way around this."

"I have a way through it," he said. "Which is different."

She waited.

"You sign it," he said.

She put the pen down harder than she meant to. "You want me to sign a permit that gives the state custody of human remains before we can establish tribal affiliation."

"I want you to sign a permit that gets you on that site before the forty-eight-hour window closes."

"Marcus —"

"Tayak." His voice was not unkind. That was the worst part of it. "The highway crew breaks ground in eleven weeks. If you don't sign, the permit expires tomorrow at five. You file a challenge, you're looking at sixty days minimum before a hearing, and that's if the commission clerk doesn't sit on the paperwork. You'll be arguing about jurisdiction while the machines are already there."

She stood up. She needed to be standing for this. "If I sign it and they invoke condition five on the first significant find, we lose the legal high ground. The moment they take custody —"

"We challenge the custody order."

"After the fact. From behind."

"Yes." He said it plainly, without apology. "That's where we are."

She walked to the window. The crow had finished the granola bar and was examining the empty planter with the same methodical attention it had brought to the food. She watched it for a moment. She had the feeling, not for the first time, that Marcus had already made this decision before he called her back — that the question of whether to sign had been settled in his mind before she finished reading condition five, and that this conversation was the part where he waited for her to arrive at the same place.

"The clause," she said. "We should be naming it."

"I know what it is."

"It's a mechanism for separating the tribe from the artifacts before recognition is established. If they invoke it on human remains, we're fighting a custody battle in state court with no federally recognized standing. We'd be arguing that the Piscataway have a cultural affiliation to ancestors in Piscataway territory, and the state gets to sit across the table and say *prove it.*"

"I know," he said.

"And you still want me to sign."

The heating system hummed on his end. She heard him shift his weight.

"The site is real," he said. "What's in that ground is real. You get on that site, you document everything correctly, you build the record — and then we have something to fight with. Right now we have a GPR printout and a recognition petition that's been in federal review for three years." He paused. "Sign it. Get in the ground. Find us something to fight with."

She looked at the permit on her desk. Condition five. The language was precise in the way that legal language was precise when someone had worked to make it so — not careless, not boilerplate, but deliberate. Seventy-two hours. *Significant historical value.* The phrase was undefined, which meant the state could define it at the moment of their choosing.

She thought about what might be down there. The GPR anomalies. The depth of them. She had traced them with her finger and felt the certainty she didn't put in grant applications, and the certainty was still there, sitting in her chest like a coal.

"If they invoke it," she said, "I want it on record that we objected to the clause before we signed."

"I'll draft a cover letter," Marcus said. "We note the clause, we state our position on tribal custody rights, we sign under protest. It's not binding but it's in the file."

"It's in the file," she repeated.

"It's something."

It was something. It was a very small something, the kind of something that looked better in the moment than it would look later, but it was the only something available.

"All right," she said.

She heard him exhale again — relief, this time, though he would not call it that.

"I'll have the cover letter to you in an hour," he said. "Don't send the acceptance until you have it."

"I know."

"Tayak."

"What."

"You're doing the right thing." He said it the way people said things they believed but knew were insufficient. "The site is what matters."

She didn't answer that. She said goodbye and hung up.

She stood at the window for another minute. The crow had gone. She hadn't seen it leave — it was simply there and then not there, the planter empty, the gray sky the same gray sky it had been all morning.

She went back to her desk and picked up the acceptance letter. She read what she'd written, deleted the second paragraph, and started again. The language had to be exact. She was accepting a permit she had reservations about, under a deadline she had not set, for a site she had spent two years trying to protect. She wrote the sentence three times before she got it right — formal, clear, nothing in it that could be used against her, nothing in it that said what she actually thought about condition five.

When Marcus's cover letter arrived, she read it twice. He had written it well. The objection was stated, the tribe's position on custody was stated, the signature was framed as a practical necessity rather than an agreement with the permit's terms. It was the best version of a bad position. She printed both documents, signed them with the university pen she kept in the left drawer — a cheap ballpoint that had run dry twice and been refilled — and put them in an envelope addressed to the State Historic Preservation Office.

She held the envelope for a moment.

Then she put it in her bag to mail on the way out, because if she thought about it any longer she would not mail it at all, and the site would be gone in eleven weeks, and whatever was in that ground would go with it.

The departmental fax machine lived in a supply closet off the main corridor, wedged between a shelving unit of surplus toner cartridges and a fire extinguisher that had not been inspected since the Obama administration. The closet smelled of warm plastic and old paper. Tayak had faxed things here she was glad to have sent and things she wished she could take back, and she had learned that the machine itself felt the same about both.

She was halfway down the hall when she heard Priya's door open.

"Hey."

Tayak stopped. Priya Suresh leaned in the doorframe with a coffee mug that said *GEOLOGY ROCKS* in letters she had long since stopped finding funny. She was still in her coat — she'd come in late, or she was going out early, or she simply wore her coat indoors in November because the building's heating system had opinions about the east wing that it did not share with the west.

"Hey," Tayak said.

Priya looked at the envelope in her hand. "You got it."

"I got it."

"Clean?"

Tayak considered this. "Clean enough."

Priya said nothing for a moment. She had the geologist's habit of looking at things the way other people looked at arguments — not to win, just to see where the pressure was. She looked at Tayak that way now. Not alarmed. Just noting.

"There's a clause," Tayak said.

"What kind of clause."

"The kind where they define the terms after the fact."

Priya turned her mug in both hands. "Seventy-two hours?"

"You read the draft."

"Kimi posted it. Half the internet read the draft." She paused. "You signed it anyway."

"Marcus drafted a cover letter. We're on record objecting."

"That's something."

"That's what I said."

Priya looked at her — not the envelope this time, just her. "You okay?"

"I'm fine."

"You look like someone who just signed something they're going to think about at three in the morning."

"I think about everything at three in the morning."

"Fair." Priya lifted her mug slightly, the way she did when she was conceding a point she hadn't actually contested. "The GPR anomalies are real, Tayak. Whatever's down there is real. You get in the ground, you document it right—"

"I know." She had heard this twice already today. It was true both times and it helped less each time. "I need to fax this before I talk myself out of it."

Priya stepped back into her doorway. "Cricket tonight. India-Australia. I'm watching at the faculty lounge if you want to come tell me why it's boring."

"It *is* boring."

"It's a nine-hour test match. It's supposed to be boring. That's the point."

Tayak almost smiled. "I'll be in Charles County."

Priya nodded. She already knew, probably. She knew most things about Tayak's schedule and pretended to know fewer of them than she did, which was the right amount of friendship for a building where everyone knew too much about everyone else. "Tell Nora I said hello."

"She doesn't know who you are."

"She knows I'm the one who brings you back in a reasonable mood. Tell her that one."

Tayak walked on down the corridor.

---

The supply closet door stuck, as it always did, because the frame had swollen sometime in 2019 and no one had planed it down. She put her shoulder into it and it gave, and the warm plastic smell came out at her. She set the envelope on the narrow shelf beside the machine and took the documents out.

The fax machine was a Brother model from approximately 2011, gray and slightly yellowed at the corners, with a display panel that read *READY* in green letters that seemed, at this moment, to be making a broader claim than the machine had any right to make.

She fed the acceptance letter in first.

The machine took it with a sound like a slow exhale and began to pull it through.

The cover letter next. Marcus's careful language — *the Piscataway Nation notes the following objection* — feeding into the roller, becoming signal, becoming transmission, becoming a fact in the file of the State Historic Preservation Office that could not be taken back.

She watched the display. *SENDING.*

The machine hummed. Somewhere in Annapolis, in a government office with fluorescent lights and a filing system that ran on inertia, the pages were arriving. Condition five was arriving. The objection to condition five was arriving. Eleven weeks of excavation window, seventy-two-hour notice clause, *significant historical value* undefined and therefore infinitely definable — all of it arriving, all of it now real in the way that only signed and transmitted things were real.

*SENT*, the display said.

She stood there a moment longer than she needed to.

The corridor outside was quiet. Somewhere down the hall a door closed. The heating system ticked in the wall. The fax machine's display returned to *READY*, already finished with the thing she had been building toward for two years, already waiting for the next thing.

She put the documents back in the envelope, folded it once, and put it in her bag.

She had a forty-minute drive ahead of her — clear day, no traffic — a grandmother she hadn't visited in three weeks, and questions she had been not-asking for long enough that they had started to feel like a second skeleton inside her own — something she carried without naming, something she had learned the shape of without ever looking directly at it.

Nora had known about the mound. Tayak was almost certain of it now. The way her grandmother had talked around it for years, the way she'd gone quiet at certain questions and then started talking about something else with the practiced ease of a woman who had been redirecting conversations her entire life — it added up to something. Not a lie. Nora didn't lie. But a silence, which was a different thing and sometimes worse.

She pulled the supply closet door shut behind her. It stuck again, and she had to pull twice.

Down the corridor, Priya's office light was still on, a yellow rectangle under the door. The cricket match would start in six hours. The drive to La Plata would take forty minutes in good traffic, and she would arrive at her grandmother's house in the early evening, when the kitchen light would already be on and Nora would already be at the table with her quilting, the needle moving through fabric in a pattern that Tayak had never been able to follow, a pattern Nora said came from her own grandmother, who had it from hers.

She had never asked where the pattern came from before that.

She was going to ask tonight.

Voss's office was the last door before the stairwell, and it was always open. He said it was a policy — *my door is always open* — but the truth was that the mechanism had stripped sometime in the previous decade and the door no longer latched. Tayak had watched him try to close it once, after a difficult phone call, and fail, and accept the failure with the particular dignity of a man who had decided not to notice things that couldn't be fixed.

She knocked on the frame.

"Tayak." He looked up from a stack of paper that had been organized and then disorganized and then left in the intermediate state. He was sixty-two and looked it in the way that men who had been handsome at forty looked it — the structure still there, the surface softer. "Come in. Sit down."

She sat in the chair across from his desk. It was a good chair, leather, the kind that announced the room's seriousness. His bookshelves ran floor to ceiling on two walls, and his own monograph — *Pre-Contact Trade Networks of the Chesapeake Tidewater* — occupied a full shelf's worth of copies in three editions, arranged with a casualness that had clearly been arranged. On his desk, a half-eaten granola bar sat on a napkin beside a coffee mug that read *HARVARD DEPARTMENT OF ANTHROPOLOGY* in letters that had faded to a suggestion.

"The permit came through," she said.

He put down his pen. His face did the thing it did when he was genuinely pleased — a small, unguarded opening, before the professional warmth closed over it. "When?"

"This morning. We start Monday."

"Monday." He sat back. "That's — Tayak, that's tremendous. Two years."

"Two years and four months."

"Two years and four months." He picked up the pen again, not to write anything, just to hold it. "The GPR results held up?"

"Wren's running the final comparison now. But yes. The anomalies are consistent with a stratified deposit. Possibly seven distinct layers."

"Seven." He set the pen down again. "That would be — if you're right about the dating —"

"Two thousand years. Give or take."

He was quiet for a moment. She could see him doing the thing he did with significant numbers, turning them over, finding where they fit in the architecture of his own thinking. His monograph's trade network analysis stopped at contact. Her site, if it held, would push the record back a millennium before his earliest data point. She had never said this to him directly. She suspected he knew.

"The state's going to watch this closely," he said.

"I know."

"Slade's office has been —"

"I know, Elliot."

He nodded. He picked up the pen a third time and this time actually wrote something — a word, or a date, she couldn't see — on the corner of the top sheet. Then he looked up, and his expression had shifted into the register she recognized as *there is something else*.

"I should mention," he said, "that I've been in contact with the State Historic Preservation Office. Slade's office reached out about three weeks ago. They were looking for an independent academic liaison — someone to review methodology, provide a kind of oversight function. A neutral party."

The heating vent above the door ticked once.

"They asked me," he said. "I said yes. I thought —" He paused, and she could see him reading her face, and she was grateful that she had spent two years in rooms with people who wanted to see her react. "I thought you'd be pleased. Having someone in the room who knows your work, who can —"

"Who asked for the liaison?" she said.

"Slade's office initiated it. But it's standard practice for a site of this —"

"It's not standard practice for sites of this type. It's standard practice for sites where the state wants a second opinion ready."

Voss opened his mouth and then closed it. He had the grace, at least, to sit with that.

Outside the window, the November campus was the color of an old photograph — the oaks stripped down, the walkways wet, a student crossing the quad with her hood up and her shoulders pulled in against a wind that had been carrying rain since Tuesday.

"I accepted in good faith," he said. "My intention was to be useful to you."

"I know that."

"My intention was not to —"

"I know, Elliot." She kept her voice level. "I'm glad you told me."

He looked at her with the expression of a man who understood that *I'm glad you told me* was not the same as *I'm fine with this*, and who was deciding whether to press the distinction. He decided not to. That was the right call. She gave him credit for it.

"The liaison role is advisory only," he said. "I have no authority over the excavation itself. Your methodology, your documentation, your interpretation — those are yours."

"And if Slade's office disagrees with my interpretation?"

"Then they disagree. That's a separate process."

"With you in the room."

A pause. "With me in the room."

She stood. She picked up her bag from the floor beside the chair and settled the strap on her shoulder. The leather had cracked near the buckle — she kept meaning to replace it, and kept not replacing it, and now it was a thing she'd had for so long that the crack felt like part of it.

"Congratulations are in order," Voss said. He stood as well, reflexively, the old courtesy. "Genuinely. Two years and four months. That's — the work you've done to get here, Tayak. Whatever happens with the site, that work is real."

"Thank you, Elliot."

She said it the way she said *I'm glad you told me* — which was to say, she meant it, and also meant something else at the same time, and he heard both, and they both let it stand.

She walked back out through the open door. In the corridor, the fluorescent tube nearest the stairwell was flickering at the edge of perception — not quite strobing, just uncertain, a light that had not yet decided whether to fail. She walked under it and pushed through the stairwell door.

Three weeks. He had said yes three weeks ago and had not mentioned it until she came to tell him the permit was signed. She turned that over as she went down the stairs. Not malice — she believed that. Voss did not operate from malice. He operated from the assumption that his presence in a room was a form of help, and that assumption had never been seriously tested, because his presence in a room usually was a form of help, for people whose work moved in the same direction as his.

Her work was about to move through his.

She came out of the stairwell into the ground-floor corridor and pushed through the heavy exterior door into the cold. The rain had thinned to a mist that was more mood than weather.

She had a forty-minute drive. She had a grandmother who had known about the mound and said nothing, and now she had a department chair who had agreed to serve the state and said nothing for three weeks, and both silences had the same shape — the shape of people who believed they were protecting her, and who had not asked whether she wanted to be protected, and who would be genuinely hurt to learn that the protection felt, from where she stood, like one more door she would have to shoulder open.

She walked toward the parking structure. The mist settled on her jacket in drops too small to feel individually, only as a general cold.

Monday. She would be in the ground Monday.

Whatever Voss reported back to Slade's office, whatever *independent academic liaison* meant in practice, whatever condition five was going to cost her — it started Monday, and the ground did not care about any of it, and the ground had been waiting for two thousand years, and she was not going to let three more weeks of someone else's silence stop her now.

---

She drove south on Route 301 with the permit copy on the passenger seat and the radio off.

The highway ran straight through Charles County the way old roads do — not because the land was easy but because someone a long time ago had decided it would be, and the land had learned to agree. Bare fields on both sides, the corn long harvested, the soil turned up in dark furrows that held the last of the afternoon light. A water tower. A gas station with one pump lit. A billboard for a personal injury lawyer whose smile had faded in the weather to something closer to a grimace.

She had driven this road three hundred times. She knew the exit before her exit by the particular angle of the overpass, knew the county line sign by the chip in its lower left corner where someone had clipped it with a trailer hitch and never reported it. She passed it now — *Welcome to Charles County* — and felt the specific weight of it settle across her shoulders. Not the weight of coming home. The weight of coming home as something other than what she had left as.

She had left as Nora's granddaughter. She was arriving as the archaeologist with the permit.

The distinction should not have mattered as much as it did.

The fields gave way to the older part of town — the low brick storefronts, the barbershop with the same pole it had always had, the hardware store that had been a hardware store when Nora was young and would probably be a hardware store when everyone Tayak knew was dead. The light was going flat and gray, the particular gray of a November afternoon that had never quite decided to be anything. She turned off the main road onto the county road, then off the county road onto the smaller road that ran between two stands of loblolly pine, and then she was in the driveway.

Nora's house sat back from the road the way it always had — white clapboard, the porch light already on though it was not yet fully dark, the two azalea bushes on either side of the front steps cut back for winter into shapes that looked like clenched fists. The kitchen window was lit. She could see the shadow of movement behind the curtain.

Tayak turned off the engine and sat.

The permit was still on the passenger seat. She had printed two copies at the office — one for the site files, one she had not quite been able to explain to herself. She looked at it now in the gray light. Twelve pages. The first three were bureaucratic boilerplate, the language of forms that had been drafted by committee and revised by subcommittee until every sentence meant exactly what it needed to mean and nothing more. Page four was the conditions list. She had read condition five so many times she could have recited it.

She did not know how much of page four she was going to tell Nora.

That was not a decision she had made. It felt more like a decision that was waiting for her to catch up to it.

The kitchen shadow moved again — Nora crossing from the counter to the stove, the unhurried rhythm of someone who had been cooking in that kitchen for fifty years and had no reason to hurry. Tayak watched the shadow and thought about the call she had made from the parking structure, two hours ago. *I'm coming down. I have news.* And Nora had said, *I'll make soup,* which was what Nora said when she understood that news was the kind that required sitting down with something warm in your hands.

Tayak picked up the permit. She folded it once, along the existing crease, and put it in her bag.

She had a feeling she could not name. It was not triumph — triumph was clean, and this was not clean. It was not dread, though dread was in it somewhere, a low note under everything else. It was something that contained both and was larger than either, the feeling of having worked toward a thing for so long that its arrival did not feel like arrival so much as the beginning of a different kind of work. Two years and four months. The permit signed. Monday, the ground.

And Nora in the kitchen, making soup, knowing something was coming — knowing, Tayak now suspected, more than she had ever said about what the ground held, and why, and whose it was.

She got out of the car. The cold came in at once, sharper than College Park, the particular cold of low country near water, and she could smell woodsmoke from somewhere down the road and the damp smell of the turned fields she had driven through. The porch light threw a yellow oval across the front steps and the two clipped azaleas and the edge of the driveway where the gravel met the grass.

She had her hand on the porch railing when the kitchen light shifted — the shadow stilling, the way a person stills when they hear a car door.

Tayak climbed the steps. She was going to tell Nora the excavation was approved. She was going to sit at the kitchen table she had done her homework at for twelve years and drink whatever Nora put in front of her and tell the story of the permit and Voss and the conditions list, and she was going to watch her grandmother's face while she told it, and she was going to pay very close attention to what she saw there.

She raised her hand to knock — she always knocked, had knocked since she was twenty-two, the habit of a woman who had learned to live elsewhere — and then stopped.

The door opened before her knuckles touched the wood.

Nora stood in the doorway in her housedress and cardigan, the one with the wooden buttons she had carved herself, her white hair pinned back, her face doing the thing it did when she was holding something carefully — not the careful of a person who was hiding, but the careful of a person who was carrying.

"Come in out of the cold," Nora said.

Tayak went in.
