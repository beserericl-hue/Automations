The permit was thirty-one pages.

Tayak had expected thirty. She'd read the standard Maryland archaeological research permit so many times in the past two years that she could have recited the section headings in her sleep: principal investigator obligations, field crew certification, provenience documentation, chain-of-custody for environmental samples, curation agreement with the state repository. She knew where every comma fell.

Page thirty-one was new.

She sat in a plastic chair across from the receptionist's empty desk — the woman had stepped out for coffee, leaving Tayak alone with the packet and the sound of an air conditioner working too hard against the August heat outside — and read the page again. The SHPO office smelled of toner and carpet cleaner, a smell she associated with every bureaucratic defeat of her professional life. A single fern on the windowsill had gone yellow at the tips from too much sun and not enough water. Nobody had noticed, or nobody had cared to fix it.

*Clause 7(b): The State of Maryland retains right of first refusal on any artifact, assemblage, or associated material determined by the State Historic Preservation Officer to be of significant historical value. Determination shall be made within sixty days of notification by the principal investigator. During the determination period, the principal investigator shall not transfer, publish findings regarding, or otherwise dispose of the artifact in question without written authorization from this office.*

She read it a third time.

Two years. Two years of quarterly status meetings with Voss, who kept suggesting she redirect her research toward the Potomac trade networks he'd spent thirty years mapping, as though her work were a tributary that might, with gentle pressure, be redirected into his river. Two years of correspondence with Marcus about the council's formal endorsement, which he'd given and then qualified and then given again in a slightly different form. Two years of the highway expansion sitting on the county commissioner's agenda like a held breath, the project engineers already boring test cores along Route 6, already talking about a spring groundbreaking. Two years of Nora asking, every Sunday at dinner, *is it done yet*, and Tayak saying *almost*, until the word had lost its shape.

She had the permit. It was in her hands.

She turned back to Clause 7(b).

*Significant historical value.* The phrase did no work on its own — it was a vessel you could fill with whatever the state needed it to hold. A glass trade bead, seventeenth century, Chesapeake provenance: significant? A ceramic pipe bowl with intact decoration: significant? Human remains — and she had to be honest with herself, she might find human remains, the mound's profile suggested it, the LiDAR suggested it — human remains were governed by NAGPRA, which was a separate fight, but an assemblage of objects buried with them? The phrase could reach anywhere it wanted.

The receptionist's heels clicked in the hallway. Tayak closed the packet.

"Everything look okay?" The woman set two coffees on her desk, then seemed to remember she hadn't offered one. "Oh — did you want—"

"I'm fine." Tayak set the packet on the edge of the desk. "I have a question about Clause 7(b)."

The woman looked at the packet, then at Tayak, with the expression of someone who had not written the clause and did not intend to defend it. "That's standard language. It went in last session."

"It wasn't in the draft I reviewed in March."

"No." The woman picked up her coffee. "It was added in June."

Tayak waited.

"You'd want to talk to Mr. Slade about the specifics." The woman's voice was not unkind. "He can walk you through the determination process."

Garrett Slade. She knew the name. Everybody in Maryland archaeology knew the name — the SHPO who'd held up the Waldorf site repatriation for eighteen months on a technicality about documentation format, who'd given a quote to the *Capital Gazette* last year about "balancing preservation interests with responsible development," a sentence so perfectly empty it could have meant anything or nothing. She had not yet met him in person.

"When is he available?"

"He's in Annapolis through Friday." The woman pulled up a screen. "He has a slot Thursday at two."

Thursday was three days away. The permit was in her hands right now, and the highway project's spring groundbreaking was not a rumor anymore, it was a line in the county commissioner's published schedule. She thought about the mound: low, eroded at its southern edge where a farm road had cut through sometime in the last century, the soil dark and clay-heavy, the kind of ground that held things for a very long time. She thought about what the ground might be holding.

She picked up the pen the receptionist had set beside the signature line.

"I'll take Thursday," she said. "Two o'clock."

She signed.

The pen was one of those cheap ballpoints with a state agency logo printed on the barrel — *Maryland Historical Trust* — and it skipped once on the last letter of her name before the ink caught. She pressed harder than she needed to. The signature looked like what it was: a decision made under pressure, in a room that smelled of carpet cleaner, with a clause she hadn't agreed to and had signed anyway because the alternative was watching a road grader take the mound apart by the yard.

She kept her copy of the packet. The receptionist kept hers.

Outside, the August heat hit her like a wet cloth. Her car was parked across a lot that shimmered at the edges, the asphalt soft enough to feel underfoot. She sat in the driver's seat without starting the engine and read Clause 7(b) one more time, the permit open across the steering wheel, the air in the car already thick and close.

*Significant historical value.*

She thought about what she was driving toward — the mound site, La Plata, Nora's house on Smallwood Drive where she'd been meaning to stop for two weeks. She thought about Nora asking *is it done yet*, and about the fact that she still didn't know, not really, what Nora expected her to find.

She started the car. The AC came on with a rattle and blew hot air for thirty seconds before it turned cold.

She had the permit. She had Clause 7(b). She had Thursday at two with a man named Slade.

She pulled out of the lot and headed south on Route 2, toward La Plata, toward the mound, toward whatever the ground had been keeping for her.

Thursday at two, the Maryland Historical Trust occupied the third floor of a building on Charles Street in La Plata that had been designed to suggest permanence without achieving it: brick facing over a steel frame, the mortar already staining at the corners, a lobby directory with half the office names in plastic letters that didn't quite match. The elevator smelled of toner and someone's lunch. Tayak rode it up with her copy of the permit in a manila folder and the folder held flat against her side, the way she carried things she didn't want to crease.

The third floor was quieter than the lobby. A window at the end of the hall looked out over the parking lot and, beyond it, a stand of loblolly pines that had survived whatever had been cleared to build the strip of storage units next door. The pines were the tallest things in the view. In the August heat their needles had gone a dull, dusty green, the color of something that was managing.

A man came out of the corner office before the receptionist could announce her.

"Dr. Moyaone." He crossed the room with the particular walk of someone who runs — hips forward, weight through the balls of his feet, no wasted motion. Fifty-five, she would have said, or close to it. Close-cropped gray at the temples, a blue oxford shirt with the sleeves rolled to the elbow, a handshake that was firm and finished in under two seconds. "Garrett Slade. Come on in."

His office was large enough to hold a round table with four chairs, a desk he clearly didn't use for meetings, and two walls of shelving stacked with binders in state-agency colors — green spines, white labels, the kind of institutional organization that said *I have been here a long time and I intend to stay.* On the wall behind the table hung a framed survey map, maybe three feet wide, the kind of large-format print that cost real money to produce. Charles County, the whole of it, rendered in the fine contour lines of a USGS base layer with overlays in red and blue marking sites of record. She registered it and looked away.

"Coffee?" he said.

"I'm fine."

He sat across from her and folded his hands on the table in the manner of a man who had learned, somewhere, that folded hands read as openness. "I understand you have questions about the language in 7(b)."

"I signed it," Tayak said. "I want to understand how the determination gets made."

"Sure." He nodded as if this were the most reasonable thing anyone had said to him all week. "So the way it works — the principal investigator flags items during the curation review, we have a look, and if something rises to the level of significant historical value under the Trust's guidelines, we initiate a conversation about placement."

"Placement."

"Repository options. Could be the Trust's own collection, could be the Smithsonian, could be a university partner. We try to be collaborative." He said *collaborative* the way people say *fair* — as a word that closed the question rather than opened it.

"And if I disagree with the determination?"

"There's an appeal process. It's in the addendum, pages eleven through fourteen." He leaned back slightly. "I want to be straight with you, Dr. Moyaone. I know this language wasn't in your March draft. That's a legitimate frustration. It came out of a legislative session and I didn't have discretion about whether it went in." He paused. "I have a daughter who does tribal rights work. New Mexico, mostly — she's with a firm in Albuquerque. Elena. She'd probably say I don't always get this right."

Tayak looked at him.

He smiled, just briefly, as if he knew how it had sounded and had decided to let it stand anyway.

"The appeal process," she said. "What's the timeline?"

"Thirty days from the determination notice."

"And during those thirty days, who has custody of the artifact?"

A small pause. "The Trust."

She wrote nothing down. She didn't need to. "Okay," she said. "Thank you for the clarification."

She stood. He stood with her, the runner's reflexes again, and they shook hands a second time — the same handshake, firm and brief, a door closing politely. He walked her to the threshold of his office and stopped there, which was the correct distance, the distance that said *I am not escorting you out because I don't need to.*

She was three steps into the hallway when she stopped.

The map.

She turned back, as if she'd forgotten something, and looked at it properly. The framed survey print, Charles County in full. The mound site was marked in red — a small polygon with a site number she recognized, 18CH447, the number she'd been writing on sample bags for two years. She found it in under a second because she had memorized that county the way other people memorize faces.

Two miles west of 18CH447, a parcel outlined in blue.

*SLADE FAMILY LAND — est. 1887.*

She stood there long enough to read it twice.

Slade was still in his doorway. If he saw her looking, he gave no sign of it. His expression was the same as it had been for the entire meeting: pleasant, measured, the face of a man who had learned to be patient with people who were about to become problems.

"Safe drive," he said.

"Thanks," she said.

She walked to the elevator and pressed the button and stood with her back straight and the folder flat against her side and did not look at her hands. The elevator took longer than it should have. When the doors opened she stepped in and watched the third-floor hallway close away behind her.

Two miles. His family's land, two miles from the mound. The mound she'd just given the state the right to pick through.

The lobby doors opened onto the August heat and the parking lot and the loblolly pines standing in their dusty green above the storage units, and she walked to her car thinking about the map — not the red polygon marking her site, but the blue one beside it, the one she hadn't known to look for until thirty seconds ago, the one that changed the shape of everything she thought she understood about why Clause 7(b) had appeared in June.

She put the folder on the passenger seat. She started the engine. The AC rattled and blew hot.

She pulled out of the lot and headed south, toward the mound, and she drove with both hands on the wheel and the particular stillness of someone who has just learned something and has not yet decided what to do with it.

The phone rang twice before she picked it up — Nora's name on the screen, though Tayak hadn't called yet. She answered.

"Grandma."

"You signed it."

It wasn't a question. Tayak kept her eyes on Route 301, the road unspooling south through a corridor of loblolly pine and fast-food signs, the August haze sitting low over everything.

"I signed it."

A pause. On Nora's end, the sound of a chair — that particular creak from the kitchen, the one Tayak had heard her whole life, the one that meant Nora had sat down to think.

"Did you read all of it?"

Tayak moved the folder six inches to the right without looking at it. "Yes."

"Good," Nora said.

And hung up.

Tayak held the phone a moment, then set it face-down on the folder.

*Good.* Not *be careful* or *call Marcus* or *I told you* — just the one word, dropped like a stone into still water, and then nothing. Nora had been a schoolteacher for thirty-one years. She knew how to end a conversation so that the silence after it did the work.

Tayak turned the AC down. The rattle softened but didn't stop.

Outside, the county changed character the way it always did south of Waldorf — the strip malls thinning, the lots widening, the roadside commerce giving way to stretches of second-growth woods and cleared fields where the soil ran dark and clay-heavy between the rows. She'd driven this road her whole life. She knew the landmarks the way she knew her own handwriting: the Baptist church with the reader board that hadn't changed its message in three years, the farm stand with the hand-lettered SWEET CORN sign, the place where the road dipped and the tree canopy closed overhead for exactly forty seconds before opening again onto a long, flat straightaway with a water tower visible to the east.

She drove the straightaway and thought about Nora's question.

*Did you read all of it.*

Not *did you read the clause* or *did you read page eleven.* All of it. As if there were something in the document Tayak had seen but not registered, or registered but not understood, or understood but not yet felt the full weight of.

She had read it. She'd read the permit the way she read a site report — systematically, from the summary to the appendices, noting language shifts and insertions, marking the places where the tone changed from research protocol to something else. The clause was on page four, half a paragraph, tucked between a section on field crew composition and a section on curation timelines. She'd read it three times in the parking lot before going inside. She'd read it again in Slade's office while he was talking about his daughter.

*Significant historical value.* Whoever wrote that phrase knew what they were doing. It was vague enough to mean anything, specific enough to sound like a standard. It would mean whatever the Trust decided it meant, case by case, artifact by artifact, with thirty days and no custody and an appeal process that existed on pages eleven through fourteen of an addendum she hadn't been sent until this morning.

She passed the Baptist church. The reader board said COME AS YOU ARE.

She thought about the map on Slade's wall. The blue polygon. *SLADE FAMILY LAND — est. 1887.*

Two miles.

She'd spent two years building the case for this permit — the site surveys, the Phase I report, the tribal consultation letters, the environmental review, the correspondence with Voss about institutional sponsorship, the conversations with Marcus about what the council would and wouldn't support, the three separate meetings with the highway authority about the construction timeline. Two years of work that had all assumed one thing: that the state wanted to preserve the site. That Clause 7(b) was bureaucratic boilerplate. That the process, however slow and frustrating, was basically neutral.

She wasn't sure she still believed that.

The folder sat on the passenger seat. The permit was inside it — twelve pages plus the addendum, her signature on page twelve in the space marked *Principal Investigator,* the ink still less than two hours old. She'd signed it because the alternative was watching a highway crew grade the mound flat in October. She'd signed it the way you sign something when the other option is losing everything, which meant she'd signed it knowing she might lose things anyway, just more slowly, with documentation.

The road dipped. The canopy closed. She counted the seconds — one, two, three, the light going green-dark, the sound of the tires changing pitch on the shaded asphalt — and then it opened again and the water tower was there to the east, white and ordinary against the haze.

She thought: *Nora knew.*

Not the clause specifically — the permit hadn't existed until this morning in its final form. But Nora had known something. Had been waiting for something. The question she'd asked wasn't the question of a woman who'd been told the news; it was the question of a woman who'd been expecting the news and wanted to know how much of it Tayak had seen.

*Did you read all of it.*

Tayak had been excavating sites for fifteen years. She knew what it meant when a layer didn't match the layers around it — when the soil color shifted, when the inclusions stopped making sense, when something turned up in a stratum where it had no business being. You didn't ignore it. You didn't explain it away. You noted it, you photographed it, you changed your interpretation of the site.

She was going to have to change her interpretation of something. She didn't know what yet.

La Plata came up the way it always did — gradually, the woods thinning into subdivisions, the subdivisions giving way to the commercial edge, a gas station and a nail salon and a mattress store in a low beige building with a parking lot that was always three-quarters empty. Then the traffic light at the main intersection, red, and she sat behind a school bus with its yellow paint sun-bleached to the color of old paper, and watched the light and waited.

The town spread out around her in its practical, unhurried way — the courthouse with its brick face and its small flagpoles, the county offices in their low-rise cluster, the hardware store that had been there since before she was born. She'd grown up three miles from this intersection. She'd ridden her bike to the library on this street. She'd filed her first FOIA request at the county records office two blocks east, age twenty-four, still in her first year of graduate school, still believing that the documents she needed were simply waiting to be asked for.

The light changed. The school bus moved.

She pulled into the lot behind the county building and parked and sat for a moment with the engine running.

The permit was still on the passenger seat. She'd put it there in Slade's parking lot and it had ridden beside her the whole drive down, twelve pages plus addendum, her name on page twelve, the clause on page four. She picked it up and looked at the cover sheet — the state seal, the Trust's letterhead, the site number she'd been writing on sample bags for two years: 18CH447. The number looked the same as it always had. The document looked like what it was: a permit, bureaucratic and dense, the kind of thing you filed and referenced and occasionally argued about. It didn't look like something that had already begun to change shape.

But she had the feeling — the specific, physical feeling she got in the field when her trowel hit resistance at the wrong depth — that it had.

She put it back on the seat. She turned off the engine. The heat came in immediately, the August air thick with the smell of hot asphalt and cut grass and, underneath that, the faint green smell of the creek that ran behind the parking lot, invisible but present, the way water always was in this county.

She got out of the car.

Nora's house was three miles south. The mound was six miles east. She had a meeting with Marcus at two o'clock that she hadn't told him was coming, and she had a question she needed to ask her grandmother before she asked anyone else — a question she hadn't fully formed yet, because she was still working out what it was she didn't know.

She locked the car. She walked toward the building. The loblolly pines at the lot's edge stood in their dusty August green, motionless in the still air, and behind them the sky had the flat white look of a sky that was building toward something.

Nora's kitchen smelled the way it always had: black tea and beeswax and the particular sweetness of the old pine floor when the afternoon heat pressed down on it. Tayak let herself in through the side door, the screen door's spring giving its familiar complaint, and found her grandmother at the kitchen table with the quilt frame pulled close and a cup of tea at her elbow that had clearly been cold for an hour.

Nora didn't look up. Her hands moved through the fabric — a slow, deliberate pull of the needle, a pause, another pull — and the quilt spread across the table in its complicated geometry, cream and rust and a deep blue-black that Tayak had never been able to name exactly. The pattern was one she'd seen all her life without understanding it. She'd stopped asking what it meant sometime in her twenties, when it became clear that Nora would answer the question she felt like answering, not the one Tayak had asked.

"There's bread," Nora said. "And the tomatoes are on the counter. Don't eat them over the sink."

Tayak sat down across from her. She'd left the permit in the car.

"I signed it."

Nora's needle went through the fabric and came back. "I know."

"You couldn't know. I just—"

"You called Marcus from the parking lot." Nora looked up then, briefly, the way she looked at something she'd already assessed. Her face was eighty-two years of southern Maryland weather and forty years of teaching other people's children to read, and it gave away exactly what she chose. "He called me."

Tayak pulled the fruit bowl toward her, found an apple, and put it back. She wasn't hungry. "There's a clause. State retains right of first refusal on anything they deem significant historical value."

"Mm."

"That's all you have?"

"I'm thinking."

"Nora."

Her grandmother set the needle down flat against the fabric — not dropped, set, with the particular care she used for things that mattered — and folded her hands on the frame. Her knuckles were swollen at the joints, the arthritis worse this summer than last, though she hadn't said so and wouldn't.

"There is something in that mound," Nora said. "Something you need to find before the state does."

The kitchen was very quiet. Outside, a crow called once from the direction of the back fence and didn't call again.

"What something," Tayak said.

"When you find it, you'll know what it is."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the answer I have."

Tayak looked at her grandmother's hands on the quilt frame. The fabric under them was old — not the new cotton she ordered from the shop in Waldorf, but something older, washed so many times it had gone soft as skin. She'd seen that piece before. She didn't know where it had come from.

"How long have you known there was something there?"

Nora picked the needle back up. "How long have you known you wanted to dig that site?"

"That's not the same question."

"Isn't it."

Tayak pushed back from the table and stood. The floorboards shifted under her — the old house settling in the heat, or just the house being the house, which had been settling since before she was born. She went to the counter and looked at the tomatoes. They were from the garden, still warm from the vine, three of them sitting in a ceramic bowl the color of river clay. She picked one up and turned it in her hands without eating it.

"The clause means they could take anything significant," she said. "Anything that matters. If I find something that proves continuous occupation — a burial, a ceremonial object, anything that goes deep enough — they could pull it out of context and put it in a repository and I'd have no legal standing to stop them."

"Yes," Nora said. "That's why you need to find it first."

"Find it first and do what with it?"

Nora was quiet for a moment. Her needle moved. The crow outside called again, farther away now, moving toward the tree line.

"Document it," she said finally. "Understand it. Know what it is before anyone tells you what it's worth."

Tayak set the tomato back in the bowl. "You're going to have to give me more than that."

"I'm going to give you supper." Nora stood, slowly, with the careful mechanics of a body that had learned to negotiate with itself. She moved to the stove without hurrying, without any sign that the conversation had been anything other than ordinary. She filled a pot from the tap. "There's corn from Delbert's stand. I got it this morning."

"Nora."

"And I have chicken. It won't take long."

The evasion was so complete it felt structural — not a wall thrown up in the moment but something load-bearing, something that had been there for years and was simply visible to Tayak now in a way it hadn't been before. She stood in the kitchen of the house where she'd grown up, in the August heat that pressed against the windows and smelled of pine and creek water and old wood, and understood that her grandmother had been waiting for this conversation for a very long time and had decided, before it happened, exactly how much of it she would have.

She sat back down.

Nora put the pot on the burner and turned the flame up and stood with her back to the room, her shoulders straight in the way they were always straight, the way Tayak had spent her whole childhood trying to imitate without knowing why.

"The quilt pattern," Tayak said. "The one you're working on. I've never seen you use that fabric before."

A pause. Small, but there.

"It was my grandmother's," Nora said. "Ida's. She gave it to me the same night she told me the family stories. I've been saving it."

"Saving it for what?"

Nora turned from the stove. She looked at Tayak the way she looked at the quilt when she was deciding where the next seam would go — with a precision that had nothing to do with sentiment and everything to do with structure.

"For when it was time," she said.

She turned back to the stove. The water in the pot began its first, barely audible movement toward heat.

Tayak looked at the quilt spread across the table. The pattern she'd looked at all her life without understanding. The old fabric at its center, cream gone ivory, the color of something that had been kept in the dark for a long time and brought carefully into the light.

She thought: *she knows what's in that mound.*

She thought: *she's known my whole life.*

The question was why she hadn't said so. And the answer to that question, Tayak suspected, was the same answer she'd been getting all afternoon — not a refusal, exactly. A waiting. As if the knowledge had a proper order to it, and the order mattered, and Nora was not going to let her skip ahead.

Outside, the sky had gone the flat, pressurized white of a storm building from the south. The first thunder came a moment later — distant, below hearing almost, more felt than heard — and the pine tree at the corner of the yard moved for the first time all day.

The room she'd slept in since she was seven held the same four things it had always held: the iron bed with its white coverlet, the dresser with the warped bottom drawer, the shelf of field guides her parents had left behind, and the map.

She'd pinned it there at sixteen, a USGS topographic sheet for the southern Charles County quadrant, 1:24,000 scale, the contour lines so close together in one spot that she'd had to use a magnifying glass to count them. She'd circled that spot in red marker — a sixteen-year-old's certainty, the line thick and slightly wobbly — and written *why* in the margin, a question she hadn't known yet was the question she'd spend the next twenty years answering.

She stood in the doorway now in her socks, the permit folded in her jacket pocket, and looked at it.

The red circle had faded to rust. The paper had yellowed at the corners where the pushpins held it, and the tape she'd added along the bottom edge — because it had started to curl in the humidity — had gone amber and brittle. But the contour lines were still there, the small, unmistakable rise above the creek drainage, the elevation signature she'd recognized from a paper she'd read in her school library: *Earthwork construction in the Chesapeake watershed: survey and preliminary findings.* She'd checked it out four times. She still had it, technically. She'd never returned it.

She went in and sat on the edge of the bed.

The storm that had been building all evening had broken an hour ago and passed, and the window screen still held a fine mist of it, the smell of wet pine and creek mud coming through in waves. The thunder was gone south. The house had settled back into its nighttime sounds — the creak of the hallway boards, the low hum of the refrigerator two rooms away, the occasional drip from the gutter Nora kept saying she'd have fixed.

Tayak took the permit out of her jacket pocket.

She'd read it four times in the car and twice at the kitchen table before Nora had put supper in front of her and she'd stopped pretending she could eat and read at the same time. She read it again now, in the light of the bedside lamp, the same lamp that had been there since she was a child, its shade slightly tilted from some long-ago collision she couldn't remember.

*The principal investigator shall retain field documentation, including all provenience records, stratigraphic profiles, and photographic evidence, in a form accessible to the State Historic Preservation Office upon request.*

Fine. She'd planned to do that anyway.

*Curation of recovered materials shall be conducted in accordance with state guidelines for archaeological resource management, with the understanding that materials of significant historical value may be subject to review for repository placement.*

There it was.

She folded the permit again, along its existing creases, the paper already softening at the folds from the number of times she'd done this. She set it on the nightstand beside the lamp.

*Significant historical value.* The phrase was designed to sound neutral. It wasn't neutral. It was a door left open, wide enough to drive a state repository truck through, and she had signed her name beneath it because the alternative was a highway expansion crew arriving in September with a grading permit and no one to stop them.

She lay back on the bed without pulling the coverlet down. The ceiling had a water stain in the far corner, roughly the shape of the Delmarva Peninsula, that had been there since a roof leak in 2009. She'd been away at graduate school when it happened. Nora had dealt with it herself, as Nora dealt with most things — efficiently, without complaint, and without mentioning it to Tayak until it was already resolved.

She thought about that.

She thought about the quilt on the kitchen table. The old fabric at its center, the color of something kept in the dark. *Ida's*, Nora had said. *I've been saving it.* And then, when Tayak had asked for what: *For when it was time.*

Nora had been eighty-two years old for four months. She had arthritis in both hands that she managed with a particular stubbornness that looked, from the outside, exactly like not having arthritis. She attended every tribal council meeting. She made quilts. She grew tomatoes and got corn from Delbert's stand and cooked chicken on a Tuesday evening as if nothing had happened, as if her granddaughter had not just signed a document that could hand the state the right to take the most significant archaeological find in the county's recorded history and put it in a box in a climate-controlled room in Annapolis where no Piscataway person would have any legal standing to touch it.

*Before the state does.* That was what Nora had said, back in the first sub-chapter of the afternoon, when Tayak had told her about the permit. *You need to find it before the state does.*

Find it. Not *study it*. Not *document it*, which was what she'd said later, the more careful version. The first version had been *find it*.

As if she knew there was something there to find.

Tayak turned her head and looked at the map.

The red circle. The wobbly line she'd drawn at sixteen, alone in this room, with a field guide open on the bed and a library book she'd stolen by accident and a feeling she hadn't had words for yet — the feeling of recognizing something. Not learning it. Recognizing it. The way you recognized a face you'd seen in a photograph and then saw in real life, and the two images locked together and became one thing.

She'd told her advisor at Maryland, years later, that she'd chosen the site for its stratigraphic potential. That was true. She'd also told him she'd identified it through a systematic survey of regional topographic data. That was also true. She had not told him that she'd been looking at that red circle on her bedroom wall for twenty years before she ever wrote a grant proposal, and that the reason she'd circled it at sixteen was that her grandmother had once pointed south from the back porch and said, *that ridge above the creek, that's where the old people are*, and Tayak had gone looking for it on the map the same night.

She hadn't thought about that in years. She thought about it now.

*How long have you known there was something there?*

*How long have you known you wanted to dig that site?*

*That's not the same question.*

*Isn't it.*

She sat up. The bedside lamp threw a yellow oval across the permit on the nightstand. She looked at it for a moment, then looked back at the map.

Nora had known. Not suspected — known. The way she'd said *before the state does* wasn't the way you said a precaution. It was the way you said a fact you'd been carrying for a long time and had finally found the right moment to set down. And the quilt — the old fabric, Ida's fabric, saved for when it was time — that wasn't coincidence. Nora didn't do coincidence. She did structure. She did order. She did things in the proper sequence because she believed the sequence was part of the meaning, and if you skipped ahead you didn't just miss a step, you missed something that couldn't be recovered.

The question was what she knew. Not whether. What.

Tayak pulled her knees up and sat cross-legged on the white coverlet in the dark-edged room, the way she'd sat here at sixteen reading the library book, and thought about the chain of it. Nora knew. Nora had learned it from Ida — that same night, 1962, the stories and the fabric together. Ida had kept a private journal. Ida had made quilts using patterns her own grandmother had taught her. And back before that, farther back, the stories had been passed from person to person, generation to generation, each one handing the next a story about the one before, the chain of it going back —

How far?

She didn't know. That was the thing she didn't know.

She lay back down. The mist on the window screen had dried to nothing. The pine tree at the corner of the yard was still. The refrigerator hummed. The house breathed its old-house breath around her.

She was going to dig that site. She had the permit. She had the grant funding, she had Wren, she had her field kit in the back of the truck and a start date of next Monday and a plan she'd spent two years building. She was going to go down through the strata, layer by layer, and document everything she found, and the state could review her records and flag whatever it wanted to flag, and she would fight that fight when it came.

But there was a question she needed to answer before she put a trowel in the ground, and it wasn't a stratigraphic question.

She looked at the map one more time before she turned off the lamp. The rust-red circle. The contour lines crowding together above the creek. The word *why* in the margin, in her sixteen-year-old hand, still legible after twenty years.

She turned off the lamp.

In the dark, she thought: *Nora has been waiting for me to ask the right question. And I still don't know what the right question is.*

She thought: *But she's running out of time to wait.*

That was the thing she hadn't let herself think clearly until now. Nora was eighty-two. The words came less easily than they used to — not dramatically, not in any way anyone outside the family would notice, but Tayak had noticed. A pause where there hadn't been one. A reaching. Small, careful corrections. The arthritis in her hands that she managed by will alone.

Whatever Nora knew, it lived in her. And it would not live there forever.

Tayak lay in the dark and did not sleep for a long time. The house settled around her. Outside, the last of the storm moved away to the east, toward the Bay, and the air that came through the screen was cooler now and smelled of pine and wet earth — the particular smell of southern Maryland in August after rain, the smell she'd grown up with and carried in her body through every city she'd ever lived in, the smell that meant, without any effort of thought, *home*.

She did not sleep.

She thought about what Nora hadn't said, and how long she'd been not saying it, and what it would cost both of them when she finally did.
