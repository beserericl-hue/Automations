## FINDING 1: The Ossuary as Communal Secondary Burial

**The Fact:** Piscataway ossuaries were not immediate burials but staged, communal events: the dead were first exposed or temporarily interred, then — roughly a year later, once flesh had decomposed — bones were gathered and transferred in a prescribed ceremonial reburial. The Ferguson Ossuary contained remains of at least 207 individuals, representing deaths accumulated over a culturally prescribed period.

**Surprise for a modern reader:** The ossuary was not a dumping ground or a mass grave but a *scheduled community event* — the dead waited, in a sense, for each other. Two hundred and seven people were brought together at once, their bones carefully gathered and moved by living hands.

**Citation:** Smithsonian demographic study (Juhle site); regional synthesis on Atlantic-coast ossuaries; Ferguson Ossuary archaeological record.

---

### Sal-and-the-Potatoes Test

**1. What is the concrete fact?**
Bones were gathered by hand — from scaffold, from temporary burial, from wherever the dead had been held — approximately a year after death, and carried to the ossuary site in a deliberate, ceremonially governed transfer. Someone's hands touched every bone. Someone decided which bones were ready.

**2. What would an existing character have done because of this fact?**

**Red Fire Woman (The First Mother)** — in the year before the communal ossuary ceremony, she has been tending the scaffold where her eldest daughter's remains are exposed. She visits regularly. She is the one who must decide, by examining the state of the bones, whether her daughter is *ready* — whether the flesh has fully released its claim. On the appointed morning, she wraps the cleaned bones herself, bundle by bundle, in bark and cedar, and carries them the distance to the ossuary pit. She is the last to place her bundle in the ground. She does not weep until the earth is closed.

**3. What is the dramatic weight of that action?**
This is not grief as modern readers understand it — private, interior, concluded at a funeral. This is grief *stretched across a year*, with a physical task at the end of it: the mother must touch the bones of her child, assess them with her hands, and judge them ready for permanent community. The act of *deciding readiness* is unbearable. It also establishes Red Fire Woman's role as someone who holds the boundary between the living and the dead — which will matter when that boundary is violated later (colonial disruption of burial grounds, NAGPRA fights, the recognition battle). Her arc moves from keeper-of-the-dead to the dead herself to the ancestor whose bones are in a museum drawer.

**4. Where in the outline does this scene fit?**
Early in the novel's historical strand — before colonial contact, establishing the world the Piscataway are about to lose. This scene is the emotional baseline: *this is what it means to bury someone correctly.* Every subsequent desecration (excavation, removal, non-repatriation) lands harder because the reader has been inside the correct ritual.

**Scene seed:** POV: Red Fire Woman. Action: She unwraps the cedar bundle containing her daughter's femur and places it in the ossuary pit — the last bundle of 207. She smooths the earth. Page placement: Act One, early. Emotional consequence: establishes the sacred compact between the living and the dead that the novel will spend the rest of its pages watching be broken.

## FINDING 2: Three Sisters Agriculture — Women's Work Parties

**The Fact:** Women led cooperative work parties that planted, tended, and harvested the Three Sisters in mounded hills, with corn going in first (late April–May), beans once corn reached several inches, squash after beans emerged. Men focused on hunting and fishing during the lean pre-harvest period. Ceremonies of renewal accompanied planting and harvest transitions.

**Surprise for a modern reader:** The fields were not individual family plots but collective women's labor organized as work parties — more like a barn-raising than a kitchen garden. The agricultural cycle was also the social calendar.

**Citation:** Chesapeake ethnohistory, Powhatan/Algonquian sources; Eastern Woodlands agricultural practice.

---

### Sal-and-the-Potatoes Test

**1. What is the concrete fact?**
In late April, women moved into the fields together. One woman's decision to plant early — or late — affected everyone whose beans depended on her corn being tall enough to climb.

**2. What would an existing character have done because of this fact?**

**Red Fire Woman** — in the spring after her daughter's ossuary ceremony, she goes to the fields with the other women. She is the one who sets the planting day — her status and knowledge give her that authority. This year she delays. The other women wait. They watch her. She is still not ready. Finally a younger woman — not unkindly — takes the seed bag from her hands and begins to form the first hill. Red Fire Woman watches, then kneels beside her and plants the second hill herself. The work party resumes around her.

**3. What is the dramatic weight of that action?**
The moment when someone takes the seed bag is the moment the community gently reclaims her from grief. It is also the moment she re-enters the living world — not through ceremony but through dirt under her fingernails. Her arc: from the dead-keeper back to the life-keeper. The Three Sisters are literally what she tends instead of bones.

**4. Where in the outline does this scene fit?**
Immediately after the ossuary scene — the turn from death-ritual to life-ritual. It gives the reader the full seasonal world before contact arrives to disrupt it.

**Scene seed:** POV: Red Fire Woman. Action: She delays the planting day; a younger woman begins without her; she joins. Page placement: Act One, 15–20 pages after the ossuary scene. Emotional consequence: her first movement back toward the living; establishes the communal, women-led social fabric that colonial contact will fracture.

## FINDING 3: Copper — Prestige, Spirituality, Long-Distance Trade

**The Fact:** Piscataway copper came primarily from the Great Lakes via down-the-line exchange — traveling 500–1,000 km through many hands before reaching the Chesapeake. It arrived as small worked pieces or blanks, cold-hammered into ornaments (ear spools, pendants, breastplates, beads) that marked high status and carried spiritual power. After European contact, brass from kettles entered the same symbolic system.

**Surprise for a modern reader:** A piece of copper worn as a pendant by a Piscataway tayac may have changed hands twenty times over two generations before it reached him — and he knew it had traveled from a world he would never see, which was part of its power.

**Citation:** Great Lakes native copper archaeological literature; Eastern Woodlands Woodland-period trade network studies.

---

### Sal-and-the-Potatoes Test

**1. What is the concrete fact?**
When a Piscataway leader held a piece of copper, it was not local. Its distance was the point. After contact, a brass kettle cut into pendants carried the same freight of distant power — but now the distance was across an ocean.

**2. What would an existing character have done because of this fact?**

**Dr. Tayak Moyaone** — in the novel's contemporary strand, he is testifying before a Maryland legislative committee on tribal recognition. He places a small copper pendant on the table in front of the committee chair. He explains, in precise, academic language, that this object — or one like it — traveled from Lake Superior to the Potomac over perhaps fifty years and a dozen exchanges. He then says: *The recognition petition you are holding has traveled for thirty-two years through this building. I am asking you to be the last set of hands.*

**3. What is the dramatic weight of that action?**
The copper pendant is the scene's turn. It transforms a bureaucratic hearing into a moment of historical depth. It also characterizes Moyaone: he is someone who uses objects to make arguments, who understands that the past is not metaphor but material. His arc — the scholar-activist who must decide how much of himself to spend on a fight the system is designed to exhaust — pivots here. The pendant is also a callback to Red Fire Woman's world; the reader recognizes it.

**4. Where in the outline does this scene fit?**
Contemporary strand, mid-novel — a scene that bridges the historical and present-day storylines. The pendant is the physical through-line.

**Scene seed:** POV: Dr. Tayak Moyaone. Action: He places a copper pendant on the committee table and draws the parallel between the object's journey and the petition's journey. Page placement: Act Two, contemporary strand. Emotional consequence: the committee chair picks it up without asking permission; Moyaone watches; the reader understands everything about the power dynamic.

## FINDING 4: Potomac Tidewater Landscape — Late Summer and Early Winter

**The Fact:** The pre-contact Chesapeake was not wilderness but an intensively managed cultural landscape. Oyster reefs were navigation hazards. Anadromous fish runs (shad, herring, sturgeon) were so dense they were described by early colonists as nearly uncountable. The shoreline was shaped by Indigenous burning, weir construction, and shellfish management. A modern visitor would not recognize it.

**Surprise for a modern reader:** The river smelled of fish and woodsmoke, not water. Oyster reefs broke the surface at low tide. The passenger pigeon migration darkened the sky for days.

**Citation:** Chesapeake Bay environmental history; paleoecological reconstruction literature; colonial travel accounts.

---

### Sal-and-the-Potatoes Test

**1. What is the concrete fact?**
The late-summer Potomac at low tide exposed oyster reefs large enough to ground a canoe. The spring shad run was so dense that nets could be filled in minutes. These were not background features — they were infrastructure.

**2. What would an existing character have done because of this fact?**

**Red Fire Woman** — on a late-summer morning, she is repairing a fish weir at low tide, knee-deep in water, her feet on an oyster reef she has known since childhood. She hears the first English pinnace before she sees it. She does not run. She watches it navigate around the reef — the English don't know where the reefs are; they are moving slowly, probing. She has time to observe them before they see her. She memorizes the ship's draft, its slowness, its vulnerability in these waters. She is not afraid. She is measuring.

**3. What is the dramatic weight of that action?**
This is the first-contact scene, and it inverts the conventional dynamic: the Indigenous woman is the one with local knowledge, and the English are the ones who are lost. Her stillness and assessment establish her as someone who will survive by understanding what the newcomers don't know. It also roots the contact moment in the physical specificity of the landscape — the reef under her feet is the reason she has the advantage, briefly.

**4. Where in the outline does this scene fit?**
Act One/Act Two transition — the first appearance of the English in the historical strand. It must immediately follow the established world of the ossuary and the planting scenes, so the reader feels the intrusion.

**Scene seed:** POV: Red Fire Woman. Action: She watches an English pinnace navigate around an oyster reef she is standing on; she assesses their ignorance of the water before they see her. Page placement: Act One/Two hinge. Emotional consequence: she has a moment of power she will spend the rest of the novel losing.

## FINDING 5: The Tayac's Authority — Misread by the English

**The Fact:** The tayac's authority rested on tribute, alliance, and ritual prestige — not coercive command. Succession followed collateral lines (brother, maternal nephew), not primogeniture. English colonial officials forced this system into European "monarchy" and "land ownership" categories, sometimes backing rival claimants to fracture the confederation. Contested succession was an opening for colonial manipulation.

**Surprise for a modern reader:** When the English signed a land treaty with the tayac, they believed they were dealing with a king who owned the land. The tayac believed he was entering an alliance that obligated the English to him. Both parties left the same meeting with completely different agreements in their heads.

**Citation:** Maryland colonial records; Jesuit accounts; Chesapeake chiefdom ethnohistory.

---

### Sal-and-the-Potatoes Test

**1. What is the concrete fact?**
The English backed a rival claimant to the tayacship to get a more compliant interlocutor. The Piscataway confederation fractured not because of military defeat but because colonial officials found the seam in the succession system and pried it open.

**2. What would an existing character have done because of this fact?**

**The Ancestors** (as a collective POV or choral voice) — in a scene structured as a council, the Ancestors observe as a younger, more accommodating kinsman is presented to the English as "the tayac." The legitimate successor — older, less willing to sign — sits in the back of the longhouse. The Ancestors' voice narrates what the English cannot see: that the man they are crowning has no standing to give away what they are asking for, and that everyone in the room except the English knows this. The deed is signed. The Ancestors fall silent.

**3. What is the dramatic weight of that action?**
This is the political catastrophe scene — the moment the confederation is broken from the inside by outside pressure. It also explains the recognition fight: if the tayacship was fractured and misrecorded in the 17th century, the paper trail that the modern recognition process demands is contaminated at the source. Dr. Moyaone knows this. It is one of the things he has to argue around.

**4. Where in the outline does this scene fit?**
Act Two, historical strand — the political unraveling that follows first contact. It is the pivot from "the English are here" to "the English have changed what it means to be Piscataway."

**Scene seed:** POV: The Ancestors (choral). Action: They witness the English-backed installation of a compliant claimant as tayac; the legitimate successor watches from the back of the longhouse. Page placement: Act Two, historical strand, mid-novel. Emotional consequence: the deed is signed; the Ancestors' silence is the scene's final image.

## FINDING 6: Piscataway Language — Tayac, Quiakros, and the Limits of the Record

**The Fact:** Direct Piscataway lexical records are extremely limited. "Tayac" (paramount chief) is the best-attested Piscataway-specific title. "Quiakros" (priest/shaman) is attested in regional Algonquian sources. Most other specific terms must be borrowed from Powhatan or reconstructed comparatively. The language is, for practical purposes, a ruin with a few standing walls.

**Surprise for a modern reader:** The language itself is a casualty of the same colonial process the novel is about. The gaps in the lexical record are not an academic inconvenience — they are evidence of what was taken.

**Citation:** Eastern Algonquian linguistic studies; 17th-century Maryland colonial wordlists; missionary records.

---

### Sal-and-the-Potatoes Test

**1. What is the concrete fact?**
Dr. Moyaone, preparing testimony or a publication, reaches for a Piscataway word — a specific, correct word for a specific thing — and finds that it is not recorded anywhere. The word is gone.

**2. What would an existing character have done because of this fact?**

**Dr. Tayak Moyaone** — late at night, preparing his recognition testimony, he is trying to find the Piscataway word for "ossuary" — the thing he is arguing the state should protect. He searches every surviving wordlist, every colonial record, every comparative Algonquian database. There is no word. He writes in his notes: *They took the bones and they took the word for where the bones go.* He uses "ossuary" in his testimony the next morning — the Latin-derived English word — and feels the loss of it as a specific, physical thing.

**3. What is the dramatic weight of that action?**
This is a scene about the compounding of erasure: the bones were taken, the language that named the bones was lost, and now the man fighting to protect the bones must use the language of the people who took them. Moyaone's arc — the scholar who has built a career on historical evidence — confronts the limit of the archive. What do you do when the evidence you need was destroyed by the same process you are documenting?

Contemporary strand, Act Two — a quiet scene that functions as an emotional low point for Moyaone before the recognition hearing. It pairs with the ossuary scene in the historical strand: Red Fire Woman places the bones; Moyaone cannot find the word for what she did.
