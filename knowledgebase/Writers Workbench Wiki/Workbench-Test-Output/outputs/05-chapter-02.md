# Chapter 2: The River's Gift

---

The field notes from the day before were wrong.

Tayak had known it since 4 a.m., lying awake in her rental in La Plata with the ceiling fan ticking above her, going over what she had written. *Copper fragments, six pieces, consistent with Woodland period pendant construction, Great Lakes origin probable based on isotope signature pending lab confirmation.* All true. All beside the point. She had not written: *I saw a woman's hands. I felt the hammer. I smelled smoke from a fire that went out two thousand years ago.* She had not written that because she did not know how to write it in a way that would survive peer review, and so she had left it out, and the notes were wrong.

She was at the site by five-thirty, before the light was good enough to work.

The burial mound rose from a stand of tulip poplars in the southeast corner of the county parcel, low and broad, grass-covered now, easy to mistake for a natural rise in the land if you didn't know what to look for. Tayak knew. She had been looking for it, in one way or another, since she was nine years old and her grandmother had pointed at a photograph in a library book and said, *those are our people's houses for the dead, and somebody ought to find them.* Her grandmother had not meant Tayak specifically. But here she was.

The excavation trench cut into the mound's eastern face, three meters wide and braced with aluminum shoring that caught the first gray light and threw it back dull. She climbed down the ladder, switched on the work lamp clamped to the brace, and crouched over the burial layer.

Yesterday's copper fragments were gone — bagged, labeled, and locked in the Pelican case in her truck. What remained was the impression they had left: six shallow depressions in the dark clay, arranged like the petals of a flower, or like a body laid out with care. She had documented the arrangement. She had measured the distances between impressions to the millimeter. She had not noticed, until this moment, that there was a seventh depression.

Smaller than the others. Rounder. Set just below the lowest petal of the flower, where a stem would be.

She leaned closer. The work lamp threw hard shadows. She picked up the Japanese watercolor brush she used for fine work — the bristles were soft enough to clear soil from ceramic without scratching — and began to move the earth aside.

The brush bent against something hard after two strokes.

She set the brush down and reached for the wooden pick.

---

It took forty minutes to clear it properly.

The stone was the size of her palm, dark gray, smooth as polished bone. River-smoothed: she could see that immediately from the even wear, no facets, no tool marks, nothing but the patient work of moving water over centuries. She photographed it in place from three angles before she touched it, then photographed the depression it sat in, then photographed the relationship between the depression and the pendant's impressions. All of this was correct procedure. All of it was also, she was beginning to understand, a way of putting off the moment when she would have to pick it up.

The stone was not random. That was the thing she could not explain away. A random stone would have been angular — the burial layer's clay held its shape; anything dropped in would keep its edges. This stone had been carried here from running water. Someone had brought it, placed it, meant it to be found with the pendant.

She wrote that in her notes, using the language of archaeology: *intentional deposition, probable ritual significance, association with copper pendant artifacts strongly suggested by spatial relationship.* Then she read it back and thought: *you brought it here for someone. You brought it here for me.*

She picked it up.

---

It was warm.

Not body-warm, not the warmth of the work lamp or the morning air, which was still cool enough that she could see her breath. Warm the way the pendant had been warm in the committee room — from the inside, as if something in the stone was generating heat rather than absorbing it.

She nearly put it down.

Instead she reached into the Pelican case and took out the pendant. She had not planned to bring it back to the site. She had told herself she was bringing it to compare the copper's patina in natural light. That had been a lie she told herself at 4 a.m., and she knew it.

She held both of them. Stone in her left hand, pendant in her right.

The Potomac was maybe three hundred meters east through the trees. She had been hearing it all morning, the low constant voice of it, the way it moved over the rocks at the bend. She heard it now. Then she heard it louder. Then she heard nothing else.

---

The water was clear enough to see the bottom.

That was the first thing Tayak noticed — not that she was somewhere else, not that the aluminum shoring and the work lamp were gone, but that the river in front of her was not the river she knew. The Potomac she knew was brown with agricultural runoff, its banks cut back for soybean fields, its shallows choked with invasive grass. This river moved over white gravel and black stone, and she could see individual fish holding in the current, their sides catching the light like thrown coins.

She was standing on the bank. Behind her, tulip poplars rose with trunks wide as cars, their canopy so dense the understory was dim even at midday. The air smelled of wet clay and iron-rich earth and something green and living she had no name for. She breathed it and felt, with a lurch she did not expect, that she had been homesick for this smell her entire life.

A young woman stood waist-deep in the river, twenty meters upstream.

She had her arms raised, palms up, face tilted toward the sky. Her hair was loose and the current moved it behind her like something breathing. She had braided *mishkos* — the long river grass that grew at the water's edge — into the hair above her left ear, a braid so tight and deliberate it could only be ceremonial. Her face was marked with red ochre in two lines from cheekbone to jaw, the color of old blood. She was thin in the way of someone who had not eaten in days, her ribs visible above the waterline, her arms shaking slightly with the effort of holding them up.

Tayak understood, without being told, that this was the third day.

---

She could not move her own body, but she could see and hear and feel. She was a witness. That was the word that came to her: *witness.* Not a ghost, not a hallucination. A witness.

The young woman — Speaks with Rivers, a name Tayak somehow knew the way you know things in dreams — lowered her arms slowly. She was exhausted. Three days of fasting and cold water and prayer, and whatever she had been waiting for had not yet come, and the look on her face was the look of someone who had begun to wonder if they had been wrong about everything.

Then her foot struck something.

She looked down. She bent at the waist and put her face into the water, and when she came up she was holding the copper pendant.

The green patina had not yet formed — the copper was the color of a new penny, bright and startling in the river light. Speaks with Rivers stared at it. She turned it over in her hands. Then she pressed it to her chest, over her heart, and her face changed in a way that Tayak recognized: the look of someone who has just been told something true that they already knew.

The visions that came to Speaks with Rivers, Tayak felt as echoes — not the full force, but enough. Hands hammering copper. A woman's face, older than old, looking forward across time. A mound rising from forest floor. Smoke. Prayers spoken in a language that Tayak's conscious mind could not follow but her body understood the way her body understood hunger.

Speaks with Rivers waded to the bank. She sat in the shallows for a long time, holding the pendant, letting the water run over her legs. Then she picked up a stone from the riverbed — smooth, gray, palm-sized — and held it in her other hand, and Tayak understood: *this is how she found it. The stone guided her foot to the right place.*

---

Tayak followed without moving her feet.

A single longhouse stood at the edge of the clearing ahead, its elm bark walls dark with smoke, the smell of it reaching her before the building did. Children stopped and stared as Speaks with Rivers came through with the pendant and the stone. An elder woman came out of the longhouse and stood waiting — her face so creased and mapped with age that it seemed to contain its own geography.

Speaks with Rivers held out the pendant.

The elder looked at it a long time. Then she looked at Speaks with Rivers' face.

"You know what it is," the elder said.

"I know what I have to do," Speaks with Rivers said.

The elder's eyes moved to the stone. "Both of them?"

"They belong together."

One of the women behind the elder stepped forward. She was young, not much older than Speaks with Rivers, and her voice was careful. "You're talking about the mound."

"Yes."

"That's not yours to decide."

Speaks with Rivers met her eyes. "I found them. The river gave them to me."

"The river gives things and takes them back. That doesn't mean—"

"She will need them." Speaks with Rivers' voice didn't rise. "She will come from a time when our people have been told they do not exist. She will need to know that we have always been here."

The young woman looked at the elder. The elder looked at the pendant in Speaks with Rivers' hand.

"Then place them where she can find them," the elder said. "Both."

The ceremony happened inside, in firelight. Tayak caught only the edges of it — the elder's voice low and steady, the smell of something burning that was not wood, a sound like water over stone that had no source she could see. When Speaks with Rivers came out, the pendant hung at her neck and the river stone rode in a small pouch of woven plant fiber at her belt. The community had gathered outside and stood quiet in the way of people who understood that something had been decided.

Speaks with Rivers looked directly at Tayak.

Not at the forest behind Tayak. Not at the sky. At Tayak.

"You came," she said.

---

Tayak was on her knees in the trench.

She did not remember sitting down. The work lamp was still on. The Pelican case was open beside her. Her hands were in her lap, and in them were the stone and the pendant, and both of them were warm.

She was crying, which surprised her. She was not a crier. She had sat through three grant rejections and a tenure denial and her grandmother's funeral without crying, and now she was kneeling in a cold excavation trench in Charles County at seven in the morning, crying onto a two-thousand-year-old river stone.

She let it happen. There was no one to see.

After a while she set both artifacts carefully in their foam-lined compartments in the Pelican case. She photographed the stone's depression one more time. She measured it. She wrote in her field notes: *water-smoothed river stone, intentional deposition, associated with copper pendant, probable ritual significance.* Then she stopped, looked at what she had written, and turned to a fresh page.

She wrote: *Speaks with Rivers brought this stone from the Potomac and placed it here for me. She saw me. She knew I was coming. She buried the stone so I would find it, so I would understand that I am not studying these people — I am one of them, and they have been waiting.*

She stared at that for a moment. Then she wrote below it, in smaller letters: *This goes in the other notebook. Not the official record. Not yet.*

She closed the field notes and looked at the pendant through the clear plastic of the bag.

*Not yet* meant she was not ready to decide what it meant — whether she had experienced a genuine ancestral vision or a grief-induced hallucination, whether she was the scientist she had trained herself to be or the granddaughter she had never stopped being. She was not ready to choose. But she had written it down, which meant some part of her already had.

She climbed out of the trench and walked east through the tulip poplars toward the river.

---

The Potomac was brown and low, its banks cut back where the soybean fields ran to the edge. An aluminum johnboat was tied to a dock two hundred meters downstream, its hull streaked with rust. Somewhere across the water, a tractor was running.

Tayak stood at the water's edge. She took the stone out of the Pelican case — still in its bag — and held it over the water.

She was not going to throw it in. She knew that. It was evidence. It was the property of the excavation, and eventually of whatever institution or tribal authority had proper custody of the site, and throwing it in the river would be destruction of an irreplaceable archaeological record. She knew all of this.

She held it over the water anyway, feeling the weight of it, feeling the warmth still coming through the plastic.

*She saw me.*

The tractor across the river kept running. A great blue heron landed on a snag twenty meters downstream, folded its wings with the slow precision of something very old, and stood watching the water.

Tayak put the stone back in the case.

She had five more burial layers to excavate. She did not know what was in them. She did not know how deep the family went, or how many of them had left things for her to find, or how long they had been watching and waiting and placing objects in the dark earth with the patience of people who understood that two thousand years was not very long.

She knew one thing: the pendant had been lost. Speaks with Rivers had found it in the river, fifty years after Red Fire Woman placed it in the mound. Which meant the mound had been opened before — disturbed, violated, its contents scattered — within living memory of the people who built it.

Someone had opened the mound. Someone had taken the pendant out.

She looked back through the trees at the earthwork's low green silhouette.

*Who?*
