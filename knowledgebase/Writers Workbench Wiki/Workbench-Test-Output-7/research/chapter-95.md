# Chapter 95 — write-time research

- **Contemporary Midwestern salvage archaeology gear**  
  In small‑scale Midwestern digs, avocational and contract archaeologists commonly carry a 4–6 inch **Mason’s pointing trowel** or a **Marshalltown margin trowel**, but often stand with it idle while taking notes, photographs, or simply visually scanning the ground surface.[4]  
  They typically wear **kneepads, work gloves, and a canvas or nylon nail apron** with pockets full of flagging tape, Sharpies, film canisters or plastic bags for artifacts, and a metric tape or folding ruler.[4]  

- **Standard field recording practice at Midwestern sites**  
  When a significant artifact or burial context is located, field crews pause excavation to **record provenience** (exact horizontal and vertical location) in a **Rite in the Rain field notebook**, on a **standardized context form**, and often by **GPS unit** for integration into a GIS map.[4]  
  Saying a feature or individual’s *name* aloud at the edge of the unit can coexist with formal terminology like “Feature 12” or “Burial 3” written in the notebook and on the site map.[4]  

- **Green ground cover on a former mound site**  
  Many Midwestern mound or village sites now lie beneath or beside **soybean, corn, or alfalfa fields**, or in **mowed park grass**, so “the ground is green” can very literally be a **close‑cropped lawn, pasture grass, or low cover crops**.[2]  
  In municipal parks that preserve small remnants of mounds, grounds crews use **rotary mowers and string trimmers**, so the air often smells of **fresh‑cut grass and gasoline exhaust**, and the mound outlines can be subtle, softened by repeated mowing.[2]  

- **Soil profile in a disturbed burial mound**  
  A cut through a Midwestern burial mound typically exposes a **dark A‑horizon topsoil** overlying lighter **yellow‑brown or gray glacial till**, with **distinct darker lenses** where ancient pits, fires, or burials once disturbed the subsoil.[4]  
  Human disturbance, plowing, and historic looting create a **mixed plowzone** near the surface, full of rootlets, worm channels, and small artifact fragments; deeper down, the soil becomes cooler, denser, and more compact, with a faint **earthy, mineral smell** and occasional **mottling of iron staining**.[4]  

- **Material culture: shell gorgets**  
  **Shell gorgets** from eastern North American mound traditions are often made from **marine whelk shell**, traded hundreds of miles inland from the Gulf or Atlantic coasts, then cut, drilled, and polished into pendants worn at the chest or throat.[4]  
  Their incised designs can include **concentric circles, cross‑in‑circle, raptors, or human‑like figures**; when freshly excavated from moist soil, they feel **chalky yet cold**, and residues of soil cling to the grooves of the engravings until gently brushed away.[4]  

- **Material culture: carved stone pipe bowls**  
  Ritual or status pipe bowls from mound‑building cultures are commonly carved from **steatite (soapstone), catlinite (red pipestone), or fine‑grained sandstone**, drilled with a bore to accept a separate organic stem.[4]  
  A newly uncovered bowl often emerges **stained dark by humic acids**, its surface slick with damp earth; when brushed off, the stone shows **subtle color banding** and tool facets from ancient shaping with stone or metal tools.[4]  

- **Material culture: small figures and pendants**  
  Mound and village sites can yield **small anthropomorphic or zoomorphic figures in stone, fired clay, or carved shell**, sometimes with drilled suspension holes indicating use as pendants or amulets.[4]  
  Clay and stone figures may show **burnishing marks, red ochre staining, or soot**, evidence that they were once handled, buried with the dead, or placed near fires in ritual contexts.[4]  

- **Oilskin and conservation of organic containers**  
  **Oilskin** is traditionally made by saturating **tightly woven cotton or linen with boiled linseed oil or similar drying oils**, creating a stiff, water‑resistant fabric used historically for weatherproof clothing, pouches, or wrappings.[4]  
  When recovered archaeologically from damp conditions, such material can be **dark, stiff, and tacky**, smelling faintly of **rancid oil and mold**, and conservators must keep it cool and supported while drying to prevent cracking and distortion.[4]  

- **Contemporary curation of repatriated items**  
  Under modern **museum and tribal partnership protocols**, repatriated grave goods are often rewrapped in **unbleached cotton muslin and acid‑free tissue**, labeled with archival ink or tags, and placed in **polyethylene boxes** for controlled reburial or ceremonial keeping.[4]  
  Documentation of their journey—old accession numbers, catalogue cards, and digital images—remains in institutional records, while descendants may keep **new names and stories** orally, outside of official paperwork.[4]  

- **Soundscape at a small Midwestern dig**  
  In a rural North American setting, sound around a mound or village site might include **wind in grass or corn leaves, distant highway noise, passing pickup trucks, chain saws, crop sprayers, and birds such as red‑winged blackbirds, meadowlarks, or crows**.[2]  
  Conversations on site mix **technical terms** (“stratigraphy,” “feature,” “context,” “plowzone,” “Munsell color”) with mundane talk about **coffee, ticks, weather, and deadlines for cultural resource management reports**.[4]  

- **Modern descendant community presence**  
  At sites linked to Native ancestors, it is increasingly common for **tribal representatives, elders, or cultural monitors** to be present during excavation or reburial, sometimes offering **prayers, songs, or tobacco** before any soil is moved.[4]  
  Spoken names may be **carefully chosen, sometimes partially withheld from official records**, and voiced more fully only within the community, especially around human remains or sacred objects.[4]  

- **Legal and ethical framework: NAGPRA context (U.S.)**  
  In the United States, the **Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act (NAGPRA, 1990)** requires institutions receiving federal funds to **inventory Native American human remains and associated funerary objects and consult with tribes for return and reburial**.[4]  
  NAGPRA‑related work often involves **document searches, consultation meetings, and site visits** where museum staff, archaeologists, and tribal representatives jointly decide how, where, and under what protocols items and remains will be reinterred.[4]  

- **Contemporary small‑town Midwestern visual details**  
  A preserved mound or dig site on the edge of a Midwestern town might be bordered by **chain‑link or split‑rail fencing, a gravel parking pull‑off, and a simple metal or fiberglass interpretive sign** with a faded map and text about “Early Inhabitants.”[2]  
  Nearby are often **utility poles with sagging lines, a water tower with the town’s name, seed company billboards, and white or aluminum‑sided houses or trailers**, all visible beyond the green ground where the mound lies.[2]  

- **Soil and human body underground: temperature and sensation**  
  A meter or more below the surface, soil in temperate regions tends to stay near the **mean annual air temperature**, making burial spaces **cool, slightly damp, and stable**, insulating remains from daily heat and cold swings.[4]  
  Roots from grasses and trees thread through the upper layers, but deeper zones become quieter and more compact, with **occasional rootlets and insect burrows**, suggesting a layered vertical world where surface weather and light barely penetrate.[4]  

- **Oral tradition and nighttime storytelling**  
  In many Native and local communities, stories tied to sacred places and ancestors are traditionally told **orally, at night, and in winter**, when people gather indoors around **woodstoves, kerosene lamps, or electric space heaters**, and when farming or hunting work is minimal.[4]  
  The darkness and spoken word, rather than written texts, are understood as the proper medium for certain histories, reinforcing an idea that **the land “hears” what is said** and that memory is **carried in human voices rather than on paper**.[4]
