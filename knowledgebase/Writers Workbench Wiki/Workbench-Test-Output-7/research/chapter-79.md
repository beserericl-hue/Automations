# Chapter 79 — write-time research

- **Massachusetts gubernatorial communications (2024)**  
  The Governor’s office typically responds to fast-moving controversies with a brief emailed or posted **“statement” or “press release”** from the Communications Director or Press Secretary, then posts the same text on the **mass.gov** official site and social media accounts.  
  Such statements often use formulaic language like **“under review,” “stakeholder input,”** and **“no further comment at this time,”** and are timestamped and archived as public records by the Secretary of the Commonwealth’s office.

- **Colonial landmark designation in Massachusetts**  
  Colonial-era sites are usually designated under the **Massachusetts Cultural Resource Information System (MACRIS)** and may also be on the **State Register of Historic Places** or the **National Register of Historic Places**, each with separate nomination forms and reference numbers.  
  When a designation is questioned, the official phrasing typically refers to a **“re‑evaluation of eligibility”** or **“review of the underlying documentation,”** rather than immediate removal, because delisting or boundary changes require formal hearings and votes by the **Massachusetts Historical Commission** or relevant local historic commission.

- **Public records and digital archives (Massachusetts)**  
  Under the **Massachusetts Public Records Law (M.G.L. c. 66, §10)**, any document made or received by a public entity is presumptively a public record unless a specific exemption applies, including historic reports, land grants, and affidavits once filed in court.  
  Grassroots activists routinely exploit this by **posting scanned PDFs of public‑record documents** (court filings, historic surveys, state reports) on their own websites or shared drives and explicitly labelling them as obtained via **Public Records Requests** or **FOIA** (even though Massachusetts technically uses “Public Records Request,” not “FOIA”).

- **1690s New England land petitions and colonial terminology**  
  A **1697 petition** in New England would likely have been addressed “To His Excellency the Governor and Council” or to the **General Court** (the colonial legislature), and written in secretary hand on rag paper with formulaic openings like **“The humble petition of…”**.  
  Names of Native petitioners in Massachusetts were often **anglicized or partially phonetic** in the record (e.g., “Taya” becoming “Tayo” or “Taya alias…”), and petitions sometimes include a **mark or symbol** instead of a written signature, with a clerk’s notation “his mark” or “her mark.”

- **Material look of colonial-era documents (paper, ink, seals)**  
  Late‑17th‑century New England petitions and land documents were written on **hand‑laid rag paper**, visible under modern scanning as **parallel chain lines and irregular deckled edges**.  
  Ink would often have browned to a **dark sepia** from iron gall composition, and official approvals, grants, or receipts might bear **wax seals** or a seal impression with **“Colony of the Massachusetts Bay”** or the lion-and-unicorn royal arms after the 1691 charter.

- **Land grant chains and Massachusetts registries**  
  The **chain of title** in Massachusetts is documented through county **Registry of Deeds** volumes and page references, with early entries often beginning in the late 1600s or early 1700s and then carried forward by **“conveyance,” “warranty deed,” “quitclaim deed,”** or **“land patent”** language.  
  Modern title abstracts typically show a **chronological list of owners**, cross‑referenced to **book and page** numbers, and in some eastern Massachusetts counties the earliest volumes have been **digitized as black‑and‑white scans of handwritten folios**, complete with marginal notes and later scribbled references.

- **Affidavit format and legal language (Massachusetts, 2020s)**  
  A **2020s Massachusetts affidavit** is usually captioned with the court name (e.g., “Superior Court Department of the Trial Court”) and case docket number, titled “Affidavit of [Name],” and includes a **jurat** such as “Signed under the pains and penalties of perjury this [date]” above the signature.  
  When notarized, the notary block follows current statutory language: **“On this ___ day of ___, 20__, before me, the undersigned notary public…”**, with the notary’s **commission expiration date**, embossed seal or ink stamp, and commissioning county.

- **Contemporary Indigenous land-back and burial protection discourse (U.S./New England)**  
  Since the 2010s, Indigenous campaigns in New England increasingly use terms like **“land back,” “rematriation,” “cultural landscape,” “sacred site,”** and **“ancestral remains”** instead of older bureaucratic phrases like “Indian burial ground.”  
  Activists frequently connect local disputes over burials and landmarks with national frameworks such as **NAGPRA (Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act)** and, in Massachusetts, state burial laws (e.g., **M.G.L. c. 9, §§26–27C**) that govern unmarked burials and trigger **emergency archaeological review** when remains are discovered.

- **Digital archive platforms used by activists (2020s)**  
  By 2024, grassroots campaigns commonly host archives on **free, easy‑to‑use platforms** such as Google Drive folders, Notion pages, or GitHub repositories, often accompanied by a more public‑facing landing page built on **WordPress, Squarespace, or Carrd**.  
  Files are usually organized into subfolders like **/documents**, **/land‑records**, **/media**, and **/press**, and filenames often include dates and short descriptors (e.g., “1697_petition_scan_hd.pdf” or “Nora_affidavit_filed_2023‑09‑14.pdf”) to make them easily referenceable in social media threads and press inquiries.

- **Social media virality metrics (2024)**  
  On major platforms in 2024, a **share count of 200,000** in under a day is in the range achieved by **breaking national news, major celebrity statements, or high‑salience political stories**, especially when boosted by journalist and influencer accounts.  
  On X (formerly Twitter), virality is measured in **retweets/reposts and quote‑tweets**; on Facebook and Instagram, in **shares and story reshares**; TikTok measures **views and saves**, but activists often emphasize the **cross‑platform share count** in media interviews.

- **Hashtags and language around Indigenous issues (2020s)**  
  Indigenous rights campaigns in 2023–2024 often use hashtags like **#LandBack, #HonorTheTreaties, #ProtectTheSacred, #IndigenousPeoplesDay, #NativeTwitter,** and local variants referencing the people or place names.  
  Posts about burial grounds and sacred mounds frequently emphasize phrases such as **“not a park, not a picnic area”** or **“we are the descendants of…”**, sometimes tagging location‑based accounts like **@MassStateHouse** or **@[State]Governor** to direct public pressure.

- **Oral genealogies and name recitations (Northeastern Indigenous traditions)**  
  Many Northeastern Indigenous communities maintain **oral kinship chains** that link present‑day individuals to historic ancestors, recited as **sequences of names** that can extend dozens of generations and are used in both ceremonial and political contexts.  
  These recitations may include **both Indigenous and colonial renditions** of names (e.g., an original name followed by “called by the English…”), and are sometimes recorded during public testimony or at **tribal council meetings** to assert continuity of community and land occupation.

- **Recording and publishing oral testimony (2020s)**  
  In 2024, it is common to record oral testimony on a **smartphone in 4K or 1080p**, sometimes with a **clip‑on lavalier mic** to ensure clarity, and then upload directly to **YouTube, Vimeo, or platforms like Frame.io** before embedding or linking from campaign sites.  
  Activists often add **burned‑in subtitles** or platform‑native captions, include **content warnings** for discussion of death or ancestral remains, and provide a **text transcript** as a downloadable PDF to make the material admissible or easily quoted in news reports and legal filings.

- **Visual language of scanned archives (historical documents online)**  
  High‑resolution scans of 17th‑century documents often show **uneven ink density**, small holes from **iron gall ink corrosion**, and **fold lines** where the document was stored in bundles or docketed with notes on the reverse.  
  Online historical archives commonly add a **black or color calibration strip** along one edge of the image and a **watermark or reference code** in a corner to indicate the holding repository, such as a state archives or historical society.

- **Massachusetts cultural resource databases and tagging**  
  The **MACRIS** database lists historic resources with fields like **“Resource Name,” “Historic Use,” “Architectural Style,” “Period,” “Themes,”** and **“Area of Significance,”** along with a **unique inventory index number**.  
  Entries sometimes reflect older terminology—e.g., “Indian burial ground” or “Indian campsite”—and may be based on **mid‑20th‑century surveys** that gave little or no space for Native community input, a point often highlighted in contemporary critiques of colonial-era designations.
