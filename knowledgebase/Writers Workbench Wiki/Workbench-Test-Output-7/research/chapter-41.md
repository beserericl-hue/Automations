# Chapter 41 — write-time research

- **Pre-contact shell gorget material**  
  Mississippian shell gorgets were typically cut from large **marine whelk shells** (often Busycon species) from the Gulf of Mexico, then traded far inland along river networks.[2][6]  
  The finished disk was usually **8–12 cm in diameter**, carefully ground thin and polished to a soft luster, with a suspension hole near the edge and sometimes smaller piercings for attachment.[1][3]

- **Surface appearance and wear**  
  Genuine pre-contact gorgets often show **fine incised lines** from engraving, slight asymmetry from hand-shaping, and a patina where the original creamy white has mellowed toward **ivory or light tan**, especially in recessed areas.[1][7]  
  Microscopic scratches and rounding of the drilled hole edges are typical of long use as a pendant, contrasting with sharper edges on recent replicas.[1]

- **Iconography around 1200 CE (Mississippian tradition)**  
  Around c. 1200 CE, many eastern North American gorgets bore **abstract swirling “coiled” designs, forked-eye motifs, birds of prey, or dancing warriors**, linked to the Southeastern Ceremonial Complex.[2][6]  
  These motifs were not decorative only; they referenced **mythic beings, warfare, and cosmology**, and would have been legible as a kind of visual language to initiated viewers.[2]

- **Pigment traces**  
  Some Mississippian gorgets were **rubbed with pigments**, such as red ochre, to highlight engraved lines or backgrounds, leaving microscopic iron-oxide staining in grooves even after centuries.[1][7]  
  In raking light or macro photography, these pigments can appear as **dull reddish or brownish residues** clinging to incisions or around drilled holes.[7]

- **Trade and distance from the sea**  
  Marine-shell gorgets found in the interior (Ohio Valley, Appalachians, mid-South) indicate **long-distance exchange** from Gulf Coast shell beds up major rivers like the Mississippi, Tennessee, and Ohio.[2][6]  
  Their presence inland marks the owner as connected to **regional elite networks**, since marine shell was a prestige material embedded in diplomatic and ceremonial exchange.[2]

- **Chronology relative to European contact**  
  The **Mississippian period (c. 900–1600 CE)** predates sustained European colonization in most of the Southeast and interior, so a c. 1200 CE gorget is firmly **pre-contact**.[6]  
  Many classic engraved gorgets date to roughly **1250–1350**, so a stylistically similar piece securely dated to c. 1200 would be among the **older known horizon** of such ornaments.[2]

- **2024 dating and analysis methods**  
  By 2024, archaeologists typically date such artifacts via **stratigraphic context, association with diagnostic ceramics or stone tools, comparative style, and radiocarbon dates from the same layer**, not from the shell itself unless collagen survives.  
  **Microscopic use-wear analysis**, **X-ray fluorescence (XRF)** for trace elements, and **isotope analysis** of the shell can help confirm a marine origin and distinguish genuine ancient artifacts from modern forgeries.

- **Photography of the artifact (technical)**  
  Professional documentation in 2024 routinely uses **DSLR or mirrorless cameras with macro lenses**, shot on a neutral background with **color calibration cards and metric scales** to record size and surface detail.  
  Conservators prefer **diffuse, raking light** to capture shallow engravings and surface relief; RAW image capture and multiple exposure angles are standard for publication-quality documentation.

- **Field handling and conservation norms (2024)**  
  Contemporary practice emphasizes **nitrile gloves**, minimal handling, and placing the artifact on **acid-free foam or inert polyethylene supports** to avoid abrasion.  
  Any soil or accretions are generally left in place until a conservator can assess them, since residues may hold **pollen, microcharcoal, or pigment** useful for research.

- **Terminology: “gorget” usage**  
  The term **“gorget”** originally referred to a piece of European neck armor, but archaeologists adopted it for Native American **neck pendants** worn on the chest or throat.[1]  
  Specialists may also call them **“shell pendants”** or **“pectoral ornaments,”** especially when worn lower on the chest.

- **Regional context: Mississippian world around 1200 CE**  
  Around 1200 CE, **Mississippian towns with platform mounds, maize agriculture, and plaza-centered layouts** extended across much of the Southeast and mid-continent.[2][6]  
  Political power was tied to **ritual authority**, and items like shell gorgets, copper plates, and ceremonial stone objects helped mark **elites and ritual specialists**.[2]

- **Female inheritance and prestige goods (ethnographic parallels)**  
  Ethnohistoric records from parts of the Southeast and Eastern Woodlands describe some **prestige objects and ritual knowledge passing through matrilines**, with women controlling important property and ceremonial prerogatives.  
  Shell ornaments, beadwork, and garments were sometimes **held and transmitted by women as part of kin-based status**, even when men displayed them in public settings.

- **Modern Indigenous perspectives on artifacts (2024)**  
  Many contemporary Native communities view pre-contact items, especially **burial and ceremonial objects**, as **ancestors’ belongings** rather than “artifacts,” and advocate for their care or repatriation under laws like NAGPRA.  
  There is often tension between **academic research, private collecting, and community protocols**, influencing how such objects can be handled, displayed, or discussed in the present.

- **Legal framework in the United States (if set there)**  
  The **Archaeological Resources Protection Act (ARPA)** and Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act (**NAGPRA**) regulate excavation, trade, and custody of Native American cultural items on federal and tribal lands.  
  By 2024, even discussing an unprovenanced pre-contact object can raise questions about **looting, site disturbance, and rightful ownership**, especially if it may have originated from a burial mound.

- **Digital communication details (Nora’s call in 2024)**  
  A 2024 call about a delicate artifact could easily involve **high-resolution photos or short video clips** sent instantly via messaging apps, followed by a voice or video call for immediate reaction.  
  On modern smartphones, a user can **switch between camera and call**, send images over LTE/5G or Wi‑Fi, and zoom in on engraved lines while discussing features in real time.

- **Ambient technology and daily life (2024)**  
  Everyday background includes **push notifications, app alerts, and cloud backups** automatically saving the photographs as they are taken.  
  Power banks, **USB‑C cables**, and portable SSDs are common for fieldworkers and researchers to ensure extra storage and battery life when working away from labs or offices.

- **Visual and tactile sensory specifics of the gorget**  
  The **inner surface** of a well-preserved shell gorget feels **silky and slightly cool**, while the engraved side can feel faintly ridged where the incisions cut across the polish.[1]  
  Under bright light, the polished shell shows a **soft, nacre-like sheen** and subtle banding from the original shell structure, with tiny pits or weathered spots interrupting the gloss on ancient pieces.[1][7]

- **Sound and handling cues**  
  When the gorget is gently tapped against a hard surface, thin marine shell can produce a **dry, clicking sound**, lighter and higher-pitched than stone.  
  If it still carries a **faint earthy or mineral smell**, this can indicate long burial in soil, especially if residues cling in the suspension hole or incised grooves.

- **Shell gorgets in museum collections (comparanda)**  
  Institutions like the **National Museum of the American Indian** and the **Metropolitan Museum of Art** hold Mississippian shell gorgets with documented dates around the **13th–14th centuries**, featuring elaborate engraved designs and occasional pigment traces.[2][4][7]  
  Such published examples often serve as **stylistic benchmarks** for specialists when identifying and roughly dating newly encountered gorgets.
