# Chapter 21 — write-time research

- **Colonial legal terminology for bans on ceremony**  
  Provincial and county-level laws often used phrases like **“heathenish practices,” “idolatrous rites,” “powaws,” “powwowing,” “Indian conjurations,”** and **“superstitious ceremonies”** when targeting Indigenous ritual life.  
  Ordinances aimed at “civilizing” Indians frequently tied religious conformity to land rights, trade access, or legal status, so a ban on “heathen implements” would logically be posted alongside notices about **land surveys, militia musters, and trade regulations** in the county seat.

- **“Heathen implements” likely targeted by ordinance**  
  Items that could realistically be listed or seized as “heathen implements” include **carved or painted masks, rattles made of gourds, turtle shells or rawhide, drums, medicine bundles wrapped in skin or cloth, carved effigies, pipes used in ceremony, feathered or painted staffs, and bundles of herbs or roots identified as “conjuror’s medicines.”**  
  Colonial officials often did not distinguish between everyday tools and ritual objects, so **carved wooden figures, bundles of tobacco, painted bark, animal skulls, and ritual clothing with feathers or quills** could all be suspect if found together in an Indigenous household.

- **Format and placement of a county ordinance (posting at the seat)**  
  A mid‑18th‑century county ordinance would typically be **handwritten or printed on a single broadside sheet**, with a bold heading such as **“By His Excellency the Governor…”** or **“At a Council held at…”**, followed by the text in dense paragraphs.  
  It would likely be **nailed or pasted to the door of the county courthouse, the Anglican church door, or a public posting board** near the main intersection or market square, exposed to weather so the paper edges might be curled, stained, or fly‑blown.

- **Language and phrasing in the ordinance text**  
  Official language would be formal and heavily legalistic, using phrases like **“Whereas divers Indians yet persist in their heathenish superstitions…”**, **“be it enacted,” “on pain of forfeiture,” “to be seized and condemned,”** and **“to be proceeded against as the law directs.”**  
  Penalties could be stated as **forfeiture of goods**, **fines expressed in shillings or pounds**, or **“commitment to the common gaol”** upon refusal to surrender the items, with enforcement entrusted to **constables, justices of the peace, or “such persons as shall be appointed thereunto.”**

- **County seat environment on a Sunday in 1738**  
  The county seat on a Sunday morning would be quieter than a market day but still **busy with church‑going families arriving by foot, horseback, or in simple carts**, tying horses to rails or trees near the church.  
  A **small cluster of buildings**—courthouse, jail, tavern/ordinary, maybe a storehouse or trading shop—would stand near a dirt lane, with **muddy ruts, animal droppings, puddles, and the smell of horse, smoke, and ale** lingering even on the Sabbath.

- **Material appearance of the ordinance itself**  
  The notice would be written or printed on **rag paper** (linen/cotton based), slightly **off‑white, thick, and fibrous**, with **iron‑gall ink** that dries to a dark brown‑black.  
  If it has been posted for days or weeks, the **upper edge may be darkened by rain and smoke**, corners dog‑eared from wind, and the **text smudged by fingers** of people who held it to read.

- **Anglican/colonial church architecture and space**  
  A typical established colonial church in 1738 would be **rectangular, timber‑framed or brick if in a wealthier area**, with **small, leaded glass windows** and a **simple steeple or bell‑cote** if funds allowed.  
  Inside, there would be **box pews** or long wooden benches, a **central aisle**, and at the east end a **raised pulpit with sounding board** above it; lighting came from **window daylight and tallow candles** in iron or brass sconces.

- **Interior sensory details of a Sunday service**  
  The interior would smell of **pine or oak boards, damp wool clothing, tallow candle smoke, and human sweat**, especially in summer when windows are partly open.  
  Sound would be dominated by **psalm singing without instruments** in many Protestant churches, the **rhythmic cadence of the minister’s sermon**, occasional coughs or shuffling, and **creaks of wooden pews and floorboards** under shifting bodies.

- **Dress of colonial congregants (English/Euro‑American)**  
  Men would typically wear **linen shirts, waistcoats, knee‑breeches, woolen stockings, and buckled shoes**, often with **tri‑corn hats** removed on entering the church; wealthier men might have **powdered wigs**.  
  Women would wear **linen shifts, stays (corset‑like), gowns or shortgowns, petticoats, aprons, and kerchiefs**, with **mob caps or kerchiefs over their hair**, colors leaning toward **browns, blues, drabs, and dark reds** among common folk.

- **Indigenous dress adapted to colonial expectation**  
  An Indigenous man attending a colonial service in 1738 might wear a **European‑style shirt, waistcoat, and breeches or leggings**, sometimes combined with **moccasins instead of shoes** and **earrings or hair style (scalp lock, braids)** that mark his identity.  
  He might carry **a blanket or matchcoat draped around the shoulders**, made of **stroud cloth or trade wool**, with subtle **quillwork, beadwork, or colored ribbons** that could draw disapproving glances from colonists who see them as “Indian finery.”

- **Anglican worship structure on a Sunday**  
  Anglican services followed the **Book of Common Prayer**, with **set prayers, readings from the Old and New Testaments, psalms, and a sermon**, often lasting an hour or more.  
  Congregants stood, knelt, and sat according to the liturgy; **responses were spoken aloud** in unison, and **psalms were lined out** (read line by line by a clerk, then sung by the congregation).

- **Attitudes toward Indigenous worship among colonists**  
  Colonial clergy and magistrates commonly regarded Native rites as **“devil worship,” “sorcery,” or “delusion,”** reinforcing laws against “powwows” and “idolatry.”  
  Missionaries might publicly praise an Indigenous man who attends church, yet privately pressure him to **renounce “pagan” objects**, viewing any continued ritual practice as **backsliding or hypocrisy**.

- **Legal and social power of the vestry/church wardens**  
  In many colonies, the **vestry** (lay leaders of the Anglican parish) had authority over **poor relief, local morals, and church discipline**, and church wardens could **report offenders** to civil authorities.  
  Church wardens or vestrymen might be the very people **noticing “heathen implements”** and passing information to magistrates, blending ecclesiastical and civil surveillance.

- **Colonial enforcement mechanisms**  
  Enforcement of such an ordinance would likely be handled by **constables, sheriffs, militia officers, and justices of the peace**, who could **search houses with a warrant** based on “credible information.”  
  Confiscated items might be **burned publicly, stored in a court house as “evidence,” or kept by officials as curiosities**, sometimes described in court records as “idols,” “rattles,” or “bundles of Indian trash.”

- **Printed vs. manuscript circulation of the ordinance**  
  In more developed colonies, **printers in port towns or larger inland seats** could produce **small runs of broadsides**, so the same ordinance might be **posted in multiple parishes or towns**.  
  In less developed areas, a **scribe or clerk** might copy the text by hand, leading to **slight variations in wording** between copies and visibly different handwriting at different posting sites.

- **1738 imperial and colonial climate**  
  In 1738, the British Empire was on the verge of the **War of Jenkins’ Ear** against Spain, formally declared that year, fueling **heightened concern about frontier security and alliances with Indigenous nations**.[1][2][5]  
  Colonial authorities were sensitive to **Native alliances with rival European powers**, contributing to efforts to **control Native religion and gatherings**, which were sometimes suspected of being covers for “seditious councils.”

- **Disease backdrop (smallpox and Indigenous communities)**  
  1738 saw **smallpox present in the Thirteen Colonies**, an ongoing threat remembered in Native and colonial communities, with scars visible on survivors’ faces and bodies.[2]  
  Fear of disease sometimes intersected with suspicion of Indigenous ritual specialists, with colonists accusing them of **spreading or failing to prevent disease** through “false charms,” strengthening justification for suppressing “heathen practices.”
