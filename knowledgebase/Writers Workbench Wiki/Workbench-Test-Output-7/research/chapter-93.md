# Chapter 93 — write-time research

- **2024 quilting tools and materials (North America)**  
  Contemporary quilters commonly use 100% cotton quilting fabric, often from well-known brands like Robert Kaufman or Moda, along with cotton or cotton‑poly batting and cotton thread for machine or hand quilting. Rotary cutters, acrylic rulers, self‑healing cutting mats, and a walking foot or free‑motion foot on a domestic sewing machine are standard tools in a 2024 home quilting setup. Quilters typically press seams with a steam iron on a padded ironing board, and many use small quilt basting pins or temporary spray adhesive to sandwich the quilt layers.

- **Modern quilt design language**  
  A quilt panel depicting a landscape from above would likely be described as an *art quilt* or *pictorial quilt*, blending piecing with raw‑edge appliqué to create the aerial view of the mound and figure. Contemporary quilters often use *thread painting* (dense, colored machine stitching) and *free‑motion quilting* to add texture like grass, soil layers, and the outline of a standing figure. The quilt’s border might include a *binding* made from a continuous fabric strip, mitered at the corners and hand‑finished on the back with a ladder stitch.

- **Archival care of textiles in a university office**  
  To protect a historically important quilt, a university faculty member is advised to keep it out of direct sunlight, which can fade natural and synthetic dyes within a few years. Museums and archives recommend hanging textiles with a *sleeve* sewn along the back and a slat or rod inside to distribute weight evenly and prevent distortion. Acid‑free tissue, controlled indoor humidity (roughly 45–55%), and protection from air vents help slow deterioration of historic textile fibers.

- **Contemporary Indigenous studies / archaeology faculty offices (North American universities)**  
  Offices in anthropology, Indigenous studies, or archaeology departments typically feature a standard institutional desk, computer workstation with dual monitors, and wall shelving filled with monographs, edited volumes, and spiral‑bound field reports. It is common to see framed maps, aerial photographs of excavation sites, stratigraphic profiles, and posters from past conferences (e.g., Society for American Archaeology, Native American and Indigenous Studies Association). Faculty frequently display cultural items or community gifts—such as beadwork, baskets, or textiles—near the entrance where students immediately notice them.

- **Terminology for ancient earthworks and burial mounds**  
  In archaeological and heritage contexts, an ancient burial mound is referred to as a *tumuli* (singular *tumulus*), *barrow*, or *earthen burial mound*, depending on the regional tradition. In North America, earthworks built by Indigenous peoples are often described as *mounds* or *earthworks*, and archaeologists distinguish *conical*, *platform*, or *effigy* mounds based on shape. When seen “from above,” archaeologists today often work from *drone imagery*, *LiDAR* (Light Detection and Ranging), or *aerial orthophotos* that clearly show the outline and vegetation cover of such features.

- **Use of trowels in modern archaeology**  
  Archaeologists commonly use small, pointed masonry trowels (often 4–6 inch Marshalltown or WHS brand) for controlled excavation, scraping thin layers of soil and defining edges of features such as grave shafts or postholes. Trowels are regularly sharpened with a file to maintain a crisp edge, and the metal surface becomes polished and slightly discolored from soil and moisture over time. In field jargon, archaeologists speak of “troweling back” an area to reveal stratigraphy or of keeping a “trowel‑clean” surface for photography and recording.

- **Academic office wall display practices**  
  To make a textile the focal point of an office, faculty commonly hang it on the wall opposite the door or directly behind the primary meeting chairs so that it is immediately visible when students enter. Many offices have institutional off‑white or light beige painted cinderblock or drywall, which makes bright textiles stand out starkly in photographs and social media posts. LED overhead lighting is standard, but some academics add a directional floor lamp or track light aimed at the display piece, creating a gallery‑like effect while keeping illumination relatively cool to avoid heat damage.

- **Contemporary university student traffic and behavior**  
  During the academic year, students typically arrive at office hours carrying laptops, reusable water bottles, and sometimes noise‑canceling headphones around their necks or in their bags. It is common for them to glance immediately at distinctive visual elements—posters, artwork, plants—before focusing on the person behind the desk, especially in spaces where they feel anxious about grades or meetings. Many departments encourage faculty to keep office doors propped open during posted office hours, allowing passersby to see prominent art or cultural items from the hallway.

- **Digital documentation of community textiles in 2020s academia**  
  Faculty working with community‑made textiles often create high‑resolution digital photographs or short video clips to be used in teaching slides and online course shells (e.g., on learning management systems like Canvas, Blackboard, or Brightspace). It is common to accompany such artifacts with a short caption crediting the makers, the community, and the date range of the work, mirroring museum label practice. Some universities maintain digital repositories where images of significant community‑created pieces are stored with metadata fields such as creator, date, cultural affiliation, materials, and associated course or project.

- **2024 environmental and sensory details in a North American university building**  
  Many campus buildings have HVAC systems that produce a constant low hum, punctuated by the faint click of thermostats and the whoosh of vents adjusting. Hallway floors are often vinyl tile or low‑pile carpet, with squeaks or dull thuds from rolling backpacks and rubber‑soled shoes as students move between classes. Hand sanitizer dispensers, recycling bins, and posted notices about mental health resources, Indigenous land acknowledgments, or campus events are common visual elements near office doors.

- **Common 2024 clothing and accessories for faculty and grad students**  
  In 2024, it is typical for faculty and graduate students to wear casual or business‑casual clothing such as dark jeans or chinos, knit tops or button‑down shirts, and comfortable footwear like Blundstone boots, sneakers, or flat ankle boots. Smartwatches (e.g., Apple Watch, Fitbit) and wireless earbuds cases are frequently visible on desks or clipped to bags. Canvas tote bags printed with conference logos, publisher names, or slogans about libraries and decolonizing research are a frequent accessory in humanities and social sciences departments.

- **Quilt hanging hardware and methods in contemporary interiors**  
  Quilts displayed in non‑museum settings are often hung with a *wooden quilt hanger* that clamps the top edge, a curtain rod threaded through a fabric sleeve, or a strip of Velcro sewn to the quilt and attached to a corresponding strip on the wall. Adhesive hooks designed for damage‑free removal (e.g., plastic hooks with pull‑tab adhesive strips) are commonly used in offices where faculty have limited ability to drill into walls. The quilt’s lower edge may hang free or be gently weighted with a hidden dowel to keep it flat and prevent curling.

- **Smell and tactile qualities of a freshly finished quilt**  
  A newly completed quilt typically smells faintly of cotton fabric, laundry detergent or fabric softener if prewashed, and sometimes starch or fabric sizing used during piecing. The surface feels slightly textured where seams intersect, and there is a noticeable difference under the fingertips between densely quilted areas (stiffer, more sculpted) and lightly quilted sections (softer, puffier). When draped or handled, a cotton‑batting quilt makes a subtle rustling sound and has a satisfying, medium weight that can be felt through the arms when it is lifted.

- **Generational language for lineages in Indigenous and archaeological contexts**  
  Phrases like “great‑great‑grandmother’s grandmother” evoke deep time and are sometimes used in community contexts to emphasize long chains of matrilineal descent. In both Indigenous and archaeological discussions, such expressions highlight temporal depth that spans pre‑contact, colonial, and contemporary periods. Academic work on Indigenous heritage frequently stresses continuity across many generations, countering narratives that treat ancient mound builders or earthwork creators as disconnected from living descendants.

- **2020s discourse on community collaboration and heritage in universities**  
  Many universities have formal statements or policies encouraging community‑engaged research and the repatriation or respectful handling of ancestral remains and grave goods, influenced by laws like NAGPRA in the United States. It is increasingly common for syllabi in archaeology and Indigenous studies to foreground collaboration with descendant communities, with language about shared authority, co‑creation of knowledge, and acknowledging community intellectual property. Faculty often highlight community‑made items (like quilts) as visible symbols of these relationships, integrating them into teaching narratives about ethics, stewardship, and respect.

- **Campus building wayfinding and signage (2020s)**  
  Office doors commonly display a printed nameplate with the faculty member’s name, title, and department, sometimes alongside pronoun stickers or small decals signifying safe‑space or allyship initiatives. Near the door, there may be a printed schedule of office hours taped to the wall or pinned to a corkboard, updated each term and sometimes annotated in pen. Hallway directional signs use standardized fonts and icons to indicate room numbers, stairwells, elevators, and restrooms, contributing to a uniform institutional aesthetic around the office doorway.
