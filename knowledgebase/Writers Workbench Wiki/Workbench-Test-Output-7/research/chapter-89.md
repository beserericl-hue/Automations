# Chapter 89 — write-time research

- **Digital archaeological documentation in 2020s**  
  Contemporary US field projects routinely combine **total station or GNSS (RTK GPS) data with photogrammetry** to create 3D models of trenches and stratigraphic units, often processed in software like Agisoft Metashape or ArcGIS Pro.  
  Printed reports increasingly embed stills from Structure-from-Motion models and export 3D stratigraphic profiles as PDFs alongside traditional Harris matrices.

- **Length and format of formal site reports**  
  A full **Phase III excavation report** for a multi-season site can easily run to several hundred pages of text, plus appendices with artifact catalogs, radiocarbon tables, and specialized analyses; 800+ pages is not unusual once graphics are included.  
  Reports are typically laid out in **US letter or A4 format** with standardized sections: abstract, project background, methods, results by context/feature, artifact analysis, environmental analysis, interpretation, and recommendations.

- **Hand-drawn artifact illustration in the 2020s**  
  Even in the age of digital photography, artifact illustrators still produce **archaeological line drawings** in ink or digital tablet, emphasizing rim profiles, cross-sections, and decoration, following conventions like 1:1 or 1:2 scale and heavy lining for exterior edges.  
  Many illustrators now ink drawings on bristol board, scan them at 600 dpi, and finish line weight, stippling, and grey fills in software like Adobe Illustrator.

- **Stratigraphic mapping and 3D representation**  
  Stratigraphic units are numbered as **contexts** or **loci**, each with recording sheets noting soil texture, Munsell color, inclusions, and relationships (e.g., “cut by,” “fills”).  
  3D mapping commonly relies on **grid coordinates tied to a site datum**, with depths recorded relative to sea level; iso-depth contours and section drawings are still standard alongside digital 3D models.

- **Principal investigator vs. field assistant roles**  
  In US cultural resource management and academic projects, the **principal investigator (PI)** is the senior archaeologist legally and ethically responsible for research design, permits, curation agreements, and final reporting, and is listed first on the title page.  
  **Field assistants** or **field technicians** perform excavation, basic recording, and sometimes preliminary analysis, but they rarely receive top billing in official documentation, even if they did most of the day‑to‑day work.

- **Acknowledgments in technical reports**  
  Archaeological reports often include **personal acknowledgments** thanking landowners, funding agencies, tribal representatives, students, and family members for support during the project.  
  It is not unusual for an author to note a **personal or ancestral connection to the land** in the acknowledgments, especially when the project took place on family property or in a home community.

- **Calvert County land grants and colonial context**  
  **Calvert County, Maryland** originated as part of a 17th‑century proprietary colony granted to **Cecil Calvert, 2nd Lord Baltimore**, under a 1632 charter from King Charles I.  
  Early **land grants in Maryland** were recorded as patents issued under the Calvert proprietorship, often measured in **acres using metes-and-bounds descriptions**, referencing natural features like trees, creeks, and “marked stones.”

- **Maryland land records and family farms**  
  Modern property records in Maryland trace back through **colonial patents, 19th‑century deeds, and subdivision plats**, often available through county land record offices and the Maryland State Archives.  
  Many **multi‑generation family farms** in Calvert County still occupy parcels originally assembled from 17th–18th century land grants, even if boundaries were adjusted over time.

- **Ethical issues of excavation on family land**  
  Professional standards (e.g., the **Society for American Archaeology principles**) emphasize **conflict-of-interest disclosure**, especially when excavating or publishing on property in which the researcher has a personal stake.  
  Families sometimes object to **publicizing burial sites or sacred places** on their land, fearing vandalism, looting, or regulatory restrictions; this can create tension when a report precisely maps features and locations.

- **US burial mound legal framework in the 2020s**  
  Any pre-contact or historic human remains discovered in the US can trigger state burial laws and, for Native American remains, **NAGPRA (Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act)** procedures if federal or tribal lands or institutions are involved.  
  On **private land in Maryland**, discovery of human remains must be reported to authorities; subsequent treatment of an archaeological burial may involve the state historic preservation office and, where applicable, tribal consultation.

- **State Historic Preservation Office (SHPO) involvement**  
  Most formal excavation reports, especially if public permitting or funding are involved, are **submitted to the State Historic Preservation Office** and become part of the permanent state site files.  
  SHPOs typically require **multiple hard copies plus a digital PDF**, with archiving standards that include acid-free paper and permanent digital formats.

- **Curation of artifacts and records**  
  Artifacts and documentation from a professional excavation are usually curated at an **approved repository**, such as a state museum or university archaeology lab, under a formal curation agreement.  
  Curation standards call for **archival boxes, acid‑free bags with Tyvek labels, stable ink pens, and separated storage for human remains** if present.

- **Technical appearance of an 847-page report**  
  A report of this size would be divided into **volumes or thick spiral/comb-bound parts**, potentially with one volume dedicated to text and others to plates, artifact drawings, and context inventories.  
  Large fold‑out site maps and stratigraphic profiles may be inserted in **pockets at the back**, or provided as oversized plates in a separate portfolio.

- **Illustration and layout workflow in 2024**  
  By 2024, most archaeological reports are composed in **Microsoft Word, LaTeX, or InDesign**, with figure numbering automated and cross‑references hyperlinked in the digital version.  
  Artifact plates are often laid out as **vector graphics** with scale bars, north arrows for plans, and consistent fonts for labels, exported at high resolution for print.

- **Maryland coastal plain environment and soils**  
  Calvert County sits on the **Western Shore of the Chesapeake Bay**, made up of unconsolidated sands, silts, and clays of the Atlantic Coastal Plain, prone to **erosion and slumping along steep bluffs**.  
  Archaeological strata often include **plowzone Ap horizons**, underlying **Bt horizons of clay-rich subsoil**, and sometimes **colluvial fans or shell-rich lenses**, with Munsell colors in yellowish-brown to reddish-brown ranges.

- **Local flora and fauna around Calvert County sites**  
  Common tree species include **loblolly pine, tulip poplar, red maple, and various oaks**, with understory plants like **greenbrier, mountain laurel, and spicebush**.  
  Typical wildlife includes **white-tailed deer, gray squirrels, raccoons, and ospreys or bald eagles** along the Chesapeake, whose calls and wingbeats are noticeable near open water.

- **Weather and field conditions in contemporary Maryland**  
  Field seasons in Maryland often avoid the **coldest winter months** and the **hottest, most humid midsummer weeks**, but spring and fall can still bring **sudden thunderstorms, clay turning slick, and mosquitoes rising from marshy areas**.  
  Chesapeake humidity leaves **paper forms limp and edges curled**, and fine silt dust adheres to sweat and sunscreen on skin and clothing.

- **Sensory details of the lab and report-writing environment**  
  An archaeology lab handling a large site generates smells of **wet soil drying in trays, archival cardboard, ethanol from cleaning tools, and coffee**.  
  The soundscape often includes **low HVAC hum, the whir of scanners, clacking of keyboards, and the snap of plastic artifact bags being opened and sealed**.

- **Common field documentation components**  
  Standard documentation includes **context sheets, feature forms, level records, photo logs, plan and profile drawings, and bag inventories**, all cross‑referenced by unique context and provenience numbers.  
  **Field notebooks** may supplement forms, containing day-by-day notes, sketches, weather, crew assignments, and interpretive thoughts written in waterproof ink.

- **Three-dimensional recording of artifacts in situ**  
  Artifacts and features of interest (e.g., burials, hearths) may have their **XYZ coordinates recorded individually**, allowing plotting in 3D space relative to the site grid.  
  High-significance finds are photographed **in situ with metric scales, north arrows, and context tags**, sometimes also captured in 3D using close-range photogrammetry.

- **Adhesives and inks used for tagging artifacts**  
  In the 2020s, ceramic and lithic artifacts are typically labeled with **B-72 (Paraloid) acrylic resin as a base and top coat**, sandwiching a written ID number in archival ink or on acid-free paper.  
  Labeling sessions produce the distinctive **acetone smell** from diluted resin, and artifacts dry on **wire racks or foam-lined trays**.
