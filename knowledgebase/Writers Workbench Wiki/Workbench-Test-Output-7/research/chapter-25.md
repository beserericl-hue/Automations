# Chapter 25 — write-time research

- **Ceremonial pipe bowl (material, form, and use)**  
  In many Eastern Woodlands and Plains cultures by the 1740s, a *ceremonial pipe* would often have a carved stone bowl (frequently red catlinite in some regions, though other soft stones and fired clay were also used) fitted to a separate wooden stem.[1]  
  Bowls used in solemn community rites were often kept smooth and undecorated, while the *stem* bore most of the carving—spirals, animal effigies, or notches marking important events.[1]  
  Pipes were not smoked casually during such rites; the smoke was understood as carrying spoken prayers upward, so each draw, and the direction in which the stem was pointed (to the four directions, sky, and earth), had prescribed meaning.[1]  

- **Carved figures (iconography and materials)**  
  Small carved ritual figures in the 18th century Eastern half of North America were commonly made from local hardwoods (maple, elm, oak) or softer basswood and sometimes from antler or bone, then darkened with bear grease and soot.[1]  
  Human or spirit figures often had simplified, forward-facing bodies, prominent noses, and drilled eyes that could be inlaid with shell or glass trade beads obtained from Europeans by the 1740s.[1][3]  
  Figures used in community rites were often oiled or smeared with red ochre before important ceremonies, leaving a faint metallic, earthy smell and a reddish residue on the hands of the person handling them.[1]  

- **Prayer sticks / prayer bundle (construction and symbolism)**  
  Prayer sticks in 18th‑century Indigenous practice were typically small peeled branches or split slats, sometimes with one end sharpened to drive into earth, the upper end wrapped in dyed plant fiber, fur, or cloth taken from trade garments.[1][3]  
  They might be tied together in a *bundle* with sinew or plant cordage, sometimes with attached feathers, small animal bones, or shells, each item representing a person, vow, or spirit being addressed.[1]  
  The sticks would often be scented with smoke from burning sage, sweetgrass, or local aromatic plants, so that when Matta unties and handles them, there is a lingering resinous, slightly sweet odor even outdoors.[1]  

- **Oilskin layer (Okwi’s stratum) – technology and feel**  
  By 1743, Native people in many regions had access to European-made oilcloth or oiled linen from traders, as well as their own waterproofed leather made by rubbing in bear fat, tallow, or fish oil and smoke-curing it.[1][3]  
  An *oilskin layer* in a burial mound would feel slightly tacky or waxy to the touch, smell faintly of rancid fat or tar, and shed water so that droplets bead and run off rather than soaking into the fabric.[1]  
  Such a layer would tend to stick to adjacent soil and leaves, so when Matta digs down, his tool and hands may pull up flakes of greasy, darkened soil clinging to the preserved sheet.[1]  

- **Stratified mound structure (recognizable layers)**  
  A reused mound in 1743 would show distinct bands as Matta digs: a darker humus-rich topsoil with roots; beneath that, a compacted layer of older fill with bits of charcoal or bone; deeper still, earlier ceremonial deposits like Okwi’s oilskin.[1]  
  Soil from older construction episodes is often more tightly packed and slightly lighter in color than the surrounding forest loam, making it feel harder under a digging stick and causing the tool to ring slightly differently when struck.[1]  
  If the mound has ever been burned as part of a previous rite, he may encounter a thin, black band of charcoal flecks and ash, with a dry, dusty smell, distinct from the moist forest earth above.[1]  

- **Night sky in 1743 (visibility and atmosphere)**  
  In 1743, there was essentially no artificial light pollution outside a few colonial ports and forts, so the **Milky Way would be vivid**, casting a faint grayish band across the entire sky on clear, moonless nights.[1]  
  The year 1743 is notable for a bright comet, C/1743 C1, which became visible from Earth in early 1743; if your scene is set around that time, some nights would feature an unusual star with a visible tail low in the sky, a phenomenon likely to attract spiritual interpretation.[3]  
  Without mechanical clocks, many Native communities reckoned time at night by the height and position of familiar stars or constellations (for example, watching the rotation of a “dipper” shape around the north star), making references like “when that star stands above the tree line.”[1]  

- **Lighting at the mound (fire technology and sensory detail)**  
  For stealth, Matta would likely avoid large open flames; if he uses fire at all, it would be a small, carefully shielded ember bundle or a tightly controlled pitch-pine or fatwood splinter torch that produces bright, resinous light and heavy smoke.[1]  
  A burning pine-splinter torch crackles softly and drips tiny beads of resin, giving off a sharp, turpentine-like smell and throwing a warm, flickering circle of light that makes tree trunks beyond seem to swim in darkness.[1]  
  Attentive people in 1743 were very aware that even a small fire’s glow carries far at night, so he might work by memory and touch once the pit is open, relying on the feel of the objects rather than sight.[1]  

- **Tools for digging and handling objects**  
  In 1743, Indigenous men typically still used *wooden digging sticks* hardened by fire, stone adzes, and antler picks for ceremonial digging, even if iron tools were available through trade; iron knives and hatchets were present but reserved or used selectively.[1][3]  
  A digging stick would be about arm’s length, slightly charred and polished at the tip, with calloused, sweat-slick handgrips polished darker from long use.[1]  
  For handling sacred objects, bare hands were common, but hands might be deliberately smeared with clean earth or ash first as a form of ritual purification, leaving noticeable dust on the objects themselves.[1]  

- **Clothing and textiles (Matta and Tessa)**  
  By the 1740s, many Eastern Native communities wore a combination of traditional dress and European trade cloth: **wool strouding** (thick blue or red broadcloth) cut into leggings, breechclouts, and wrap-around skirts, often trimmed with narrow ribbon.[1][3]  
  Shirts or shifts were frequently made from linen obtained via trade, smelling faintly of lye soap or smoke if washed and dried by the fire; older buckskin garments smelled of smoke and animal fat and softened with body heat and movement.[1]  
  Glass seed beads and metal trade brooches were increasingly common, catching and briefly flashing back any stray firelight or starlight when the wearer moves.[1]  

- **Footwear and movement in the dark**  
  Soft-soled **buckskin moccasins** were standard, often lined with dried grass or fur for warmth, allowing nearly silent movement over forest duff and making a characteristic soft scuffing in dry leaves.[1]  
  After a day’s wear, moccasins would be damp from sweat and ground moisture, smelling of wet leather and earth, and would pick up needles, leaves, and bits of soil in the stitching along the sole.[1]  
  In damp or cold conditions, people might rub bear grease or tallow into their moccasins before heading out, both waterproofing the leather and leaving a faint animal-fat smell noticeable in close quarters.[1]  

- **Local forest environment (eastern woodlands example)**  
  A typical 18th‑century North American **eastern deciduous forest** at night would be dense with oaks, maples, hickories, and beeches, with an understory of saplings and shrubs that brush and snag clothing as one moves.[1]  
  The ground underfoot would be a thick, slightly springy mat of last year’s leaves and twigs, with damp, fungal scents and occasional patches of exposed roots that catch bare toes or the edge of a moccasin.[1]  
  At night, insect hum (crickets, katydids) and distant sounds of small mammals moving through the leaves are constant, so a sudden silence often signals the approach of a larger animal or human.[1]  

- **Tree line vantage (Tessa’s position and sensory detail)**  
  From just inside the tree line, someone watching the mound would see only a broken view of any light—flickers between trunks and branches, not a steady glow—making it easy to miss unless she is actively looking.[1]  
  Tree bark in older forests is rough and often damp or mossy; leaning against it leaves a cool, slightly wet spot on clothing and the smell of lichen and crushed moss on hands or shoulders.[1]  
  At night, eyes adjust so that colors wash out, turning reds to dark gray; a red wool trade blanket or skirt seen from the tree line would register mainly as a deeper patch of shadow against lighter leaves.[1]
