# Chapter 90 — write-time research

- **NAGPRA-style reburial protocols (U.S.)**  
  In contemporary U.S. practice under the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act (NAGPRA), reburials are often done quietly with only tribal representatives, key elders, and designated cultural staff present, and locations may be kept confidential to prevent looting.  
  Items are typically placed back into the ground in relation to their original provenience (the specific layer or locus where they were excavated), following the archaeological records as closely as possible.  
  Soil removed during excavation is often stockpiled and later sifted or screened before being replaced, so the backfill fills the stratigraphic layers in their previous order as much as is feasible.

- **Stratigraphy and artifact “layers”**  
  Archaeologists record artifacts by **stratigraphic unit** (distinct soil layers differentiated by color, texture, or composition) and by **depth below datum**, so each object has a precise vertical and horizontal coordinate.  
  When “returning” artifacts, tribal monitors and archaeologists may use field maps and context sheets to approximate where in the profile each object originally lay, even if the mound’s interior has been disturbed.  
  Different layers often have characteristic soils: older buried surfaces might be darker, richer in organic matter, while construction layers can be lighter, sandier, or more compacted with small gravels or crushed shell.

- **Backfilling the chamber**  
  In many protected mound sites, re-sealing involves filling the chamber with clean fill or screened original soil, tamping it in gradually so there are no large air voids that could cause later collapse.  
  The uppermost part of the fill might be finished with a layer of sod or native grasses taken from nearby so the surface visually blends back into the landscape and resists erosion.  
  Temporary wooden forms or shoring used during excavation are often removed before final closure so that only soil and original stone or earth remain in the chamber.

- **Wooden figure: material and weathering**  
  A wooden figure from an Eastern woodland mound would likely be carved from rot-resistant hardwood such as **black walnut, oak, hickory, or maple**, selected for fine grain.  
  After centuries underground, wood often becomes spongy or mineral-stained; conservation sometimes leaves a faint scent of solvents or consolidants (like Paraloid resins), a chemical tang mixed with the earthy smell of old soil.  
  Surfaces may show microscopic tool marks from stone or metal blades, and recessed areas hold persistent dark soil staining that never fully cleans off.

- **Copper pendant: “Old Copper Complex” traits**  
  Pre-contact copper artifacts in the Eastern Woodlands were often made from **cold-hammered native copper**, giving them a slightly rough, hammered texture rather than a cast smoothness.  
  Once excavated, such pendants usually oxidize to mottled greens and browns (malachite and other copper corrosion products), and may have bright scar-like areas where conservators removed more recent corrosion or encrusted soil.  
  Under strong light, even heavily patinated copper can show flashes of dull reddish metal on edges or high points where centuries of handling or soil pressure polished the surface.

- **Shell gorgets: engraving and feel**  
  Shell gorgets made from marine conch or whelk have a soft, chalky feel at broken edges, but the surviving polished face is smooth and cool, often slightly concave.  
  Carved designs—spirals, raptors, dancers, weeping eye motifs—are incised with shallow grooves that trap dark residue from the burial environment, making the patterns more visible.  
  When handled, shell gives off a faint calcium dust smell, and very old pieces can emit a dry, brittle “tick” when lightly tapped against stone or bone.

- **Pipe bowl: stone and residue**  
  Many ceremonial pipes from mound contexts are carved from **steatite (soapstone)** or fine-grained sandstones, giving them a soft, soapy texture that warms quickly in the hand.  
  Inside the bowl and along the bore, there may be blackened residue from ancient tobacco or other plant mixtures; in modern labs, this residue has been used to identify species like **Nicotiana rustica**.  
  Any modern handling with gloves may leave faint latex or nitrile smell, contrasting sharply with the organic tar scent of old combusted plant material.

- **Antler figure: texture and aging**  
  Antler artifacts show a distinctive porous core and denser outer cortex; centuries underground often leave the outer surface polished smooth by soil movement, while the inner core appears chalky.  
  Mineralization can give antler a warm, bone-like weight and may produce subtle bands of brown, gray, or orange where groundwater carried different minerals.  
  When rubbed between fingers, old antler often leaves a fine light-colored powder, and under strong light, the faint “grain” of vascular channels becomes visible.

- **Smell and feel of freshly closed earth**  
  Turned mound soil typically smells strongly of **humus**, wet leaves, and iron; if the soil is clay-rich, there is a metallic, almost blood-like tang when damp.  
  As backfill is tamped down, small stones and roots grind softly, and the surface under a hand feels slightly springy at first, then firmer as layers settle.  
  If the day is cool and the soil recently disturbed, a thin mist of moisture may rise from the darker patch, making the closed area look temporarily richer in color than the surrounding turf.

- **Mound surface vegetation (Eastern U.S.)**  
  Many preserved mounds in the Eastern and Midwestern U.S. are covered by **mixed grasses, plantain, clover, and opportunistic wildflowers** such as goldenrod and asters, depending on season.  
  Management crews often avoid deep-rooted trees on mound tops to prevent root damage, so nearby you may see mature oaks and maples, but the mound itself is kept mostly in low grass.  
  In high summer, insects—crickets, grasshoppers, cicadas—create a constant sound bed around mounds, while in cooler months the main ambient noises are wind in grass and distant traffic or birds.

- **Site protection: fencing and signage**  
  Contemporary mound sites frequently feature **low split-rail or metal post-and-cable fencing** around the main earthwork to keep visitors off the structure itself.  
  Interpretive signs often reference the site’s archaeological designation (e.g., **“23BO13” or “40HS1”**), list the culture periods represented (e.g., **Late Woodland, Mississippian**), and carry a note that human remains have been reburied in consultation with tribal nations.  
  Many sites also include signage requesting visitors **not to climb, sit, or picnic on the mound**, and some include a land acknowledgment naming descendant communities.

- **Terminology: chambers and contexts**  
  The deepest interior space would likely be referred to as a **“subsurface burial vault,” “central chamber,”** or **“primary burial feature”** in archaeological documentation.  
  Each artifact’s original spot in that chamber would be recorded as a **“feature context,” “locus,” or “unit,”** often labeled with codes (e.g., “Feature 12, Unit N5E3, Level 7”).  
  Tribal representatives may deliberately avoid certain clinical terms like “remains” or “specimens,” preferring **“ancestors,” “relatives,”** or specific clan or personal names.

- **Ceremonial speech and silence**  
  In many Indigenous traditions, names of ancestors or sacred figures are spoken **directly to the land**, understood as a living being capable of hearing and responding.  
  Structured sequences—saying names in a particular order, from oldest to youngest or from most distant to closest—reflect the importance of lineage and proper relational hierarchy.  
  Extended silence after such an act is common; it is a form of respect and attentiveness, allowing the words to “settle” into the place and for participants to listen for any felt response.

- **Tribal cultural officer / monitor role**  
  A contemporary tribal representative like Tayak might hold a title such as **“Tribal Historic Preservation Officer (THPO),” “Cultural Resources Officer,”** or **“NAGPRA representative.”**  
  THPOs often carry binders or tablets with copies of site maps, repatriation inventories, and consultation notes, using them alongside oral instructions from elders.  
  On-site, they may wear a mix of everyday clothing and subtle cultural markers—beaded lanyards, medallions, shell or copper jewelry—visible against generic field gear like work boots and fleece jackets.

- **Archaeologist–tribal collaboration on site**  
  Present-day practice increasingly centers tribal decision-making; archaeologists might stand back or follow instructions during reburial, acting more as technical support than leaders.  
  Tools such as **trowels, shovels, line levels, and measuring tapes** may lie nearby, but during the final moment of closure, work often stops and all metal tools are set aside.  
  Photography is frequently restricted or prohibited during certain ritual moments, so cameras and phones may be intentionally turned off or left in vehicles.
