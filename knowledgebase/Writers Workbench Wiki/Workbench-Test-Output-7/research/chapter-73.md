# Chapter 73 — write-time research

- **Contemporary excavation safety protocol (2024)**  
  Modern excavations of human burials in 2024 typically halt intrusive work the moment an intact skeleton or burial container appears, pending updated permits, osteologist scheduling, and sometimes ethics board review.[7]  
  Archaeologists photograph, laser-scan, and create context sheets before any object is moved, and they often consult with descendant communities or local authorities if the burial might be culturally sensitive.[4]  

- **Stratigraphic terminology**  
  The **deepest chamber** would be recorded as a discrete **feature** (e.g., “Feature 124: burial chamber”) within a stratigraphic unit (context) at the bottom of **Stratum Seven**, with each soil change logged in a context register.[1][7]  
  Notes would reference the **Harris matrix**, a standard diagram for visualizing stratigraphic relationships, especially important at the lowest, most complex levels of a mound.  

- **Burial chamber architecture (earthen/stone mound)**  
  Deep mound burials often occupy a **cut** dug into sterile subsoil, roofed with timber or stone slabs, then sealed with compacted earth or clay that can produce a noticeably harder, more resistant “cap” during excavation.[8]  
  Interior walls may show a distinct **“burial stain”**—a darker, more organic-rich soil lens against the lighter natural subsoil—visually separating the chamber fill from surrounding deposits.[7]  

- **Copper grave goods: corrosion and appearance**  
  Copper objects in old burials typically show heavy **green and blue-green corrosion** (malachite and other copper carbonates), sometimes forming thick, crusty nodules that obscure the original shape.[5]  
  Areas in close contact with bone can show a distinct **green staining on the skeleton**, a known effect of copper corrosion products migrating into bone tissue.  

- **Shell artifacts in burials**  
  Shell grave goods may include **beads, pendants, or small discs** drilled for suspension, often made from marine species not locally available, indicating long-distance exchange networks.[8]  
  Shell surfaces in a sealed, dry chamber can retain a smooth, slightly pearly luster, but edges tend to be chipped, and growth lines become more visible under sediment residues.  

- **Red ochre in mortuary contexts**  
  **Red ochre** (iron oxide pigment) is widely documented in prehistoric and early historic burials, often sprinkled over the body, skull, or grave floor, leaving a vivid red-orange layer that stands out sharply against surrounding soil.[8]  
  When a grave is opened, ochre appears as **fine, powdery, intensely colored patches** that cling to bone, textile fragments, and grave goods; it can smear easily if touched before documentation.  

- **Wood preservation at deep levels**  
  Carved wood can survive if the burial chamber remained **waterlogged, anoxic, or very dry and sealed**, with preservation enhanced by contact with copper, which has mild biocidal properties.[8]  
  Preserved wood often appears **dark brown to black, lightweight, and brittle**, with the grain still visible but surfaces sometimes “furred” or flaking if not immediately stabilized.  

- **Carved wooden figure: tool marks and finish**  
  Hand-carved wooden figures usually show **fine chisel or knife facets**, especially in recessed areas like around eyes, mouth, or carved clothing folds.  
  If originally coated, residues of **natural resin, oil, or pigment** may survive in crevices, creating darker, slightly glossy streaks that stand out against matte, weathered wood.  

- **Incised inscription on wooden base**  
  Incised notations on wood are made with a **fine blade, burin, or awl**, resulting in V-shaped grooves whose edges may soften over time but remain visible if sheltered from abrasion.  
  In low raking light, such incisions become dramatically clearer, a standard reason archaeologists adjust light angles or use side-lighting and macro photography when documenting carved surfaces.  

- **Notation system / non-alphabetic marks**  
  Non-alphabetic inscription systems may consist of **short, repeated motifs—lines, chevrons, dots, cross-hatching, or abstract “signs”** arranged in a fixed sequence, more like a tally or mnemonic code than phonetic writing.[1]  
  In archaeological practice, these are often described neutrally as **“sign sequences” or “non-lexical incisions”** until clear linguistic values are established.  

- **Photography procedure in a burial chamber**  
  Before touching an object, archaeologists take **overall context shots**, then **medium-range photos**, and finally **close-ups with scales and north arrows**, always recording image numbers in field notes.[7]  
  A 2024 field kit typically includes a **DSLR or mirrorless camera**, LED panel or ring lights, and a **color calibration card** so later digital processing can correct for the chamber’s tinted light.  

- **Digital recording and 3D modeling (2024)**  
  Many projects now employ **structure-from-motion (SfM) photogrammetry**, taking dozens of overlapping photos to produce a 3D model of the burial and each artifact’s exact location.  
  The deepest chamber would likely be scanned with a **tablet or smartphone LiDAR app** (where available) in addition to traditional photography, especially at a key discovery moment.  

- **Personal protective equipment in the pit**  
  Field archaeologists working in deep excavations use **hard hats, nitrile gloves, dust masks or respirators in dusty conditions, and sometimes safety glasses** when working under overhangs or loose sediment.[4]  
  Gloves are often changed before handling delicate organic artifacts to prevent cross-contamination for future **residue or DNA analysis**.  

- **Excavation tools at the deepest layer**  
  At the base of a mound, excavators generally use **small trowels (e.g., Marshalltown type), dental picks, bamboo skewers, and soft brushes** to avoid damaging bone or organic remains.[7]  
  Loose sediment is sometimes collected in **plastic trays or labeled buckets**, then screened through fine mesh to recover tiny grave goods such as beads or micro-flakes.  

- **Lighting and sensory atmosphere underground**  
  A deep chamber is typically dim, lit by **headlamps and portable LED work lights**, producing sharp contrasts and shadows, and causing metallic surfaces (like copper) to glint visibly when first exposed.  
  Old, sealed soil often has a distinct **earthy, slightly mineral smell**, and if organic material is present (wood, bone, textiles) there can be a **musty, slightly sour note** when the chamber is opened.  

- **Soil profile at the burial floor**  
  The burial floor may show a **thin, compacted layer** of slightly different texture or color, representing the original walking surface or laid platform under the body.[7]  
  Archaeologists record Munsell color values (e.g., “10YR 3/2 very dark grayish brown”) for the chamber floor and backfill, providing precise, standardized descriptors for soil appearance.  

- **Standard field documentation (paper and digital)**  
  Even in 2024, many teams still use **waterproof notebooks and pre-printed context forms** in pencil, alongside tablets or laptops for digital entry.[7]  
  Each newly recognized feature (like the carved figure) receives a **unique find number** linked to its context number, which will follow it into storage, conservation, and publication.  

- **Communications from a remote dig (cell and data)**  
  Many excavation sites rely on **cell boosters, satellite phones, or local 4G hotspots** to communicate with off-site experts, especially when a major find requires immediate consultation.[3]  
  High-resolution photos are often sent via **encrypted messaging apps or cloud storage links**, since email attachment limits can be restrictive for RAW or large JPEG files.  

- **Handling an intact wooden artifact on discovery**  
  On first lifting, a preserved wooden object is usually supported on **both hands or on a rigid tray**, with padding, because wood that looks solid can crumble if flexed.  
  If the wood is damp, conservators often wrap it in **plastic film or sealed polyethylene bags** to prevent rapid drying and cracking before it reaches a conservation lab.  

- **Immediate conservation notes and labels**  
  Artifacts are usually placed in **polyethylene bags or acid-free boxes** with pencil-written Tyvek or archival paper labels, not just marker on plastic, to ensure long-term legibility.[7]  
  Any visible inscription is sketched quickly in the field notebook, with notations on **orientation, reading direction (if known), and any damaged or ambiguous signs**.  

- **Archaeological terminology for context description**  
  The burial would be described in notes as an **“articulated inhumation”** if bones remain in anatomical order, or as a **“disturbed / disarticulated burial”** if not.  
  The chamber itself may be defined as a **“cut feature”**, with the refilled material described as **“grave fill”** distinct from the natural subsoil or mound matrix.

  In many jurisdictions, discovery of human remains triggers **mandatory reporting to regional heritage authorities or a coroner**, who confirm that the burial is archaeological rather than recent forensic.[4]
