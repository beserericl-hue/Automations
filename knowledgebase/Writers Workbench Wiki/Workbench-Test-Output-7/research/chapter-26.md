# Chapter 26 — write-time research

- **NAGPRA basic scope (1990 law)**  
  NAGPRA is a federal law enacted in 1990 that governs the treatment and return of **Native American human remains, funerary objects, sacred objects, and objects of cultural patrimony** held by federally funded institutions or found on federal/tribal land.[5]  
  It applies specifically to “**Indian Tribes**” and “**Native Hawaiian organizations**” as those terms are defined in federal law, not to any Indigenous community generically.[5]

- **Requirement of federal recognition to be an “Indian Tribe” under NAGPRA**  
  NAGPRA’s protections and procedures (consultation, repatriation, notices, claims) are triggered for entities that qualify legally as “**Indian Tribes**,” meaning tribes that are recognized as eligible for services provided by the U.S. government.[5]  
  Indigenous communities without federal recognition—such as many state-recognized or unrecognized tribes—cannot act as NAGPRA claimants unless they participate through a federally recognized tribe or as individually identified lineal descendants.[5]

- **Piscataway political status in Maryland**  
  The **Piscataway Indian Nation** and the **Piscataway Conoy Tribe** are **state‑recognized tribes in Maryland**, but are not federally recognized by the U.S. government as of the mid‑2020s (this is widely documented in tribal status lists outside NAGPRA sources).  
  Because they lack federal recognition, they are not “Indian Tribes” for NAGPRA purposes and thus cannot independently invoke NAGPRA’s tribal procedures; they would have to rely on lineal descent claims or collaboration with a federally recognized tribe.[5]

- **NAGPRA and permits / notices for excavations on federal or tribal land**  
  When Native American human remains or funerary objects are discovered on **federal or tribal lands**, NAGPRA requires immediate notification of the appropriate **lineal descendants and Indian Tribes** and a halt to activities that may harm the remains.[5]  
  Federal agencies must build **plans of action** and consult with tribes before issuing or continuing permits for excavations that may affect Native American burials; failure to respond or identify an eligible tribe within statutory timelines can lead to remains being treated as “unclaimed” or transferred under regulatory default rules.[5][3]

- **Time‑sensitive “notice” requirements under NAGPRA regulations**  
  After a museum or federal agency completes an **inventory** of human remains and associated funerary objects, it must submit a **notice of inventory completion** to the National Park Service within **6 months**.[1]  
  The NPS must accept a notice for publication in the *Federal Register* within **21 days** of receipt under updated regulations.[1]  
  For certain dispositions of unclaimed remains or intended repatriations, transfer or reinterment must occur **no earlier than 30 days and no later than 90 days** after publication of the notice if there are no objections.[3]

- **2024 NAGPRA regulatory changes tightening deadlines**  
  Major revisions to the NAGPRA regulations took effect **January 12, 2024**, aiming to speed repatriation and strengthen tribal authority.[2][3][7][8]  
  Institutions must **update inventories within five years** and provide **annual reports**, and they must prove they have the right to retain items rather than forcing tribes to prove claims.[2]  
  The new rules require repatriation of human remains and associated funerary objects by **2029**, imposing a system of deadlines that can feel like a bureaucratic countdown.[2][4]

- **“Free, prior, and informed consent” and display bans (2024 rules)**  
  As of 2024, museums must obtain **“free, prior, and informed consent”** from tribes or Native Hawaiian organizations before any **exhibition, access, or research** involving Native American human remains or cultural items covered by NAGPRA.[4]  
  Many museums have reacted by **covering or temporarily removing exhibits** that contain ancestors or sensitive items until consultation and consent are obtained.[3][4]

- **“Culturally unidentifiable” loophole and its closure**  
  For years, many remains were labeled **“culturally unidentifiable,”** which stalled or prevented repatriations; the 2024 regulatory changes were explicitly designed to eliminate this loophole and defer more to Indigenous knowledge and oral history.[2][3]  
  Under the new framework, **a single line of evidence** (such as oral tradition alone) can be sufficient to establish cultural affiliation for repatriation.[2]

- **Lineal descendants vs. tribal claims**  
  NAGPRA allows **lineal descendants** to submit repatriation requests if they can demonstrate direct ancestry, but this is often difficult to prove in legal terms for ancient burials.[5][3]  
  If no lineal descendants or eligible tribes can be identified, institutions may ultimately classify remains as unclaimed and pursue transfer or reinterment according to regulatory procedures rather than direct repatriation to a specific community.[3][5]

- **NAGPRA administrative language in real documents**  
  Official communications and notices frequently use phrases like **“human remains representing X individuals,” “associated funerary objects numbering Y,” “cultural items,” “cultural affiliation reasonably traceable,” “lineal descendants, Indian Tribes, and Native Hawaiian organizations,”** and **“notice of inventory completion”**.[1][5]  
  The Federal Register notices are written in highly formal regulatory prose, with structured sections for **“History and Description,” “Determinations,”** and **“Requests for Repatriation”**.[3][6]

- **National Park Service (NPS) as NAGPRA administrator**  
  The **National NAGPRA Program** is administered by the National Park Service within the Department of the Interior.[7][8]  
  The NPS maintains the **regulations page** and issues guidance, webinars, and templates for notices, and is the gatekeeper for Federal Register publication of NAGPRA notices.[1][7]

- **Bureau of Indian Affairs (BIA) role and contact details**  
  The Bureau of Indian Affairs participates in NAGPRA implementation and reports that by 2024 it has repatriated **over 2,612 ancestors and 35,826 cultural items** under the law.[5]  
  One real administrative address used in NAGPRA‑related cultural resources work is the **Branch of Cultural Resources Management, 1001 Indian School Road NW MB44, Albuquerque, NM 87104, open 8:30 a.m.–4:30 p.m., Monday–Friday**.[5]

- **Legal “trap” for non‑federally recognized tribes**  
  Because NAGPRA is triggered by status as an **“Indian Tribe”** or **lineal descendant**, communities like the Piscataway with state but not federal recognition can see sacred remains technically covered by NAGPRA, yet have **no straightforward standing** to demand repatriation as a tribe.[5]  
  A federally funded university or developer’s compliance might involve consultation with **other** federally recognized tribes in the region while the locally affiliated, non‑recognized community sits outside the formal process, producing a sense of legal betrayal grounded in the statute’s definitions.[5]

- **Maryland / Chesapeake burial mound context (general)**  
  In the Chesapeake, Indigenous burials historically include **primary inhumations and ossuaries**, often on river terraces or bluffs overlooking waterways; many were disturbed by agriculture and development in the 19th–20th centuries (documented in regional archaeology literature).  
  Construction projects that hit such sites today in Maryland typically trigger a complex overlay of **state historic preservation law**, Section 106 of the **National Historic Preservation Act**, and NAGPRA where federal funds or permits are involved, creating multiple, sometimes conflicting, bureaucratic timelines.

- **Federal Register as public notice mechanism**  
  NAGPRA notices are published in the **Federal Register**, the daily journal of the U.S. government, which is accessible online as a text‑heavy interface organized by volume and issue.[3][6]  
  Each notice has a citation format like “**88 FR [page number] (year)**” and lists contact information for the responsible museum or federal agency NAGPRA officer.[6]

- **Post‑2024 university and museum atmosphere**  
  With the 2024 rule changes, universities and museums that receive federal funds are under pressure to **complete updated inventories within five years** and submit **annual compliance statements**; compliance offices are dealing with spreadsheets of accession numbers and deadlines.[2][3][4]  
  Institutions now carry the **burden of proof** to justify retention of remains and cultural items, shifting staff conversations from “do tribes have enough evidence?” to “do we have any legally defensible reason to hold this?”[2]
