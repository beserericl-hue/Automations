# Chapter 62 — write-time research

- **Tribal affidavit practice (U.S., 2020s)**  
  In many U.S. jurisdictions, a **sworn affidavit** can be taken by a licensed attorney or notary and later filed as an exhibit or sworn declaration in federal or state court; in tribal court, written affidavits may be accepted as testimony under tribal rules of evidence.  
  Affidavits are typically formatted with a **caption** (court name, case number, parties), the affiant’s full legal name, numbered paragraphs, and a **jurat** at the end (“Subscribed and sworn before me…”), with signature lines for the affiant and the person administering the oath.

- **Recording oral history as legal evidence**  
  It is common to **audio‑ or video‑record** oral histories, then have a court reporter or paralegal **transcribe** them verbatim for use in litigation, especially in land, treaty, or sacred‑site cases.  
  Attorneys and historians sometimes use the term **“orality as evidence”** or refer to “**traditional knowledge**” or “**traditional cultural knowledge**” when arguing that Indigenous oral history should be afforded evidentiary weight comparable to written documents.

- **Tribal attorney role and environment**  
  A **tribal attorney** may be an enrolled tribal member or non‑Native lawyer, usually licensed in at least one state bar and admitted to practice before the specific tribal court; their office is often within a **tribal government complex** that can include council chambers, enrollment offices, and cultural preservation or historic preservation departments.  
  Legal meetings often happen in a **small conference room** with a rectangular laminate table, stackable office chairs, a desktop computer or laptop, a **portable digital audio recorder** or smartphone on the table, and a printed intake or “witness information” form on a clipboard.

- **Standard legal technology in 2024**  
  By 2024, lawyers frequently record statements on **smartphones** using built‑in voice‑memo apps or dedicated digital recorders (Zoom, Tascam, Olympus), often placed in the middle of the table and checked for battery and red “record” indicator light.  
  Case files and transcripts are commonly stored and shared via **cloud‑based case‑management systems** or secure platforms (e.g., encrypted email, SharePoint, Clio‑type software), with the attorney sometimes typing **contemporaneous notes** on a laptop as the speaker talks.

- **Evidentiary terminology likely to be spoken**  
  The attorney may verbally mark the record with phrases like **“for the record,” “let the record reflect,” “this will be Exhibit 12,”** or **“off the record”** when pausing the interview.  
  They may ask clarifying questions using legal terms such as **“chain of title,” “custom and practice,” “knowledge based on personal observation,” “hearsay,” “ancestral remains,” “cultural patrimony,”** or **“traditional cultural property.”**

- **Federal Indian law and sacred‑site context**  
  For protection of burial mounds or sacred sites, attorneys frequently reference **NAGPRA** (Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act of 1990) and **NHPA Section 106** (National Historic Preservation Act review of undertakings affecting historic properties), which both explicitly consider tribal oral traditions and traditional knowledge in consultation processes.  
  The phrase **“government‑to‑government consultation”** is standard when describing formal interactions between tribes and federal or state agencies over sites like burial mounds.

- **Tribal Historic Preservation Officer (THPO) system**  
  Many tribes have a **Tribal Historic Preservation Officer (THPO)** recognized by the National Park Service, responsible for identifying and protecting historic properties on tribal lands, including **mounds, cemeteries, and ceremonial sites**.  
  THPO offices often use a mix of **GIS mapping software** and community oral histories to document sacred sites, leading to maps that look very different from state archaeological site files.

- **Contemporary Indigenous material culture (quilts)**  
  Many Native nations use **quilts** as significant cultural items—Star quilts on the Plains, patchwork and appliqué in the Southeast and Northeast—often given at graduations, naming ceremonies, and funerals, or displayed at wakes.[1][5]  
  Quilts can incorporate **tribal motifs, clan symbols, river or trail lines, and color‑coded patterns** (e.g., particular colors associated with directions, seasons, or ceremonial roles), making them plausible carriers of embedded “maps” or genealogies.

- **Quilt as map and mnemonic device**  
  In some Indigenous traditions, **visual patterning** on textiles, beadwork, or quillwork functions as a **mnemonic**—rows, color changes, and motifs can cue stories about migration routes, treaty boundaries, or kinship relations.[1][6]  
  A “hidden map” could realistically be encoded as a **sequence of colored squares representing specific lakes or bends in a river, a zigzag denoting a trail over ridges, or repeated motifs for particular family lines**, knowable only to those taught the key.

- **Naming generations in oral history**  
  Detailed oral testimonies about land commonly **recite ancestors by name** generation by generation (e.g., “my great‑grandmother, her mother before her”), anchoring stories to specific people and time periods.  
  Elders often use **kinship terms** (“my mother’s mother’s older sister”) both as identifiers and to convey how knowledge is legitimately passed, sometimes more important than precise Gregorian dates.

- **Contemporary Indigenous language use**  
  Speakers may **shift between English and their tribal language**, especially for kinship terms, place‑names, ceremonial concepts, or words that don’t translate cleanly (for example, a term for the mound that means something like “place where they are listening” rather than “burial”).  
  It is common for younger professionals like Tayak to understand some ceremonial or kinship vocabulary but **not the deeper layers of meaning** attached to old place‑names and phrases, leading to moments of sudden re‑interpretation when elders unpack them.

- **Tribal government building sensory details**  
  Modern tribal offices typically have **fluorescent lighting**, off‑white or beige painted cinderblock or drywall walls, **framed photos of past tribal chairs or chiefs**, and posters or certificates for federal grants and cultural programs.  
  The air may carry a blend of **office smells** (coffee, copier toner, carpet cleaner) and occasional outdoor scents—dust, pine, or sage—if windows are opened or people come in from ceremony.

- **Weather and light (contemporary continental U.S., late in the day)**  
  In much of the U.S. in 2024, office windows late in the day will show **LED parking‑lot lights** flicking on, the **blue‑white glow** of cars with daytime running lights, and perhaps the blinking red lights of a distant cell tower.  
  If it is winter or late fall, darkness falls early; the room might feel **closed‑in and humming**, with the faint buzz of fluorescent ballasts and the whoosh of a central HVAC system cycling on and off.

- **Contemporary office clothing and accessories**  
  A tribal attorney in 2024 is likely to wear **business‑casual** clothing—button‑down shirt or blouse, slacks or a knee‑length skirt, perhaps a blazer—sometimes combined with **Native jewelry** such as turquoise earrings, a beaded lanyard, or a bolo tie.  
  Elders attending such meetings often wear **comfortable layered clothes**—zip‑up fleece jackets or cardigans over T‑shirts or blouses, walking shoes, and may carry a **reusable water bottle, pill organizer, or small cloth bag** for personal items.

- **Recording protocol and physical actions**  
  Before starting, the attorney will usually **state on the recording** the date, location, case name, and identities of those present, then ask Nora to confirm her full name and that she understands she is under oath.  
  When the interview runs long (two hours), the recorder may need to **swap memory cards, adjust the microphone placement, or plug into a wall outlet**, creating small pauses while everyone sits in silence or stretches.

- **Physicality of a two‑hour oral testimony**  
  After more than an hour, the **elder’s voice may grow hoarse**, prompting sips of water or coffee, and the attorney might offer a short break, especially if emotion surfaces when describing ancestors or burial places.  
  People commonly develop **stiff backs and tingling legs** from sitting in office chairs that are slightly too high or too low; small movements—rubbing knees, rolling shoulders, resting elbows on the table—become more frequent as time passes.

- **Emotional dynamics and body language**  
  In cross‑cultural or cross‑generational Indigenous settings, listeners often show respect by **remaining quiet, looking down or at their hands**, and avoiding direct eye contact when stories turn painful or sacred, relying on **nods and murmured acknowledgments** instead.  
  A younger professional like Tayak might unconsciously click a pen, tighten her jaw, or **press lips together** when she hears things that challenge long‑held assumptions about agency actions, family decisions, or her own work.

- **Maps and land records around burial mounds**  
  County and state offices may have **plat maps and parcel records** that show the mound property only as lot lines and numbers, whereas tribal cultural offices or elders’ mental maps include **burial locations, ceremony routes, and story sites*
