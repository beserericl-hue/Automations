# Chapter 28 — write-time research

- **1680s Maryland land grants & Calverts**  
  Provincial land grants in the 1680s were issued in the name of **Charles Calvert, 3rd Lord Baltimore**, then proprietor of Maryland, and recorded in bound parchment volumes of the **Provincial Land Office** in St. Mary’s City and later Annapolis.  
  Grants were typically written in formulaic legal English on vellum, referring to the proprietor as “**Charles Absolute Lord and Proprietary of the Provinces of Maryland and Avalon**,” and describing acreage by **metes and bounds** using trees, streams, and existing tracts as reference points.  
  The Calvert name appears both as the proprietary authority granting land and as private grantees from various Calvert family branches, often tied to **patents** measured in “acres” and sometimes in “perches.”

- **Piscataway dispossession in the late 17th century**  
  By the 1670s–1680s, **Piscataway (Conoy) people** on the north shore of the Potomac and lower Patuxent were being pressured off their agricultural lands by tobacco planters expanding inland from the tidewater.  
  Colonial authorities in Maryland used **treaties, “Indian towns,” and “reservation” tracts on paper** that were later fragmented or absorbed into private patents, effectively turning Piscataway homelands into grants for elite colonial families.  
  Displacement often followed epidemics, warfare (including fallout from **Bacon’s Rebellion** in neighboring Virginia), and the construction of **stockaded English settlements** that blocked access to traditional fields and fishing sites.

- **Geography: Piscataway homelands vs. modern DC–Maryland region**  
  Piscataway territory historically encompassed the **western shore of the Chesapeake Bay in southern Maryland**, including the lower **Potomac River** and adjacent tributaries, overlapping with what is now **Prince George’s, Charles, and St. Mary’s Counties**.  
  Many colonial patents in these areas bordered named waterways still on modern maps, such as **Piscataway Creek, the Patuxent River, the Port Tobacco River, and Mattawoman Creek**, anchoring old grant descriptions to current geography.  
  The ancestral village and mound areas once sat on elevated terraces above tidal rivers and creeks, with **river bluffs and broad floodplains** that were prime agricultural and settlement zones both for the Piscataway and later planters.

- **Burial mounds and Piscataway/Chesapeake archaeology**  
  Indigenous **burial mounds and ossuaries** in the Chesapeake region were often located on slightly raised knolls or terraces overlooking waterways, sometimes later cut by plows or roads.  
  Archaeological excavations in these areas commonly recover **shell-tempered pottery, quartz and rhyolite projectile points, pipe fragments, and trade items like glass beads or copper** in later-contact layers.  
  Soil in mound contexts may show **contrasting bands of darker, organic-rich fill**, occasional charcoal flecks, and areas of bone staining even where bones themselves have deteriorated due to acidic Mid-Atlantic soils.

- **1680s plantation landscape on former Piscataway land**  
  An English plantation established on newly patented land in the 1680s would have included a **timber-framed dwelling**, outbuildings, and cleared tobacco fields, often sited near a creek landing for boat access.  
  Early houses were commonly **earthfast (post-in-ground)** structures with clapboard or plank walls, brick or clay chimneys, and packed earth or simple plank floors.  
  Surrounding the house, archaeologists often find artifact scatters of **handmade brick, tobacco pipe stems, coarse earthenware, and bottle glass**, marking the overlay of colonial domestic life on older Indigenous ground.

- **Archival setting: university special collections in 2024**  
  A modern university archive reading room typically requires researchers to **check bags and coats in lockers**, sign in with ID, and handle manuscripts with clean, dry hands or **cotton/nitrile gloves** depending on material type.  
  Bound land-record volumes are stored in **climate-controlled stacks** and brought out on **wheeled book trucks**, then used on large wooden tables under **indirect, filtered light** to protect the documents.  
  The room is usually quiet except for **HVAC hum, the soft roll of carts, laptop keys, and the occasional muted printer or scanner**, with staff at a reference desk monitoring use.

- **Physicality of an original 17th‑century land grant**  
  A surviving 1680s patent or grant page may be **handwritten in iron-gall ink** in a tight secretary or italic hand, with long “s” characters and heavy abbreviations, making it initially difficult to read.  
  The parchment or high-rag-paper surface often shows **cockling, edge tears, and brown ink halos**, with a faint musty or slightly metallic smell from the ink and storage.  
  Many such documents retain traces of **red wax seals** or written references to attached great seals, even when the seal itself is missing, and may have marginal annotations made by later clerks.

- **Digital vs. analog archival tools**  
  Many land records have been **digitized into searchable databases**, allowing staff to provide Wren with a call number or **microfilm reel** that corresponds to the volume and folio where the Calvert patent appears.  
  Researchers often use **foam cradles, weight snakes (soft, bead-filled tubes), and book supports** to hold large volumes open without damaging spines.  
  Standard tools at the table include **pencils (no pens allowed), acid-free flags, and a laptop or notepad**, with rules prohibiting food and drink.

- **Terminology inside the land record**  
  The 1680s document would likely call the tract a **“tract,” “parcel,” “manor,” or “dividend” of land**, specify the number of **acres**, and outline the boundaries by reference to **“marked oaks,” “bounded trees,” “branches” of creeks, and neighboring patents**.  
  It might note the **“caveat”**, the date of **“survey”**, and the “**patent**” date, and reference a “**warrant**” that had been issued earlier authorizing the survey.  
  The grant could also impose a **quitrent or annual rent**, recorded in **pounds of tobacco** or in money, due yearly to the Lord Proprietor.

- **Indigenous naming vs. colonial renaming**  
  The patent may overlay an area known to the Piscataway by an Indigenous place-name, but the document itself likely **renames the tract** after an English person, estate, or religious phrase (for example, “St. ___,” or an English village name).  
  However, some colonial records preserve **anglicized versions of Indigenous toponyms**, so a creek, branch, or nearby tract might carry a distorted Piscataway name alongside the Calvert patent.  
  Over time, these colonial tract names can survive as **modern road names, neighborhood names, or minor topographic labels** on county maps.

- **Modern estate on old plantation ground**  
  A great-great-grandmother’s “estate” in Maryland is often a **20th‑century house sitting on a much older property boundary**, where the metes and bounds still secretly follow 17th‑century lines.  
  The driveway, tree lines, and fence lines can parallel **old hedgerows or property ditches** first created to manage livestock and tobacco fields under the original patent.  
  The lawn may cover archaeological remains of **earlier house foundations, slave quarters, outbuildings, and Indigenous features**, all invisible at the surface but legible in soil and artifact distributions.

- **Smell and feel of archival and archaeological spaces**  
  A special collections room often smells of **archival boxes, aging paper, binder’s glue, and recently cleaned carpet**, distinct from the earthy, mineral smell of an excavation site.  
  The vault-like coolness of **air conditioning and low humidity** contrasts with the often **humid, loamy air of Maryland summer fieldwork** at a mound site.  
  Handling old volumes leaves a faint residue of **dust and paper fiber** on fingertips, while fieldwork leaves **grit from silty-clay soil, fine sand, and occasional charcoal** under nails and on skin.

- **Contemporary university technology and security**  
  Many archives now require booking a **time slot in advance** via an online system, and some materials are visible only on **reading-room terminals** showing high-resolution scans rather than originals.  
  Surveillance cameras positioned in corners or in globe housings on the ceiling monitor the reading room, and some materials require **supervised use at a designated table**.  
  Cell phone use is often restricted to **silent mode**, with photos of documents allowed only under certain conditions and sometimes with a **copy stand or non-flash photography** rules.

- **Genealogical overlap in Maryland families**

  Family Bible records, early **county probate files**, and 19th‑century **county atlases** can link a modern surname to a specific tract name that first appears in 17th‑century patents.
