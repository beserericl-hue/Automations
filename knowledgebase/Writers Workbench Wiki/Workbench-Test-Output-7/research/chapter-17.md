# Chapter 17 — write-time research

- **Smartphone (late 2020s norm)**  
  By 2024, most people in North America use a **glass-slab touchscreen smartphone** with a multi‑lens rear camera bump, USB‑C or Lightning port, and no physical keypad.  
  Incoming calls typically light the screen with a **contact photo or simple name listing**, and the phone vibrates with haptic feedback even when set to silent.  

- **Calling and not calling (telecom detail)**  
  Modern phones show **call duration, “missed call” notifications, and last-called contact** immediately after hanging up, so ending a call before it connects leaves no duration but can still log an “outgoing” attempt.  
  Many people keep their phones on **vibrate or “Do Not Disturb”** in the evening, so Nora realistically cannot be sure Tayak would answer even if she presses “call.”  

- **Contemporary lighting and atmosphere (indoors, evening)**  
  By 2024, most U.S. households have replaced older incandescent bulbs with **LED bulbs** that give either warm (2700K) yellowish light or cooler (4000K+) bluish-white light.  
  LED bulbs come on instantly and do not hum the way older fluorescents did, but they can cast a slightly **flat, shadowless light** that affects how colors in a quilt appear at night.  

- **Reading and needlework glasses**  
  People in their 50s–70s commonly use **drugstore “cheater” reading glasses** with +1.25 to +3.00 diopters for close work like hand‑stitching.  
  Quilters often keep a **chain or cord** on their glasses or perch them on the end of the nose to switch focus quickly between the quilt and the room.  

- **Quilt scale and weight (queen/king bed)**  
  A typical **queen‑size quilt** is about 90" × 108" (roughly 230 × 275 cm), large enough that it must be folded or draped over a substantial surface when one person works alone.  
  With cotton top, cotton batting, and backing, a heavily pieced, hand‑quilted queen or king quilt can weigh **10–15 pounds**, giving it a noticeable, warm weight across the lap.  

- **Hand quilting tools (material culture)**  
  Traditional hand quilters use **betweens** or **quilting needles** (short, fine needles, sizes 9–12) and **quilting thread**, often slightly thicker or glazed cotton to resist tangling.  
  A **thimble**—metal, leather, or rubberized—is typically worn on the middle finger of the dominant hand, and some quilters also use a **leather “under‑thimble” or adhesive pad** on the finger beneath the quilt.  

- **Marking a hidden pattern (technical detail)**  
  Quilters commonly mark complex patterns with **water‑soluble fabric pens, chalk wheels, or Hera markers** that crease but do not stain the cloth.  
  A secret map could realistically be drawn first on **pattern paper or freezer paper**, then traced or transferred onto fabric using a light box or window as a backlight.  

- **Quilt construction vocabulary**  
  The front of a quilt is the **“top”**, composed of **blocks** (repeated units) separated by **sashing** (strips between blocks), with an outer **border** framing everything.  
  The three layers—top, **batting**, and **backing**—are held together by quilting stitches; binding is the **narrow strip around the outer edge** that finishes the quilt.  

- **Period‑accurate fabrics and colors (1990s–2020s)**  
  Quilt‑shop cotton in the U.S. since the 1990s is typically **100% cotton, 40–44 inches wide**, often from lines by designers like Moda, Robert Kaufman, or Andover.  
  Over thirty years, a quilt started in the 1990s might show **small calico florals and dusty “country” colors** (mauves, forest greens, navy) alongside later **modern solids, grays, and clear brights** from the 2010s–2020s.  

- **Thread over decades**  
  In the early 1990s, many home quilters still used **cotton‑wrapped polyester thread** or all‑cotton thread on spools from brands like Coats & Clark or Mettler.  
  By the 2010s, high‑quality long‑staple cotton threads like **Aurifil** became popular, with quilters often keeping **half‑used, sun‑faded spools** in sewing baskets accumulated over years.  

- **Visible aging of textiles**  
  Cotton quilts from the 1990s show **fading where light hits and slight weakening at frequent fold lines**, especially if stored draped over a sofa back or folded in the same way for years.  
  White or cream fabrics can develop a **slight yellowing** from airborne dust and skin oils; darker fabrics may show **abraded spots where the nap looks softer**.  

- **Family quilting as oral tradition (U.S. context)**  
  In many rural and small‑town U.S. communities, quilting knowledge is passed through **“quilting bees,” church sewing circles, and intergenerational teaching**, often without written patterns.  
  Older women may describe learning “by **watching Grandmother’s hands**,” with instructions conveyed in **spoken rules of thumb** (e.g., “stitches no bigger than a grain of rice”).  

- **Map‑like patterns in quilting history**  
  Quilts with **maze, labyrinth, or “trip around the world”** designs lend themselves visually to maps, with concentric squares or paths leading to a center.  
  Some traditional patterns like **“Log Cabin”** can be arranged so **light and dark values** form paths or hidden shapes visible only from a distance or to someone who knows what to look for.  

- **2020s domestic soundscape**  
  At night in a typical American home, background noise often includes a **refrigerator compressor cycling, HVAC fan, distant traffic, occasional smartphone notification pings**, and perhaps a television murmuring from another room.  
  A person sewing might also hear the **soft scrape of thread pulling through fabric, the tap of a metal thimble on the needle, and the slight crinkle of the quilt’s batting** with each shift.  

- **Furniture likely around a long‑term project**  
  Hand quilters often keep a **dedicated armchair or loveseat** near a good lamp, with a **side table** for scissors, thread snips, a pin cushion, reading glasses, and a mug or water glass.  
  For a large, thirty‑year quilt, a **floor‑standing quilting frame** or collapsible **lap hoop** may be present, even if currently pushed aside.  

- **Terminology: “UFOs” and “WIPs”**  
  In quilting slang, a long‑term, unfinished quilt is a **UFO (UnFinished Object)** or **WIP (Work In Progress)**, terms widely used in quilting magazines, blogs, and guilds by the 2010s.  
  Many quilters keep **plastic bins or labeled totes** of UFOs, some dating back decades, often with notes or bagged sets of cut pieces.  

- **Digital quilting culture (background, not on the page)**  
  By 2024, quilt patterns, tutorials, and inspiration are widely shared on **Instagram, Pinterest, YouTube, and quilting blogs**, and many quilters follow specific designers or hashtags.  
  Even traditional quilters may have **PDF patterns** saved on tablets or laptops and might buy fabric from **online quilt shops**, delivered in branded cardboard mailers.  

- **Electrical and climate details (North American house)**  
  Standard wall outlets in North America are **120 volts with Type A/B sockets**, often with extension cords or power strips near a favorite chair to power a lamp and phone charger.  
  In many regions, evening indoor air is **conditioned in summer and forced‑air heated in winter**, producing a faint **dryness in the nose and throat** and soft whooshing sounds from vents.  

- **2024 calendar detail (if month/season matters)**  
  In 2024, **February 29 existed** (leap year), which affects “this many years” calculations for someone counting anniversaries or exact spans back thirty years.  
  Daylight Saving Time in most of the U.S. began on **March 10, 2024**, shifting sunset later and affecting how much natural light remains in the evening.  

- **Everyday clothing at home**  
  A woman in late middle age sewing at home in 2024 is likely to wear **stretch knit fabrics**—yoga pants, leggings, or soft jeans with a cotton T‑shirt or sweatshirt, and possibly **compression socks or house slippers**.  
  Many people use **hooded zip‑up sweatshirts** or cardigans as an easy on/off extra layer when sitting still for long periods.
