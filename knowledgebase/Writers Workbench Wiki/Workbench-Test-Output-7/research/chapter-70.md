# Chapter 70 — write-time research

- **2024 Doomsday Clock setting**  
  In January 2024, the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists kept the **Doomsday Clock at 90 seconds to midnight**, matching the unprecedented closeness first set in 2023.[1][2]  
  The 2024 announcement explicitly cited the ongoing war in Ukraine, the war in Gaza after the October 7 attacks, stalled nuclear arms talks, climate inaction, and AI risks as reasons for maintaining the setting.[1][3]

- **Symbolism and terminology of the Clock**  
  The Doomsday Clock is a **symbolic timepiece** created in 1947 to represent perceived proximity to global catastrophe, with “midnight” meaning theoretical apocalypse.[2][4]  
  It is adjusted annually by the **Science and Security Board** of the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists, whose members consider nuclear risk, climate change, biological threats, and disruptive technologies.[2][4]

- **2024 global risk atmosphere**  
  Commentary around the 2024 decision emphasizes a “continuing and unprecedented level of risk,” using phrases like “global catastrophe” and “existential threat” in official statements and coverage.[1][3]  
  Media reports frame “90 seconds to midnight” as the “closest ever” to catastrophe as of 2024, reinforcing a mood of anxious urgency and deadline pressure.[1][3][5]

- **Historical range of the Clock (for characters’ perspective)**  
  The farthest the Clock has ever been from midnight was **17 minutes** in 1991, after the Cold War thaw.[2][5]  
  By contrast, from 2020 onward it remained under two minutes, tightening from 100 seconds (2020) to 90 seconds (2023–2024), then edging even closer in subsequent years.[2][5]

- **Common public reactions and discourse**  
  Public discussion often oscillates between **dismissal as symbolic theater** and **alarm that humanity is “closer than ever” to self-destruction**, giving characters plausible lines about “they’ve been warning us for years” or “it’s just a metaphor.”[1][3][4]  
  Coverage routinely pairs the Clock with images of nuclear mushroom clouds, climate disasters, or protests, visually linking abstract seconds to concrete fears.[3]

- **War in Ukraine as a cited factor**  
  The continued Russian invasion of Ukraine and Russia’s suspension of participation in key arms-control agreements are listed as primary reasons for the 2024 setting.[1][4][5]  
  Language used includes “ongoing war,” “nuclear saber-rattling,” and “erosion of arms control,” reflecting the security-focused rationale.[1][4]

- **War in Gaza and regional instability**  
  The 2024 announcement refers to “Israel’s actions following an Oct. 7 attack” and broader Middle East proxy conflicts as contributors to global instability.[1][3]  
  This ties the Clock to real-time images of urban bombardment, hostages, and humanitarian crises circulating in global media through late 2023–2024.[1][3]

- **Climate change framing in 2024**  
  The Bulletin notes that **2023 was the hottest year on record**, emphasizing worsening climate impacts—heat waves, wildfires, floods—as part of the justification for the 90‑second setting.[1][4]  
  Official and analytical commentary frequently stresses “insufficient progress on climate change,” “continued inaction,” and “accelerating impacts” as stock phrases.[1][4][5]

- **Artificial intelligence as an emerging risk**  
  The 2024 announcement and related commentary list **AI and emerging technologies** among reasons for heightened concern, alongside nuclear and climate threats.[1][3][4]  
  Phrases like “increasing risks associated with artificial intelligence” appear in news coverage of the Clock, blending tech anxiety into the broader existential-risk narrative.[3][4]

- **Media format of the 2024 announcement**  
  The 2024 update was delivered in a **live virtual press conference** by the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists, featuring science communicators like Bill Nye and senior Bulletin officials.[1][6]  
  Viewers would have seen a staged backdrop with the Clock graphic, speakers at a podium or on video, and repeated verbal emphasis on “90 seconds to midnight.”[3][6]

- **Statistical and graphical representation**  
  Outlets such as Statista publish **time-series charts** of “minutes to midnight” from 1947 onward, where 2023–2024 stand out as the tightest cluster near zero.[5]  
  These graphs are commonly shared on social media whenever the Clock is updated, functioning as a quick visual summary of worsening risk.[5]

- **Technical phrasing of the setting**  
  In formal descriptions and data visualizations, the 2024 setting is written as **“90 seconds before midnight”** rather than in minutes.[1][5]  
  Historical entries in such datasets explicitly list “2024 – 90” alongside the corresponding reasons (Ukraine war, pandemics, climate inaction).[5]

- **Institutional identity of the Bulletin**  
  The Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists is a Chicago-based organization founded by Manhattan Project scientists, giving it a historical association with nuclear weapons and arms control debates.[1][4]  
  Its logo and publications often blend scientific imagery (atoms, equations) with stark graphic design, reinforcing an expert but advocacy-oriented identity.[4]

- **Timing within the calendar year**  
  Clock updates typically occur in **January**, so by “present—2024,” characters would be speaking about a very recent decision still in the news cycle and analysis pieces.[1][5]  
  Early-year coverage includes think pieces, interviews with board members, and explainer articles that break down what “90 seconds to midnight” is meant to convey.[1][4]

- **Numerical comparison language**  
  Journalistic summaries often mention that the current setting is “the closest it has ever been, including during the Cold War,” underscoring that 2023–2024 surpass Cuban Missile Crisis-era fear in symbolic proximity.[1][2][5]  
  This gives characters an easy comparative reference, contrasting older family memories of Cold War drills with present-day numbers.
