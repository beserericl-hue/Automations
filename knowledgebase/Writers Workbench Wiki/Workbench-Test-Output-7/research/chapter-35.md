# Chapter 35 — write-time research

- **NSF submission platform (2024)**  
  Proposals to the U.S. National Science Foundation are submitted electronically through **Research.gov** or **Grants.gov**, with Research.gov now the primary portal for most PI-driven research proposals.[3][7]  
  The NSF site’s navigation prominently features a **“Find Funding”** and **“Prepare & Submit Proposal”** pathway at the top of the homepage.[2][7]

- **How Tayak sees the theft (NSF listing)**  
  Once logged into Research.gov, a user can search **“Submitted Proposals”** and see entries by **title, institution, PI name, and status** (e.g., “Submitted,” “Pending”).[3]  
  The NSF funding search and awards databases allow searching by **keyword, PI, and institution**, showing a short project title, PI of record, and sponsoring organization, which is how a colleague could easily spot Voss’s submission.[2][3]

- **Standard NSF proposal components visible in listings**  
  A complete NSF proposal typically contains a **Cover Sheet, Project Summary, Project Description (up to ~15 pages), References Cited, Biographical Sketches, Budget, Budget Justification, Current & Pending Support, Facilities & Equipment, and a Data Management Plan**.[1][6]  
  The NSF **PAPPG (NSF 24‑1)** governs these components for proposals due on or after May 20, 2024, and is explicitly cited in the online instructions.[6]

- **Principal Investigator designation (technical detail)**  
  In NSF systems, the **Principal Investigator (PI)** is the person with primary responsibility for the project and is explicitly named on the **Cover Sheet and within the Research.gov record**; co‑PIs are listed separately.[1][3]  
  Because PI identity is central for eligibility and accountability, a listing showing **“PI: Voss”** and no mention of Tayak would be unambiguous to any academic as a claim of intellectual ownership.

- **Institutional routing and time stamps**  
  Proposals in Research.gov show **submission date/time** and institutional routing status; many universities require an **internal deadline days before the NSF deadline** for review by the Office of Sponsored Programs.[1][3]  
  The NSF overview notes that solicitations usually allow **a minimum of 90 days** between announcement and submission deadline, so the listing would display a firm due date alongside the already‑submitted timestamp.[3]

- **NSF terminology on screens and PDFs**  
  The application and PDF cover sheet routinely use terms like **“Sponsoring Organization,” “Award Instrument,” “CFDA/Assistance Listing Number,” “Estimated Start Date,”** and **“Project Title.”**[1][8]  
  Budget sections are labeled by **Year 1, Year 2, etc.**, with lines for **personnel, fringe benefits, travel, participant support, equipment, and indirect costs**, and a separate **“Budget Justification (5 pages max)”** field.[1][2]

- **NSF ethics and policy context**  
  The PAPPG emphasizes that PIs must adhere to **responsible and ethical conduct of research** and that institutions certify compliance upon submission.[6]  
  Proposals must disclose **“Current and Pending (Other) Support”** for all senior personnel, so omitting Tayak or her separate work on the Charles County site would be a concrete, policy‑relevant misrepresentation.[1][6]

- **The forwarded evidence (email + PDF printout)**  
  The colleague is likely to send a **system‑generated PDF** of the “Cover Sheet” or a screenshot from Research.gov, which prominently displays **PI name, project title, NSF program, and submitting institution**.[1][3]  
  University email systems in 2024 commonly use **Microsoft Outlook or Gmail interfaces**, so the forwarded message might carry metadata like **“Sent via Research.gov <no‑reply@research.gov>”** in the header.

- **Printing the listing (material details)**  
  On campus or at home, printing is typically done to **8.5 x 11‑inch white copy paper** from a laser printer, with faint **Research.gov or NSF logo** and small footer text showing **date/time and page number** from the browser.[1][3]  
  Multi‑page cover sheets often have **“NSF Cover Sheet”** at the top, with sections boxed off for PI information, organization, and compliance certifications, creating a bureaucratic, official look.

- **Charles County, Maryland – geographic frame**  
  Charles County lies in **Southern Maryland**, bordered by the **Potomac River** to the west and south, and is about **20–30 miles south of Washington, D.C.**, depending on the route.  
  The county is a mix of **suburban sprawl (especially around Waldorf)**, rural **farmland**, and **wooded areas** along rivers and creeks, with low, humid terrain typical of the mid‑Atlantic coastal plain.

- **Charles County archaeology context**  
  Charles County contains documented **Indigenous sites associated with the Piscataway and related peoples**, including shell middens, village sites, and burial grounds noted in Maryland archaeological surveys.  
  Cultural resource management (CRM) work in the county often occurs along **transportation corridors, waterfront developments, and utility projects**, and is routinely recorded in state site files.

- **Drive from Charles County to campus (probable routes)**  
  From central Charles County (e.g., near La Plata), a drive toward a major research campus in the D.C.–Baltimore region typically uses **US‑301 North**, connecting to **MD‑5, I‑495 (Capital Beltway), or MD‑210 (Indian Head Highway)** depending on the destination.  
  Traffic on weekday afternoons can be heavy, especially where **US‑301 intersects with suburban shopping strips in Waldorf** and merges toward the Beltway, with frequent stoplights, brake lights, and big‑box store signage.

- **Seasonal and weather specifics (mid‑Atlantic)**  
  In **spring and early fall**, Southern Maryland and the D.C. region are typically **humid but mild**, with temperatures often in the **60s–70s°F**, pollen in the air, and trees fully leafed out.  
  Roadside scenery features **oak, tulip poplar, and pine**, with **roadkill deer**, overhead powerlines, and frequent construction zones contributing to the sensory landscape.

- **Contemporary Maryland campus environment (R1 or regional university)**  
  A public research campus in Maryland in 2024 will have **mixed architecture**: 1960s–1980s brick classroom blocks, newer **glass‑and‑steel STEM buildings**, and scattered **portable or modular offices**.  
  Campus walkways are usually lined with **recycling/landfill bins**, bike racks, and branded **banners on lamp posts** displaying the university logo and slogans like “Research. Innovation. Impact.”

- **Office of Research / Sponsored Programs setting**  
  The **Office of Research, Sponsored Programs, or Sponsored Projects Administration** is often housed in an administrative or graduate studies building and features **cubicles, filing cabinets with labeled grant folders, and dual‑monitor setups**.  
  Walls commonly display **NSF and NIH award posters**, compliance notices (e.g., **IRB, export control**), and printed timelines for common sponsor deadlines.

- **Typical academic department hallway (anthropology/archaeology)**  
  Hallways in anthropology or archaeology departments commonly feature **glass display cases** with artifacts, **posters from conferences** (e.g., SAA, AAA), maps of **local excavation sites**, and flyers for **field schools**.  
  Office doors often carry **name plates with titles** (“Assistant Professor,” “Principal Investigator”) and taped‑up student office hours sheets.

- **Archaeology lab material culture (campus)**  
  A university archaeology lab usually contains **metal shelving with labeled acid‑free boxes**, plastic artifact bags, **sieves**, trowels, and **total station or GPS equipment cases** stacked against a wall.  
  Tables may hold **sherd sorting trays**, calipers, Munsell soil color charts, and **desktop computers** running GIS or database software (e.g., ArcGIS, Access), with faint dust and soil residue near work areas.

- **Terminology around site data and theft**  
  Archaeologists refer to early results as **“preliminary data,” “field notes,” “context sheets,”** and **“provenience records,”** all of which are considered part of the PI’s intellectual work.  
  Using another archaeologist’s unpublished site coordinates, unit plans, or artifact catalogs **without permission or attribution** is widely understood in the field as **data theft** or **misappropriation of research materials**, even if not yet formally published.

- **Academic status markers that sharpen the betrayal**  
  NSF grants are often central to **tenure and promotion** at research universities, used as metrics of **“extramural funding”** success on annual reviews and CVs.  
  A PI designation on a large NSF award can significantly affect **teaching load reductions, graduate student support (RAs), and lab space allocations**, making the theft materially consequential for Tayak’s career.
