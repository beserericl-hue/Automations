# Chapter 11 — write-time research

- **Annapolis as capital (1695 move)**  
  The capital of Maryland was formally moved from St. Mary’s City to **Anne Arundel Town** in 1695, and the town was renamed **Annapolis** in honor of Princess Anne soon afterward.[6]  
  The new capital was still partly a construction site in 1695–1697, with public buildings, streets, and residences rapidly going up around the harbor.

- **Assembly location and town layout**  
  The Maryland General Assembly met at Annapolis on the **high ground above the harbor**, in a small planned town laid out on a baroque radial plan with two main circles: one for the State House and one for the Anglican church (St. Anne’s).[6]  
  From the assembly hall steps a person could look downhill toward the creeks and the busy port, with masts and rigging visible above low wooden and brick houses.

- **State House / assembly chamber architecture**  
  The late-17th‑century Maryland State House was a **two‑story brick building** with a steeply pitched roof and small-paned sash or casement windows, not the later Georgian dome.  
  Inside, the assembly chamber would be a **rectangular room with plastered walls**, exposed beams or heavy timber framing overhead, and a wooden floor that creaked under boots.

- **Furnishings of the assembly room**  
  Delegates sat on **plain wooden benches or chairs** at long tables, while the Speaker or presiding officer occupied a **raised chair or dais** at one end of the room.  
  The clerk’s table would hold **inkstands, quill pens, loose paper, and bound journals**, with sand or pounce used to dry wet ink.

- **Number and composition of assembly members**  
  A full session of the lower house of the Maryland Assembly in the late 17th century could involve around **forty male members**, all propertied men of European descent.  
  They would include planters, merchants, lawyers, and local officeholders, many wearing the marks of tobacco wealth—good cloth and imported accessories—but still provincial compared to London fashion.

- **Contemporary political context (1695–1697)**  
  The period coincided with **King William’s War (1689–1697)** in North America, which heightened colonial concerns about defense, alliances with Native nations, and imperial authority.[6]  
  The Maryland government was under **royal control** after the 1689 Protestant Associators’ revolt, so colonial officials were particularly wary of anything that might seem to challenge Crown interests.

- **Legal vocabulary for petitions**  
  A formal petition to the assembly would be titled something like **“The humble petition of…”**, addressed to “**the honorable the Governor and Assembly of Maryland**.”  
  The assembly might respond with phrases such as **“Ordered, that the said petition be referred…”**, “**hereupon resolved**,” or “**that the consideration thereof be deferred**,” and record that it was **“tabled” or “laid upon the table”** pending further information.

- **“Tabled” and survey language**  
  To “table” an issue in this era was to **lay it aside or postpone consideration**, often “until the next session” or “until further inquiry be made.”  
  Ordering a “**further survey**” would invoke the work of the **surveyor general or county surveyor**, and the language might specify that “the bounds be more fully ascertained” or “the lands in question be again surveyed and returned into this house.”

- **Colonial land-survey practice**  
  A survey in Maryland would involve **measuring distances with a Gunter’s chain**, using a **compass** to take bearings, and marking trees or placing stakes or stones at boundary points.  
  Surveys were recorded in **plats (drawn plans)** and written descriptions using natural landmarks—“bounded by a great white oak,” “running down the river,” etc.—which left plenty of room for dispute.

- **Ink, pen, and paper**  
  Ink was typically a **dark iron‑gall ink**, made from oak galls, iron salts, and gum arabic; fresh it went on black and could dry to a slightly brownish tone.  
  Paper used for legal documents was **rag-based laid paper**, often folded into folio, with visible chain lines when held to the light, and sealed or stitched at the top or side.

- **Handprints and signatures**  
  Literate signers would write their **name in a flowing, somewhat angular late‑17th‑century hand**, with long tails and flourishes.  
  For illiterate or excluded signers, a **“mark”** (often an X or simple figure) was commonly used; pressing a **handprint in ink** would be unusual but understandable to contemporaries as a personal mark of identity and consent, akin to older sealing practices.

- **Seals and authentication**  
  Official assembly copies might carry **wax seals**—red or brownish wax impressed with a die—and the notation “**In testimony whereof…**” or “**Given under our hands and seals**.”  
  The clerk might write “**Examined and compared**” on a copy to show it matched the official entry in the assembly journal.

- **Smell and sound inside the chamber**  
  The room would smell of **tallow candles**, smoke from fireplaces or nearby hearths, **wool and leather**, tobacco breath, and occasionally spilled ale or wine from earlier conviviality.  
  Soundscape: **boots on wood, the scrape of chairs**, murmured side conversations, quills scratching, the presiding officer’s voice calling “**Order**,” and the clerk reading in a formal monotone.

- **Voting procedure and terminology**  
  Votes were often taken by **voice vote**—“**those in favor say Aye**,” “**those against say No**”—or by calling for members to stand or divide to different sides of the room.  
  The record might note that “**the question was put**” and that the matter was “**carried in the affirmative**” or “**resolved that the said petition lie upon the table**.”

- **Forms of address and decorum**  
  Members addressed each other as “**Mr. Speaker**,” “**Gentlemen of this House**,” or “**Honored Sir**,” and even sharp disagreements were couched in formally respectful language.  
  A woman or Native person appearing in the chamber would be highly unusual and might be referred to in the record in third person (“**the Indian woman called…**,” “**the said petitioner**”) rather than with full legal equality.

- **Racial and gender hierarchy in law**  
  In the 1690s, Maryland law codified **racialized slavery and limits on Native and African rights**, including restrictions on testimony and property holding in certain circumstances.  
  Women—especially Native women—had **no formal political standing** in the assembly; her presence and direct address would be extraordinary against this legal backdrop.

- **Anglican establishment and church nearby**  
  Annapolis was the seat of an established **Anglican church** (St. Anne’s), and many assemblymen would be vestrymen or regular attendees.  
  Church bells could be heard from the State House area marking hours or calling to service, blending with the secular business of lawmaking.

- **Clothing of English colonial men (1690s)**  
  A typical Maryland gentleman in 1697 would wear a **white linen shirt**, a neckcloth or cravat, a **long waistcoat**, and a **long-skirted coat (justaucorps)** with wide cuffs, plus knee-length **breeches**, wool or silk stockings, and leather shoes with small buckles.[1]  
  Colors could be sober browns and grays or richer blues and reds; fabric was usually **wool broadcloth** or serge, with finer men in imported English cloth.

- **Headgear and wigs**  
  Many officials and wealthier men wore **periwigs**—shoulder-length, curled wigs powdered lightly—or at least longer natural hair tied back; poorer members might go bareheaded or in their own hair.  
  **Broad-brimmed felt hats** were common when outdoors but removed or held under the arm in formal indoor assembly.

- **Women’s English clothing (urban, 1690s)**  
  An English or Anglo-colonial woman in Annapolis would wear a **linen shift**, **boned stays**, one or more **petticoats**, and a **mantua**, a gown cut in one piece from shoulder to hem and looped back to show the skirt.[1]  
  A **linen coif or cap** covered the hair, sometimes with an additional hat; colors and fabrics varied by wealth, from plain wool to silk or printed calicoes.

- **Possible Native women’s dress (Piscataway)**  
  In the late 17th century Chesapeake, Piscataway women commonly wore **wrap-around skirts of deerskin or woven fiber** and mantles of skin or cloth, sometimes decorated with beads or painted designs, though by 1697 some adopted **trade cloth** garments.  
  **Wampum belts, shell or glass bead necklaces, and metal trade ornaments** (such as brass or copper bracelets) were visible markers of status and alliance through trade.
