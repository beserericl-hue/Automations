# Chapter 20 — write-time research

- **Likely setting — Norway, 1962**  
  Norway in 1962 was a constitutional monarchy under King Olav V and a social‑democratic welfare state in its early postwar “golden age of industry,” with rapid growth, expanding hydroelectric development, and rising living standards.[1]  
  About 42% of the population still lived in rural districts or small towns, and many older people had grown up on self‑sufficient farms that combined small‑scale agriculture with forestry or fishing.[1]

- **Kitchen layout in an older wooden house**  
  Many mid‑century Norwegian homes, especially older timber houses, had a large *kjøkken* that doubled as living space, with the table near the window and a wood‑ or coal‑fired range plus, by the 1960s, often a modern electric stove.[1]  
  Walls were commonly painted in light, practical colors (pale green, cream) or covered in simple patterned wallpaper, with open shelving showing everyday enamelware, cups, and tins.  
  A woven rag rug (*fillerye*) could lie on the painted floorboards, worn thin along the walking paths between stove, sink, and table.

- **Material of the kitchen table and seating**  
  The kitchen table was typically plain scrubbed pine or birch, scarred by decades of bread‑making and knife work rather than covered with a decorative tablecloth for everyday use.  
  Chairs were often mismatched wooden chairs; in more traditional homes a built‑in corner bench (*kjøkkenbenk*) lined one wall, with a wool cushion that smelled faintly of smoke and old soap.

- **Lighting and electricity**  
  By 1962 rural Norway was largely electrified, so the main light would be a single bare bulb or a simple glass‑shaded pendant over the table, turned on by a pull‑cord or wall switch.  
  In the night hours, people often supplemented the harsh ceiling light with a kerosene lamp or wax candle on the table for a warmer, more intimate glow, especially in older houses where frugality and habit lingered.

- **Heating and indoor temperature**  
  Many older homes combined an iron wood‑burning stove in the kitchen with a tiled or brick stove in another room; by early 1960s winter nights, the kitchen would still be the warmest room.[1]  
  The air would carry a mix of woodsmoke, dried pine, and a faint chill drafting from single‑pane windows, which might show condensation or frost patterns in colder seasons.

- **Common kitchen objects and brands**  
  Enamel pots and mugs (often white with blue rims), a heavy cast‑iron frying pan, and aluminum coffee pots were standard; glass jars reused from store‑bought jam or herring held sugar and flour.  
  A wall‑hung coffee grinder, a wooden butter churn that might now be mostly decorative, and a simple weighing scale with metal weights are plausible for a woman born c. 1898.  
  Branded items such as tins of Freia cocoa or sugar, blue‑and‑white salt packets, and maybe an old margarine tin would stand on shelves, their printed Norwegian labels slightly faded.

- **Food smells and late‑night coffee**  
  Coffee (*kaffe*) was central to hospitality; by 1962 it was brewed in a stovetop pot, often medium‑roasted and served black, perhaps with sugar and evaporated milk or farm milk.  
  The kitchen might smell of boiled potatoes, fried herring or meatballs from supper, and lingering aromas of strong coffee, boiled milk, and yeast from homemade bread or *lefse*.  
  Leftover slices of dark rye bread (*grovbrød*) or flatbread with brown cheese (*brunost*) or homemade jam would be the natural late‑night snack.

- **Domestic routines and women’s work**  
  Older Norwegian women who had grown up on self‑sufficient farms were used to baking bread weekly, preserving berries as jam, salting or drying fish, and sewing or mending clothes through the dark evenings.[1]  
  The 1960s generation of housewives often combined these older skills with newer conveniences like electric mixers, refrigerators, and store‑bought bread, but many in their sixties still trusted traditional methods.

- **Radio culture in 1962**  
  In many homes the *Norsk Rikskringkasting* (NRK) radio sat on a shelf or counter, a wooden or Bakelite set broadcasting national news, Lutheran services, farm reports, and evening programs.  
  Late at night, the radio might be off, but its presence as a familiar object—cloth dust cover, the faint smell of warm dust when turned on—is period‑accurate for a 64‑year‑old’s kitchen.

- **Religious backdrop and iconography**  
  Most Norwegians in 1962 belonged to the Church of Norway (Lutheran), though practice varied; older women often kept a Bible, a *salmebok* (hymn book), and devotional booklets on a shelf or in a drawer.  
  A simple wall cross or a framed print of Jesus as shepherd, the Good Samaritan, or a confirmation photograph in stiff formal dress might hang near the kitchen or in the adjoining *stue*.

- **Norwegian terms for kin and respect**  
  Grandmothers were commonly addressed as *bestemor* or *mormor*/*farmor* depending on maternal or paternal line.  
  A girl of 20 in 1962 speaking to a 64‑year‑old grandmother would typically use informal pronouns but retain deference through tone and posture; older generations might still remember when the formal *De* was common, though it was fading from everyday speech.

- **Oral storytelling customs**  
  Norway has a long tradition of *fortellinger* (tales) and *sagn* (legends) passed down orally in families, especially in rural districts where stories about burial mounds, trolls, and local spirits were tied to specific landmarks.[1]  
  Older narrators often anchored tales with exact place names—fields, lakes, ridges—and with remembered ancestors (“that was in old‑Matta’s time, before the new road was built”), linking story to genealogy.

- **Burial mounds and local landscape**  
  Prehistoric burial mounds (*gravhauger*) are scattered throughout much of rural Norway, often on slight rises overlooking fields or waterways; many were incorporated into farm boundaries, used as pasture, or simply left as grassy knolls.[1]  
  By the mid‑20th century, locals might know which mounds were protected, and children would be warned not to dig because of both law and superstition about disturbing the dead.

- **Layers of land use around a mound**  
  A burial mound on a working farm would likely be surrounded by strip fields or grazing pasture; in the 19th and early 20th centuries these areas might have seen crop rotations of barley, oats, and later potatoes as these displaced older barley porridge diets.[1]  
  Stone clearance piles, low dry‑stone walls, and traces of older field boundaries can mark changing use over generations; hay drying racks (*hesjer*) in summer would stand nearby, turning the mound into just another element of a working landscape.

- **Generational memory of hardship**  
  A woman born c. 1898 would remember stories of 19th‑century hunger years, when simple diets of porridge, gruel, and flatbreads predominated, and when famine and starvation were recurring threats in rural Norway.[1]  
  She would herself have lived through World War I shortages, the interwar depression, and World War II occupation, when rationing and substitute foods reawakened older memories of scarcity.

- **World War II as shared reference**  
  Ida in 1962 would vividly recall the German occupation of Norway (1940–1945), including blackout curtains, curfews, German patrols on roads and docks, and ration cards for food and fuel.  
  Many rural families secretly listened to BBC broadcasts, hid radios, or knew of neighbors who joined the resistance, were arrested, or saw sabotage actions against rail lines or ships.

- **Postwar modernization reaching the countryside**  
  After 1945, Norwegian rural life saw steadily increasing access to electricity, tractors replacing horse labor, and better roads connecting isolated farms to market towns.[1]  
  By 1962, buses or battered private cars served routes that had once been traveled only on foot, by boat, or horse sled; for an elderly woman, this pace of change could be startling and a frequent subject of commentary.

- **Health care and a heart condition in 1962**  
  Norway established a public health system and gradually expanding social insurance in the decades before 1962, so Ida likely had access to a local *distriktslege* (district doctor) and possibly a small hospital in the nearest town.[1]  
  Diagnosis of a heart condition would typically involve a stethoscope exam and blood pressure measurement; electrocardiograms were available in hospitals but not in every rural clinic, and older patients often underplayed symptoms, viewing breathlessness or chest tightness as “just age.”

- **Attitudes toward illness and secrecy**  
  Older Norwegians who had grown up in times of scarcity and limited medical options were often stoical about chronic illness, reluctant to “burden” family by talking about it.
