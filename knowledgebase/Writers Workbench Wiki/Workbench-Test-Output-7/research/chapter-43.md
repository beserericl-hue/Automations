# Chapter 43 — write-time research

- **Approximate date and wider world (c. 1212 CE)**  
  Around 1212 CE, **Innocent III** is pope and the **Fifth Crusade** is being organized in Europe, with rumors of crusading and distant wars sometimes filtering into Indigenous awareness through Norse and later European contacts.[3]  
  Norse Greenland and the last phases of **Norse presence in North America** are still within living cultural memory for some North Atlantic peoples around this time.[3]  

- **Likely cultural‑linguistic setting (Eastern Woodlands, Mississippian interaction sphere)**  
  Circa 1200–1250, large mound centers like **Cahokia** near the Mississippi River are at or just past their peak, with wide trade networks spreading shell, copper, and stone across the Eastern Woodlands.  
  Shell gorgets and copper ornaments travel hundreds of miles along river systems, moving between peoples linked by alliance, marriage, ritual, and trade rather than centralized empires.  

- **Burial mounds and ancestor veneration**  
  Many Eastern Woodlands societies build **earthen mounds** as burial or ceremonial places, sometimes reused for generations as a focus for family or lineage burials.  
  Grave goods often include **shell gorgets, copper ornaments, stone tools, and pottery**, placed intentionally to accompany the dead or to mark status or spiritual roles.  

- **Shell gorgets as prestige items**  
  Shell gorgets in this period are often carved from large **marine conch or whelk** shells brought inland from the Gulf or Atlantic coasts via trade routes.  
  Gorgets are commonly engraved with motifs such as **coiled rattlesnakes, birds of prey, hand‑and‑eye symbols, or spiral designs**, each carrying layered cosmological meaning.  

- **Copper pendants: material and origin**  
  Native copper used in pendants and small ornaments is often obtained from **Great Lakes copper deposits**, hammered and cold‑worked rather than smelted.  
  Copper objects are labor‑intensive to produce; even small pendants are meaningful items, signifying connections, status, or spiritual power rather than casual decoration.  

- **Transmission of heirloom objects**  
  Prestigious items like shell gorgets and copper pendants frequently circulate within families as **heirlooms**, passed down through generations to mark continuity and obligation to ancestors.  
  Objects linked to key life events—births, deaths, visions, war honors—are often retained for years before being deliberately buried, broken, or deposited in a mound as an offering.  

- **Infant and child mortality realities**  
  In Indigenous North America circa 1200, **child mortality is high**, with fevers and sudden illnesses common and often without apparent cause or cure.  
  Brief illnesses that progress rapidly to death are associated with **hot fevers, rapid breathing, delirium, and dehydration**, with families relying on herbal remedies, sweating, and prayer when available.  

- **Concepts of grief and silence**  
  Many societies consider grief a **private, dangerous, or powerful state**, with strict expectations about when and how mourning is voiced and who may be addressed.  
  Prolonged silence, withdrawal, or solitary tasks like hunting or gathering can serve as socially recognizable ways to express grief without openly speaking the loss.  

- **Women hunting in forested environments**  
  While many Eastern Woodlands cultures have gendered hunting norms, women sometimes hunt small game, trap animals, or forage extensively—especially during personal crises or when food is needed.  
  Solitary hunting in dense forest demands keen attention to **wind direction, ground noise, and animal sign**; moving alone heightens awareness of every twig snap, bird alarm call, and shift of light.  

- **Typical Eastern Woodlands forest biome**  
  Mixed hardwood forests include **oak, hickory, maple, beech, and walnut**, with undergrowth of hazel, sumac, brambles, and young saplings.  
  In late spring or summer, the forest air can be **humid and still**, carrying smells of damp leaf mold, crushed plants, and animal musk; at other seasons it is sharp with cold and woodsmoke.  

- **Riverine and wetland landscapes**  
  Communities are frequently located near **rivers, streams, and backwater sloughs**, with canoe routes serving as major travel and trade corridors.  
  Wetlands feature **cattails, sedges, and reeds**, humming with insects and frogs; the water surface may steam in cool mornings or mirror the treeline at dusk.  

- **Hunting weapons and techniques**  
  Hunters use **self bows or short recurved bows** of hickory or other hardwoods, with river‑cane or hardwood arrows tipped with finely knapped stone or bone points.  
  Small game such as **rabbits, squirrels, and birds** can be taken with light arrows or clubs; larger game like **deer** require patience at trails, salt licks, or feeding sites, often using natural blinds.  

- **Carrying and field processing game**  
  Game may be field‑dressed with a **flaked chert knife**, removing entrails to lighten the load and cool the meat quickly.  
  Meat and hides are transported on a **tump‑line** across the forehead or shoulder, or slung on a pole between two people; a lone hunter might drag larger carcasses on leaf‑covered ground.  

- **Everyday clothing (woodland setting)**  
  Clothing commonly consists of **tanned deerskin leggings, wrap skirts or breechcloths, and a belted tunic or mantle**, adjusted for season.  
  Footwear is often **soft‑soled moccasins** of smoked buckskin, which darken with moisture and pick up the smell of earth, crushed plants, and smoke.  

- **Ornament wear and body feel**  
  A shell gorget hangs on a **twisted plant‑fiber, sinew, or leather thong**, resting cool and hard against the upper chest until warmed by body heat.  
  A copper pendant warms quickly on the skin but can leave a faint **greenish discoloration** or metallic scent where sweat touches the metal.  

- **Hairstyles and grooming**  
  Hair is frequently worn **long and braided**, sometimes with **otter fur wraps, feathers, or small shell and copper beads** woven in for ceremony or personal expression.  
  Daily grooming includes **combing with bone or wooden combs**, plucking stray facial hair, and sometimes applying bear grease or rendered fat to the hair for gloss and protection.  

- **Domestic architecture (house and village layout)**  
  Many Eastern Woodlands villages use **pole‑and‑thatch houses or wattle‑and‑daub structures**, with curved or rectilinear walls and smoke holes in the roof.  
  Houses cluster around open communal spaces, fire pits, or plazas, with **storage pits**, drying racks, and work areas arranged between dwellings.  

- **Interior of the house**  
  Inside a typical house are **packed earth floors**, raised sleeping platforms of poles and mats, hanging **bundles of dried herbs, maize, and meat**, and personal possessions in skin bags or baskets.  
  Smoke from the central hearth blackens roof beams and leaves a **persistent smell of woodsmoke**, which clings to clothing and hair and helps repel insects.  

- **Daily subsistence work (women’s sphere)**  
  Women’s daily tasks often center on **maize horticulture**, tending gardens of corn, beans, squash, sunflowers, and tobacco near the village.  
  Other routine work includes **grinding dried maize on stone slabs, tanning hides, weaving mats, making pottery, and gathering wild plants and firewood**.  

- **Seasonal rhythms and timekeeping**  
  Time is tracked by **moons and seasonal events** (e.g., “planting moon,” “ripe corn moon”), with certain activities expected at particular times in the yearly cycle.  
  Sudden death of a child at an “out of season” time—such as during planting or harvest—may disturb the normal flow of work and ritual, forcing relatives to redistribute tasks.  

- **Healing practices for fevers**  
  Herbal remedies might include teas or decoctions from **willow, willow bark, or other local plants** with fever‑reducing and pain‑relieving properties.  
  Sweat practices—such as **heated stones with water in enclosed spaces**—may be used to “sweat out” illness, followed by immersion in cool water and rest.  

- **Soundscape of a solitary hunt**  
  Away from the village, sounds shift to the **rustle of leaves, bird calls, squirrel chatter, woodpeckers, and distant crows**, with sudden silences when a predator or human approaches.  
  Insects form a constant background of **buzzing, clicking, or droning**, especially near wetlands or in warm seasons.  

- **Olfactory and tactile environment**  
  The forest alternates between the moist scent of **decaying leaves and moss** in low hollows and the drier smell of sun‑warmed bark and pine needles on ridges.  
  Bare or moccasin‑covered feet feel **roots, stones, damp soil, and patches of cold water**, with occasional sharpness of twigs or burrs piercing worn soles.
