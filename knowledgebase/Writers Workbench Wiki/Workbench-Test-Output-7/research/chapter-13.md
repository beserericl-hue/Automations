# Chapter 13 — write-time research

- **Oilskin wrapping (material and use)**  
  In the late 17th century, “oilskin” was usually **linen or hemp cloth** soaked in linseed oil, sometimes mixed with resin, then hung to dry until it formed a slightly stiff, water‑repellent sheet.[1]  
  Such oiled cloth was commonly used in Europe and coastal Africa for protecting charts, letters, and goods from damp in a climate where mildew and insects quickly destroyed untreated paper.[1]  

- **Petition document (paper, ink, script)**  
  By 1697, petitions to European or coastal African authorities were typically written on **rag-based laid paper** with visible chain lines when held to firelight.[1]  
  Ink would likely be **iron gall ink**, which under lamplight looks brown‑black and slightly glossy where heavily applied, and the hand could be an uneven secretary or italic script if written by a local scribe trained in European styles.[1]  

- **Glass bead (trade bead types)**  
  In the 17th century, coastal West and Central Africa were flooded with **European glass trade beads**, especially small blue “**aggry**” or “Guinea” beads made in Venice and the Dutch Republic.[1][4]  
  Such beads often had slightly pitted surfaces from imperfect firing and a cold, heavy feel in the hand compared with local organic ornaments.  

- **Bead as grave or memorial item**  
  Archaeology from West African and Atlantic-African contexts shows **glass beads placed with the dead or as mementos** on burial sites during the 17th century, blending older bead traditions with imported trade items.[3][7]  
  The bead Saya left him could plausibly be a **single blue or white wound bead**, a valued object but common enough in coastal trading communities.  

- **Burial mound as layered deposit (stratigraphy)**  
  Old mounds in West Africa and along the Atlantic seaboard often consist of **accumulated layers of compacted earth, ash, pottery sherds, and organic refuse**, producing distinct “strata” visible when cut.[3]  
  The **surface layer** in 1697 would likely be a thin zone of loose soil mixed with recent leaf litter, small charcoal flecks from cooking fires, and occasional broken ceramics or shell fragments from daily dumping.  

- **Soil feel and smell at night**  
  In a humid tropical or sub‑tropical environment, **topsoil at night** is cool and slightly clammy to the touch, with a strong smell of wet earth, rotting leaves, and smoke residue from evening cooking fires.  
  Digging with bare hands or a small hoe would quickly blacken fingernails with humus and release earthworms and beetle larvae common in midden-rich mound soils.  

- **No formal ceremony (contrast with norm)**  
  Many 17th‑century African societies marked important burials with **libations (poured drink), spoken invocations, and offerings**, sometimes including beads, cloth, or food.[3][4]  
  His choice to speak **no words and pour no libation** is a stark departure from these norms and would be felt as consciously withholding the expected rites rather than simple omission.  

- **Night sounds (acoustic environment)**  
  Away from major towns in 1697, nights were densely filled with sound: **insects, frogs after rain, distant dogs, and the occasional call of night birds or fruit bats**.  
  If near fields or forest edge, there would be rustling from small mammals and the intermittent thud of falling fruit or pods in the dark.  

- **Night light sources and sky**  
  Artificial light at night was limited to **oil lamps, tallow candles, or resinous torches**, all of which smoked and gave a wavering, yellow‑orange light with a strong smell of fat or pitch.[1]  
  Away from dense settlement, the **Milky Way and bright planets** were strikingly visible, and on a clear, moonless night, a mound’s outline would be only a darker mass against the sky.  

- **Clothing fibers and feel (everyday male dress)**  
  In 17th‑century West Africa, men commonly wore **handwoven cotton cloth**, sometimes in strip-woven panels sewn together, with coarser, homespun texture than European broadcloth.[3][4]  
  Where European contact was intense, a man might mix local garments with **European woolen or linen items**—a traded shirt or coat—whose seams and buttons felt different and heavier than indigenous wrap garments.  

- **Footwear and ground contact**  
  Many men went **barefoot or in simple leather sandals**, so each step on the mound would transmit small changes: packed earth vs. looser soil; crunch of potsherds or crushed shell; dampness in depressions after rain.[3]  
  Bare soles would register **ant bites, small stones, and temperature gradients**—warm where ash lay near the surface, cooler in shaded or grassy patches.  

- **Tools for digging (availability and form)**  
  Agricultural communities commonly used **short-handled iron hoes** with wooden shafts for field work, which also served for digging small pits and graves.[3][7]  
  Iron was valuable, so a single hoe might be carefully maintained: the blade hammered thinner from repeated sharpening, the wooden handle polished smooth and slightly greasy from years of use.  

- **Household at night (when he returns)**  
  In 1697, households typically settled into darkness soon after **evening cooking**, with fires banked under ash to preserve coals for the morning.  
  Indoors, smoke from the hearth and lamps would linger as a faint haze, impregnating **woven mats, thatch, and clothing** with a sweet‑acrid, familiar smell.  

- **Sleep arrangements and surfaces**  
  Sleeping surfaces were usually **woven mats on packed earth floors**, sometimes with folded cloths or stuffed pallets for elders or higher‑status individuals.[3][4]  
  Nights without sleep meant feeling every hardness of the ground, every small stone under the mat, and hearing the slow creak of the roof frame as it cooled.  

- **Disease and fever (Saya’s death)**  
  In late 17th‑century West Africa and similar climates, **recurrent fevers**—from malaria, typhoid, and other infections—were common causes of death, especially in the cool, mosquito‑heavy months after the rains.[3][7]  
  Such fevers often progressed with bouts of sweating, chills, and delirium over several days rather than spectacular external symptoms, leaving survivors with little more than a memory of heat and weakness.  

- **Burial and mourning timing (previous winter)**  
  In many African communities, the dead were buried **within a day** because of climate, but mourning—marked by specific clothing, hair, or jewelry changes—could stretch for months.[3][4]  
  The glass bead Saya left could plausibly be **part of a mourning necklace or bracelet** he has worn or carried since her burial, now relocated to the mound.  

- **Terminology for petition (local and European)**  
  A written plea to authorities in 1697 could be called a **“petition”** in European usage, while local interpreters might gloss it as a **“letter of complaint” or “words tied on paper”**, distinguishing it from oral palaver.  
  Intermediaries—scribes, interpreters, or Christian catechists—were often known by titles referencing **“writer” or “book-man”**, marking literacy as a specialized craft.  

- **Trade and political backdrop in 1697**  
  In 1697, the **English Parliament ended the Royal African Company’s monopoly**, opening trade with Africa to more English merchants and increasing competition on the coast.[1]  
  This shift brought more ships, more independent traders, and heightened tensions around treaties, land, and slave‑raiding, giving petitions and written documents unusual political weight.  

- **Everyday smells around the mound**  
  Near settlements, a mound reused as a refuse or burial place would carry mixed odors: **wood smoke, dried fish, human and animal waste, crushed herbs, and decaying organic matter**.  
  At night, humidity intensifies these scents; stepping near disturbed earth or old refuse layers releases sudden, sharp whiffs of rot or sour fermentation.  

- **Insects and animal traces in soil**  
  In 17th‑century tropical and sub‑tropical soils, shallow digging turns up **termite tunnels, ant galleries, beetle grubs, and occasional fragments of bone** from prior deposits.  
  Ants and termites quickly investigate any new disturbance, so by the time he returns home, a few stray ants could be crawling on his clothing or skin from the mound.  

- **Use of oilskin in the hand (tactile detail)**  
  Properly cured oilskin feels **stiff but slightly tacky**, especially in warmth; when folded, it creases with a faint crackle rather than a soft rustle like plain cloth.[1]  
  As it warms against the body, a subtle **smell of oil and resin** emerges, and in a quiet night he could hear the faint squeak of layers rubbing together as he walks.
