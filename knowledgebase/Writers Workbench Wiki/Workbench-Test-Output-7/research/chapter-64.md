# Chapter 64 — write-time research

- **NSF withdrawal process** — A proposal could be withdrawn *at any time before a funding recommendation was made* by the cognizant NSF Program Officer, and by 2021 NSF had added withdrawal functionality in Research.gov for both single submissions and subawards.[3][7]

- **2024 tribal approval requirement** — For proposals that might impact Tribal Nation resources, NSF’s 2024 PAPPG required researchers to obtain Tribal Nation approval before award; at submission time, the proposal had to document the *request* for approval, and if funded, written approval was required before NSF would issue the award.[1]

- **Formal institutional support language** — The NSF materials distinguish between a proposal’s technical components and supplemental documentation such as a *request for approval* or supporting institutional materials, so a letter of institutional support would fit the kind of nontechnical attachment or correspondence used to document compliance and backing in the proposal record.[1][3]

- **Mound site fieldwork** — A “mound” in an archaeological context is a deliberately constructed earthen rise, often associated with burial, ceremonial, or habitation features; in the field it is commonly marked by flagged grid lines, measuring tapes, photo scales, bags for artifacts, and careful note-taking in a field log. When access is sensitive or politically charged, archaeologists often document site visits with explicit terminology about *consultation*, *tribal approval*, and *resource impact*.[1]

- **Field notes and logs** — Archaeological field logs are typically written in terse, time-stamped prose that records weather, soil color/texture, equipment used, who was present, and interpretive distinctions between *observation*, *inference*, and *institutional communication*. A novelist can realistically have Tayak note the difference between “support offered” and “forgiveness withheld” as a professional distinction in a logbook, because field documentation commonly separates factual observation from personal assessment.[1]

- **Contemporary research administration** — By 2024, NSF proposal preparation had moved into Research.gov for many functions, and the agency was updating proposal components such as mentoring plans, biosketch formatting, and new disclosure forms; that administrative context supports a scene in which an applicant responds to a compliance or institutional development rather than an informal handshake resolution.[1][9]

- **Sensitivity around tribal consultation** — In contemporary U.S. heritage work, interactions involving burial mounds and tribal recognition are often framed by consultation and sovereignty language rather than purely academic ownership claims. The most realistic institutional tone is formal, cautious, and paper-driven, with letters, approvals, and documented requests carrying real procedural weight.[1]

- **Local archaeological work habits** — On a mound site, day-to-day work often involves sunscreen, insect repellent, work gloves, field boots, water jugs, clipboards or rugged tablets, and repeated visual checks of slope stability and soil disturbance. The sensory backdrop is usually wind over open ground, the scrape of trowels, the smell of damp earth, and the visual contrast of pale cut soil against darker undisturbed deposits.
