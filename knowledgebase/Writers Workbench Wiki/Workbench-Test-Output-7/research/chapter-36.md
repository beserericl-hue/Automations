# Chapter 36 — write-time research

- **Geographic setting (North American Eastern Woodlands, c.1205 CE)**  
  Many Indigenous maize‑farming societies in this period lived in **river‑valley floodplains** along tributaries of the Mississippi and Ohio, where flat alluvial soils were easy to hoe and normally held moisture well in summer.  
  Surrounding the fields, upland **oak–hickory forest** provided nuts, firewood, and construction timber, with patches of wetland reeds and willows along stream margins.

- **Burial mound as community center**  
  In many Mississippian‑influenced towns, an older **burial mound** (rounded, often forest‑capped) could sit near or within a newer village marked by flat‑topped platform mounds and a central plaza, functioning as an ancestral focus rather than elite residence.  
  Such mounds were often slightly raised above floodplain level, making them one of the few reliably dry, open places when surrounding ground was muddy or seasonally flooded.

- **Construction and appearance of the mound**  
  Burial mounds were built in **successive basket loads of earth**, producing visible soil layers if a section eroded—dark bands of midden soil alternating with lighter subsoil.  
  The surface might be partly **sodded with short grass**, with areas worn bare where people regularly climbed or circled during ceremonies, exposing packed, sun‑hardened clay that cracks in drought.

- **Village layout around the mound**  
  Typical villages had **closely spaced houses** around a central open space, with palisade walls of upright logs encircling the settlement if conflict was a concern.  
  Paths ran like spokes from the central space—toward the river landing, outlying fields, and neighboring settlements—visibly less trodden when families left, leaving grass and small weeds reclaiming the earth.

- **House construction and interiors**  
  Houses were usually **rectangular or square wattle‑and‑daub** structures: a framework of saplings woven with branches and plastered with clay, topped by a thatched roof of marsh grass or bark shingles.  
  Inside, **packed earth floors** bore ash stains from hearths, storage pits for maize and beans, and reed or skin sleeping mats; in drought, dust from the floor rose more easily with each footstep or when mats were shaken.

- **Fields and crops under drought stress**  
  Agricultural plots were dominated by the **“Three Sisters”**: maize, beans, and squash, often planted together in small mounds or ridges.  
  In the **third year of drought**, maize leaves would curl and yellow at the tips, ears staying stunted with loose, incompletely filled kernels, while bean vines on the stalks wither instead of thickening the green shade between rows.

- **Signs of multi‑year drought in the landscape**  
  River levels drop, leaving **wide bands of exposed, sun‑bleached gravel and cracked mud** below the usual waterline, with beached canoe landings farther from the water than people remember.  
  Shallow streams that once ran clear become **stagnant pools** with algae scum and trapped fish, surrounded by a sour, fish‑rot smell, and reeds that once rustled green turn brown and brittle.

- **Food shortages and dietary shifts**  
  In persistent drought, families rely more on **stored maize** from previous years, pounding older, harder kernels into meal for thin porridge, with fewer fresh vegetables to vary the diet.  
  People intensify **foraging for drought‑tolerant foods**—acorns leached of bitterness, hickory nuts pounded into nut milk, dried venison or fish—and may chew on parched, half‑moldy kernels that would once have been fed only to dogs.

- **Storage pits and dwindling reserves**  
  Subterranean **storage pits** lined with basketry or bark keep grain cool and dry, but in a long drought the earth around them becomes powdery and cracks radiate from their mouths.  
  When opened, pits that once exhaled the smell of abundance now release a **faint sour, dusty odor** and reveal low heaps of cobwebbed cobs or sacks, the bottom layer powdery and eaten by weevils.

- **Clothing and adornment in everyday use**  
  Clothing commonly combined **tanned deerskin** (soft, smoky smell, suede texture) with woven plant fibers; men might wear breechcloths and leggings, women wrap skirts or dresses, with mantles against chill mornings.  
  Jewelry and ritual adornment could include **shell beads, copper ornaments, and dyed feathers**, which in drought feel heavy and hot against sun‑baked skin, leaving pale, sweat‑salt outlines when removed.

- **Water sources and daily labor**  
  As springs weaken, women and older children walk **farther to fetch water**, carrying it back in large ceramic jars or tightly woven waterproof baskets, the sloshing weight cutting into shoulder straps.  
  Water in household pots turns **cloudy more quickly**, sometimes with a faint clay or algae taste, so people let sediment settle, watching a thin line of mud form at the bottom before dipping a gourd ladle.

- **Ceramic vessels and sounds**  
  Pottery is hand‑built and low‑fired, with **temper** (crushed shell, sand, or grit) visible in the clay; surfaces may be burnished smooth or decorated with incised or stamped geometric patterns.  
  In dry air, **empty jars ring with a higher, sharper sound** when struck or moved, and when many vessels are set down on hard ground at once, they produce a chorus of hollow, brittle clinks.

- **Fire, smoke, and air quality**  
  Drought means **more frequent small grass or brush fires**, sending a persistent tang of ash across the settlement that mixes with the everyday woodsmoke from cooking fires.  
  The air on hot days hangs **motionless and dusty**, so smoke from hearths climbs slowly, then spreads in a thin sheet that stings eyes and clings to hair and clothing.

- **Forest and animal behavior in drought**  
  Trees drop **premature, yellowed leaves**, and some smaller saplings simply die back, leaving stands of gray, leafless switches along paths.  
  Game animals concentrate around the few remaining waterholes; **deer trails become deeply etched, dusty corridors**, trampled bare, and deer grow thinner, with ribs faintly visible under their summer coats.

- **Social signs of families leaving**  
  When a family leaves, their house may be **dismantled partially**, with reusable posts, mats, and tools taken; remaining walls slouch as lashings rot, and wind rattles loose thatch.  
  Abandoned hearths fill with **wind‑blown leaves and ash**, and storage pits stand open and empty, their rims crumbling inward, while once‑crowded household areas grow quiet enough that weeds sprout where children used to play.

- **Counting families and social units**  
  The community can be thought of in **households clustered by kin**, each with its own fire but linked in larger lineages that share fields and obligations.  
  Counting “seven families” gone would imply the loss of multiple **hearths, field labor groups, and ritual participants**, leaving visible gaps at ceremonies and in the work parties that tend fields or maintain the palisade.

- **Ceremonial use of the mound**  
  Mounds often served as places for **offerings to ancestors and spirit powers**, with shallow pits or stone‑lined spots where people left food, tobacco, shell beads, or red ochre.  
  Regular processions might circle the mound **sunwise (clockwise)**, feet treading a distinct path around its base; in drought, the path becomes a **ring of fine dust** that puffs under every step and clings to damp ankles.

- **Ritual paraphernalia for rain ceremonies**  
  Ceremonial leaders might carry **painted gourds, feathered staffs, carved wooden pipes, or rattles filled with pebbles or seeds**, their sounds punctuating chant and prayer.  
  A **smoking pipe** filled with sacred tobacco or herb mixtures could be lit and the smoke directed toward the sky directions, the leader’s breath tasting bitter and dry as it catches in a throat already parched by the heat.

- **Colors, pigments, and body paint**  
  People used mineral pigments like **red ochre, white clay, and black charcoal** to paint faces and bodies in patterns linked to clan, ritual role, or petition.  
  In hot, rainless weather, body paint **cracks and flakes** more quickly, leaving streaks on cheeks and arms, and the fine dust mixes with sweat, coloring the damp lines that run down from temples and along the spine.

- **Soundscape during ceremony**  
  In drought‑stilled air, even soft **foot scuffs on dry dirt** and the rustle of garments around the mound carry clearly; insects buzz insistently in the grasses, louder than usual human murmurs.  
  Rattles, drums made from **sun‑tightened rawhide**, and chanting voices echo slightly off the mound’s slope and nearby houses, but there is no answering roll of thunder—only the dry crackle of grass in a faint breeze.

- **Sky and weather cues in a failing rainy season**
