# Chapter 67 — write-time research

- **Chronological context (c. 99 BC)**  
  Around 99 BC, many regions of the Old World were in the late Iron Age, with small agrarian communities using iron tools alongside traditional stone, bone, and wooden implements.[1]  
  In many non-urban societies, ritual deposits in the ground (weapons, jewelry, figurines) were made at spots in the landscape considered sacred or “special” long before formal mounds or monuments were built over them.[1]

- **Meaning of a low rise in the landscape**  
  Slight natural rises, knolls, or drumlins were often treated as spiritually charged places, especially if they stood out from surrounding flat land or were aligned with a watercourse or the path of the sun.[1]  
  Even when their origin was unknown, communities frequently maintained taboos or customs around such places (not plowing them, leaving offerings, or gathering there at particular times of year).[1]

- **Early stages of a burial mound**  
  Many prehistoric burial mounds began as a single small grave or deposit, with additional burials or offerings gradually enlarging the rise into a noticeable artificial hill.[1]  
  The earliest layer typically contained the most important or original deposit, often more carefully placed and sometimes associated with a named ancestor or founding figure.[1]

- **Soil and digging by hand**  
  In temperate zones, topsoil on a low rise would likely be a dark, loose humus layer over denser subsoil; digging by hand would first stain and pack under the fingernails, then meet cooler, slightly wetter earth beneath.  
  Without tools, she would break the surface crust using fingers and the heel of her hand, then scoop earth out, feeling small roots, stones, and fibrous plant matter tug and snap.

- **Copper pendant: material and appearance**  
  By the late Iron Age, copper ornaments often had a dull reddish-brown color, sometimes with green spots of malachite patina where they had weathered.[1]  
  Hand-worked copper was usually hammered into thin sheet or simple forms, leaving faint hammer marks and slightly irregular edges that could be felt by touch.

- **Common forms of small copper ornaments**  
  Simple pendants were often looped discs, small plaques, or stylized figures with a hole punched near the top for suspension on a cord.  
  Surface decoration might consist of incised lines, punched dots, or shallow grooves forming patterns or simple symbols, which could be felt as fine ridges under the fingers.

- **Organic cord for the pendant**  
  Pendants were commonly hung on cords made from twisted plant fibers (flax, nettle, hemp, inner bark) or animal products such as sinew or leather thong.  
  Plant-fiber cords feel slightly rough and fibrous, sometimes leaving a faint vegetable smell, whereas leather thongs feel smoother, slightly oily, and warm quickly against the skin.

- **Small carved figures (Ranu’s figure)**  
  Small household figures in many ancient cultures were carved from readily available materials: local wood, bone, antler, or soft stone.  
  A wooden figurine would show knife or scraper marks, a slight grain pattern, and spots where handling had polished it darker and smoother over time, especially on the head and torso.

- **Burial positioning of objects**  
  Ritual deposits frequently placed paired objects side by side or with the more precious object slightly deeper and the companion object nearer the surface.  
  Careful placement—straightening the pendant, aligning the figurine, smoothing the soil over them—was a common act of respect and intentionality in such deposits.

- **Spoken names and ritual repetition**  
  Repeating a name three times in ritual or solemn contexts was common across many ancient traditions, associated with binding an intention, summoning, or ensuring the words “took hold.”  
  Saying a name aloud over a grave or offering was a way of ensuring both the dead and the land “heard” and remembered the person being invoked.

- **Concept of earth as a living presence**  
  Many pre-modern societies personified the earth as a female or maternal figure that could “know,” “remember,” and “receive” what was buried within her.  
  Speaking directly to the ground, touching it, and smoothing it after burial were physical ways people enacted a relationship with this living earth.

- **Daily clothing of a rural woman (temperate Old World analog)**  
  A typical woman in a small agrarian community might wear a simple wool or linen tunic belted at the waist, with a cloak or shawl for warmth and protection from dirt.  
  Clothing was usually handwoven, dyed in muted tones (browns, greys, soft reds, or yellows) with vegetable dyes, and fastened with simple pins, ties, or brooches.

- **Footwear and contact with the ground**  
  Footwear in many rural communities consisted of simple leather shoes or low boots, sometimes crudely stitched, often worn without socks.  
  On uneven ground near a rise, she would feel small stones and packed earth through the thin soles; if barefoot, the soil’s temperature difference—warmer on the surface, cooler where it has been turned—would be pronounced.

- **Hair and adornment**  
  Women’s hair was often worn in braids, buns, or knots secured with bone or wooden pins or simple bronze/copper hair rings.  
  Everyday adornment might be minimal: one or two small pendants, a simple bracelet, or bead necklace, items that could carry personal or familial meaning.

- **Landscape around a low natural rise**  
  A small rise in an agrarian landscape would likely be surrounded by mixed-use land: fields, rough pasture, or scrub, with stands of bushes and hardy grasses on the slope itself where plows did not reach.  
  The knoll might offer a slightly wider view: distant tree lines, a visible river or stream glinting in the distance, and the outlines of her settlement’s rooftops or smoke.

- **Vegetation and ground cover**  
  On such a rise, the soil is often thinner and drier at the top, supporting short grasses, low herbs, and scattered wildflowers more than large trees.  
  The scent near the ground would include dry grass, crushed leaves, and, where she digs, the richer, cooler smell of freshly opened earth, sometimes with an earthy, slightly metallic tang.

- **Weather and light near sunset**  
  Near sunset, the temperature drops quickly, especially on open ground; bare skin on hands and arms would feel the shift as the sun goes lower.  
  Light becomes slanted and warmer in color, casting long shadows that exaggerate the small rise and making the disturbed soil look darker against the paler undisturbed ground.

- **Soundscape of an open knoll**  
  Slight elevation alters sound: distant village noises (voices, animals, tools) may feel faint but distinct, while wind sound becomes more noticeable, rustling grasses and shrubs.  
  Bird calls (especially ground-feeders returning to roost) and insect noise typically rise and then gradually diminish as the sun sets.

- **Tactile sensations while burying**  
  As she digs and then refills the hole, the soil clumps break against her fingers; small stones scrape or nick the skin, and fine roots brush and catch at her nails.  
  When she presses the earth back, the top layer is looser and cooler than the surrounding ground, and her hands become coated with a thin paste of dust and sweat.

- **Copper and earth interaction (sensory)**  
  Copper warmed by her skin would feel cool at first touch, then quickly take on body heat; any patina lends it a slightly waxy or chalky texture in spots.  
  Once placed in the ground, if the soil is slightly moist, a faint metallic smell may mix with the smell of fresh earth where the pendant rested in her hand.

- **Sitting beside the burial until sundown**  
  After digging, her breathing and heartbeat would gradually slow as she sits; the body cools, and she may feel sweat drying stiffly where dust has settled on her arms and clothing.  
  As she remains still, small animals and insects that had retreated during her digging would begin to move again around the disturbed patch of soil.

- **Communal significance of named places**  
  Important local spots were often known by descriptive names (“the hill that rises,” “the old rise,” “the high place by the stream”) and held in collective memory over generations.  
  Even without a clear origin story, parents and elders would tell children not to disturb such places or would bring them there during certain rites or transitions.

- **Transmission of memory through objects**  
  In many ancient households, objects associated with a person—especially jewelry and small figurines—served as focal points for remembrance and could be passed down generations.  
  Burying such an object was a significant choice, effectively transferring the memory it carried from the household to the earth itself.

- **Burial depth and practicality**  
  In hand-dug ritual deposits, a common practical depth was roughly from the fingertips to the elbow (about 40–50 cm) or less, deep enough to hide and protect the object but achievable without tools.  
  At that depth, soil is noticeably cooler; in many climates, a thin layer of moisture can be felt compared to the dry upper crust.

- **Erosion and the eventual growth of the mound**  
  Over time, repeated small deposits, trampling the same spot, and the gradual collapse of filled holes can make a slight rise more pronounced.
