# Chapter 19 — write-time research

- **Piscataway ceramic pipe style (mid‑18th c.)**  
  Locally made Chesapeake/Piscataway pipes were hand‑formed from **local Middle Atlantic Coastal Plain clays**, often low‑fired earthenware rather than the very white, highly fired English ball clay used for imported pipes.[4][5]  
  Bowls were typically **shorter and thicker** than English long‑stem pipes, with thicker walls and a more bulbous “belly” to resist breakage.[1][4]  
  Surfaces could be smoothed by hand burnishing rather than glazing, leaving a slightly satin sheen and faint finger tracks.

- **Color and surface of the pipe bowl**  
  Use of **iron‑rich local clays** produced pipe bodies that fired to **buff, reddish, or chocolate brown** rather than pure white.[4]  
  Some Chesapeake‑style pipes exhibit **uneven firing clouds**—paler on one side, darker on another—where they sat in changing heat and smoke within the firing pit or kiln.[4]  
  After 280 years in the ground, the surface would likely show **root stains, mineral encrustation, and abrasion**, especially around the rim and heel, but decorative motifs in low relief could still be legible.[4]

- **Decorative motifs and symbolism**  
  Chesapeake region terra‑cotta pipes often have **molded or incised panel designs and star motifs**, sometimes called “star‑and‑panel” pipes.[4]  
  An **eight‑pointed star** on pipe bowls is archaeologically documented in the region and is interpreted as a hybrid design drawing on European, African, and Native aesthetics.[4]  
  Incised lines, punctate dots, and simple geometric bands around the bowl or stem reflect **creolized design vocabularies** shared among Native, African, and European craftspeople.[4]

- **Creolized object and social context**  
  Scholars describe locally made Chesapeake pipes as **“hybrid or creolized objects”** that emerged from intense interaction among white planters, enslaved Africans, and American Indians in the 17th–18th centuries.[4]  
  A Piscataway‑made pipe circulating in the 1740s could easily have passed through **multi‑ethnic hands**—Native farmers, free and enslaved Black laborers, and colonists—before ending in the mound.[4][5]

- **Tobacco and everyday use**  
  Tobacco pipes in the mid‑Atlantic were a **mobile personal possession** that moved with people from field to village to trading post.[5]  
  Bowl capacity and burn patterns on excavated pipes show that people often **smoked multiple short bowls a day**, refilling from a small twist of cured tobacco carried on their person.[5]  
  The interior of a recovered bowl typically shows **thick, glossy black carbonization**, evidence of repeated firing and long use before discard.[5]

- **Manufacture techniques for clay pipes**  
  Indigenous and local makers in the region often **hand‑modeled bowls and stems**, sometimes using simple molds for repeated decorative elements, and then joined bowl and stem with scoring and slip (liquid clay).[3][4]  
  Artisan pipe makers shaped the bowl as a small clay **sphere or pinch pot**, pierced it for the draft hole, then rolled a separate clay rod for the stem and attached it while leather‑hard.[3]  
  Before firing, makers had to **re‑clear the stem channel** repeatedly so it wouldn’t close as the clay shrank and warped during drying.[3]

- **Shape and feel in hand (artifact handling detail)**  
  An 18th‑c. earthenware bowl fragment would feel **denser and warmer** to the touch than modern porcelain, with a very fine but detectable grit from temper and inclusions.[4]  
  Where the stem joins the bowl, there is often a **slight ridge or seam line** from hand finishing or mold lines, which a careful modern observer could trace with a fingertip.[4]  
  The broken stem cross‑section would show **concentric firing colors**—darker core, lighter outer edge—revealing oxygen levels in firing.[4]

- **Comparing Piscataway/Chesapeake vs English pipes (visual detail)**  
  English ball clay pipes of the era tend to be **chalk‑white, thin‑walled, with long, slender stems**, whereas local Chesapeake/Piscataway pipes are **thicker, shorter, and colored buff to red‑brown**.[1][4][5]  
  English pipes typically used standardized bowl shapes that grew larger over time with changing tobacco prices, while local pipes show **more variety in bowl angle, profile, and ornamentation**.[1][4]

- **Archaeological “stratum” and context**  
  Archaeologists routinely interpret pipes as **chronological markers**, because bowl size and form changed over decades and can be dated comparatively.[2][5]  
  A pipe bowl at a particular depth within a mound would be used to **anchor the layer to a time range**, cross‑checked with other artifacts like ceramics or glass.[2][5]  
  Because pipes were personal, their presence is often read as evidence of **individual daily activity**—resting, talking, working—rather than just ceremonial deposit.[5]

- **Harvard pipe bowl dating example (for Tayak’s reference)**  
  A 17th‑c. English clay pipe bowl from Harvard Yard has been dated to **1650–1680** using a standard typology of bowl size and profile developed by archaeologist Ivor Noël Hume.[2]  
  This kind of typological chart is what a modern archaeologist like Tayak would mentally consult to confirm a mid‑18th‑c. date (e.g., 1740s) for his Piscataway bowl.[2]

- **Excavation and photography practice (present‑day fieldwork)**  
  Contemporary archaeologists typically **document small finds in situ** with scale bars, north arrows, and context tags before lifting them from the soil.  
  After recovery, a pipe bowl would be **bagged and labeled with provenience data** (site, unit, level, feature, date, initials), then brought to a lab for gentle cleaning and cataloging.  
  Field photography often uses a **macro lens or smartphone with a clip‑on macro**, ring light, and neutral background to reveal incised decoration and wear patterns clearly.

- **Digital communication of finds (2024 archaeologist)**  
  It is now standard for field archaeologists to **text or message preliminary artifact photos** to collaborators the same evening, especially for diagnostic pieces like pipes.  
  High‑resolution phone cameras can capture **fine incisions, firing clouds, and hairline cracks**, allowing off‑site specialists to comment almost immediately.  
  Archaeologists often keep a **shared digital database or cloud folder** where each significant artifact image is uploaded with its context number and initial interpretation.

- **Piscataway homeland geography (Maryland region)**  
  The Piscataway homeland spans the **western shore of the Chesapeake Bay and the Potomac River valley** in what is now southern Maryland.  
  Historic settlements were concentrated along **tidal rivers and creeks**, on elevated ground above marshes, with ready access to freshwater springs and arable upland fields.  
  By the 18th century, Piscataway people were navigating a landscape increasingly dominated by **tobacco plantations, mission sites, and colonial forts**, yet still maintaining village and kin networks along the Potomac.

- **Soundscape and environment near historic Piscataway sites**  
  Summer evenings along the lower Potomac and Chesapeake in Maryland feature **tree frogs, cicadas, and the distant slap of water against bulkheads or marsh grass**.  
  Depending on proximity to military installations and airports, contemporary sound layers also include **low helicopter thrum, distant highway noise, and occasional boat engines**, overlaid on the older river soundscape.  
  Light pollution from the DC–Baltimore corridor creates a **grey‑orange skyglow**, but stars remain visible low over darker stretches of river and forest.

- **Contemporary Piscataway communities and identity**  
  Piscataway people today maintain **tribal organizations, cultural programs, and public educational work** focused on continuity of community in their ancestral lands.  
  There is active emphasis on **oral history, family genealogies, and reclaiming archival mentions** of ancestors from colonial records, church books, and court documents.  
  Ceremonies, language reclamation efforts, and school presentations often highlight that Piscataway history did **not** end in the 17th or 18th centuries but runs directly into the present.

- **Oral history transmission (structure and language)**  
  Ethnographers in the region describe oral history as often passed **“from grandmother to grandchild”** in multi‑generational households, with repeated retellings that fix certain phrases and names.  
  Family tradition typically anchors a story with **a named ancestor, a place, and an event** (“when your great‑great‑grandfather was at…”) to maintain the chain of transmission.  
  Some Piscataway and neighboring Native families emphasize the **responsibility of the listener**—the one who “carries the story”—as a specific, almost formal role in the kin network.
