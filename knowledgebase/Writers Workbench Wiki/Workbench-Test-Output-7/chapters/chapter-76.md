The generator had been running for three hours and the shelter smelled of diesel and cold canvas. Tayak sat at the field table with her laptop open and a mug of coffee going cold at her right elbow, the screen the only real light in the space. Outside, the mound was a darkness against a darker sky. She knew it was there the way you know a thing you have looked at for eleven months — not by seeing it but by the weight it puts on the air around you.

The official template was on screen. She had opened it at ten-thirty, looked at it for twenty minutes, and had not typed a single character.

*SITE DESIGNATION: 18CH-447*

*REPORT TYPE: Final Stratigraphy and Osteological Assessment*

*PRINCIPAL INVESTIGATOR:*

The cursor blinked in the field after her title. Patient. Indifferent.

She knew what went there. She had written versions of this document before — the Patuxent lowlands report, the Phase II at Nanjemoy, the interim assessment she had filed with the state eight weeks ago. She knew the rhythm of it the way a carpenter knows the grain of a board: *provenience, association, context, chain of custody.* She knew how to write a sentence that carried enormous weight while appearing to carry none. *The osteological material recovered from Stratum Seven is consistent with a pre-contact depositional sequence dating to approximately 100 BC, with radiocarbon results pending confirmation from Beta Analytic.* She could write that sentence. She could write forty pages of sentences like it and every one of them would be true.

And every one of them would be a kind of burial.

She reached for the coffee, found it cold, and set it back down. The generator coughed once and steadied. Somewhere out on the field road, Dawes was doing his evening rounds — she had heard the truck twenty minutes ago, the slow crunch of gravel, the engine idling, then moving on. The site was quiet now. Wren had gone home at six. Delia and Osei had left before that. The mound and the trench and the shelter were hers alone.

She looked at the template.

The clean report would protect the evidence. That was not a small thing. Slade's office was watching; Voss was watching; and somewhere behind both of them, Garrett Pruitt's lawyers were watching, waiting for a procedural crack they could push a bulldozer through. A report with a personal claim of direct ancestry was a report with a target on it. She could hear the language already — *conflict of interest, compromised objectivity, investigator bias* — the words that would let them dismiss not just her but the pendant, the gorget, the document, the whole eleven months of careful work. Cecile Marant had said it plainly enough, years ago in a Patuxent field shelter not unlike this one: *Your feelings are not data, Tayak.* She had said it without cruelty. It was simply the rule.

But Cecile had also said: *The ground tells you the truth. Institutions tell you what they've decided.*

Tayak closed the template. Not dramatically — she just clicked the X, and when the dialog asked if she wanted to save changes she clicked *Don't Save,* because there were no changes. There was nothing there to save.

She opened a new document.

The blank page had a quality the template did not. The template already knew what it was. The blank page was waiting to find out.

She typed:

*Dr. Tayak Moyaone*

*Piscataway Conoy Tribe of Maryland*

*Personal Statement of Conflict and Connection*

*Site 18CH-447, Charles County, Maryland*

*November 2024*

She stopped.

Her hands were flat on the table on either side of the keyboard, the way her grandmother had taught her to press them flat on the earth at the mound — *you say the name, you press your hands down, and you don't lift them until you've finished saying it.* She had been nine years old and she had not understood what finishing meant. She understood it now.

The header sat on the screen. Five lines. Her name, her people, and a description of what she was about to do. The cursor blinked at the beginning of the sixth line, waiting.

She did not type the sixth line yet.

What she was about to write would be read by Slade, who was methodical and not cruel and would look for any reason the designation could be challenged. It would be read by Voss, who had spent the last three weeks trying to position himself on the right side of this and would find the personal statement either useful or alarming depending on which way the wind blew when he opened the PDF. It would be read by Marcus, who had asked her twice in the last month whether she was certain about the scope of what she was filing. It would be read by the OFA, whose criteria for federal recognition required documented continuous presence and who did not, as a rule, accept the word of the person with the most to gain.

It would be read by Nora.

That was the one she kept coming back to. Nora, who had carried the name of the woman in the deepest layer for sixty-two years without saying it on the phone — who had told Tayak plainly that some things required you to be standing in the right place first, and that the right place was not a phone call. Who had said *the record is not the thing, the thing is the thing, but the record is how the thing survives you* — words Tayak had first heard from Nora's own mouth, understanding only later that they had traveled the same chain as the names, fire to fire, deathbed to deathbed. Who had made Tayak wait, had made her go down through six strata and two thousand years before she would say the name. Nora had known, all along, what was at the bottom. She had made Tayak find it herself.

The cursor blinked.

Outside, the mound was still there in the dark.

Tayak thought about the clean report. She thought about it the way you think about a door you have decided not to open — not with longing, exactly, but with a clear understanding of what was on the other side of it. Safety. Credibility. The evidence preserved and the investigator invisible, which was the bargain the discipline had always offered: *give us your name and we will give you objectivity.* She had taken that bargain before. She had been good at it. The Patuxent report, the Nanjemoy assessment — her name on the cover page and her self nowhere in the text, which was how it was supposed to work.

The woman in Stratum Seven had been in the ground for two thousand years. She had no name in any state database. She had no accession number, no provenience tag, no chain of custody before Tayak's trowel found the edge of the burial. She had only the chain of names that Nora had carried — story to story, fire to fire, deathbed to deathbed — back through Ida, back through Lena, back through Saya and Okwi and Tessa and Matta and Waya, all the way to a woman who had pressed her hands flat on a small rise above the river terrace and said a name into the earth and started something that had lasted two thousand years.

And Tayak was at the end of that chain. Not as an investigator. As a descendant.

She could write the clean report and protect the evidence and erase herself from the story, and the evidence would go into the record and the record would go into the case and the case would go before the commission and maybe they would rule in the tribe's favor and maybe they would not, and either way the woman in Stratum Seven would remain *consistent with* and *associated with* and *concordant with* — a body of evidence, not a person. Not someone's ancestor. Not *her* ancestor.

Or she could write the full truth.

She had not decided, yet, which one she was going to do. She knew which one she was going to do — she had known it since she closed the template — but she had not admitted it to herself in so many words, because once she admitted it there was no going back and the header on the screen was already five lines she could not unsay.

*Dr. Tayak Moyaone. Piscataway Conoy Tribe of Maryland. Personal Statement of Conflict and Connection.*

The cursor blinked.

She left her hands flat on the table and looked at the screen and did not move. The generator ran. The canvas shelter shifted in a small wind off the creek. The coffee was cold. The mound was outside in the dark, forty feet from where she sat, and two thousand years below the surface of it was a woman whose name Nora knew and had not yet said aloud to her — would not say until Tayak was standing in the right place, until the right proceeding — and Tayak was the person who had found her.

She picked up her hands and put them on the keyboard.

And then she stopped again, because she had just understood something she had not understood before, and it was this: the report she was about to write was not a scientific document and it was not a legal document and it was not, exactly, a personal statement. It was the next link in the chain. It was the thing she would hand forward. Saya had handed Lena the petition. Nora had carried the name. And Tayak was going to hand someone — the commission, the court, the record, whoever came after — the truth of what she had found and who she was and what that meant, written down in one sitting without stopping, because that was what the chain required.

She had not known, until this moment, that she was inside the chain. She had thought she was studying it.

Her hands were on the keyboard. The cursor blinked at the beginning of the sixth line.

She did not start writing yet. She sat in the diesel-smelling dark with the mound outside and her name on the screen and the whole weight of what she was about to do pressing down on her hands, and she breathed in once, and out, and then she thought: *I don't know how to start.*

Which was, she realized, exactly what every person in the chain must have thought, at the moment before they started.

She started with the clean version.

Not as a draft. As a test. She told herself it was a test — that she needed to see what the sanitized report looked like before she could decide whether to abandon it — and she opened a new document and typed the header in the standard format she had used for eleven years, the format Marant had made her memorize in her second field year until she could produce it without thinking: site designation, project title, principal investigator, institutional affiliation, date of submission, report classification.

*Site 18CH-447 — Stratum Seven Excavation Summary and Osteological Context Report*
*Principal Investigator: Dr. Tayak Moyaone*
*University of Maryland, Department of Anthropology*
*Submitted: [date]*
*Classification: Active Case — Pending OFA Review*

The header looked right. It looked the way a report was supposed to look — authoritative, neutral, the kind of document that moved through institutional channels without snagging on anything. She had written forty-seven reports in this format. She could write this one in her sleep.

She started with stratigraphy.

*Stratum Seven represents the deepest confirmed cultural deposit at Site 18CH-447, encountered at a depth of 2.34 meters below surface datum in grid square C-4. The deposit is characterized by a compact, dark-stained matrix with elevated organic content, consistent with prepared burial fill. The stratum is sealed below an undisturbed subsoil layer and above a discrete lens of river-washed gravel interpreted as a deliberate founding deposit.*

She typed without stopping. The words came easily — they always came easily in this register, which was part of the problem with it. The discipline had given her a language that moved smoothly over everything, that described the most charged material in the most frictionless terms, and she was fluent in it the way you are fluent in a language you learned before you understood what fluency costs.

*Radiocarbon analysis of associated charcoal samples (Lab Accession Nos. UMD-2024-1187 through UMD-2024-1191) yielded calibrated date ranges of 120 BC–60 BC (2σ), consistent with a late pre-contact depositional event. Bone collagen from the primary interment (Specimen 18CH-447-088) returned a date of 95 BC–45 BC (2σ), placing the burial within the same depositional phase.*

She paused and read the two paragraphs back. They were correct. Every number was right. She had the lab reports open in a second window — Adaeze had sent the final results three days ago, and Tayak had read them four times, each time with the same feeling of the ground shifting under her — and she had copied the accession numbers from the chain-of-custody log that she kept in the field notebook with the weathered cover and the ruled lines separating observation from inference, the way Marant had taught her.

*Your feelings are not data, Tayak.*

She typed the artifact section.

*Artifact 18CH-447-051 (copper pendant, annular form, 4.2 cm diameter) was recovered from the primary burial context in direct association with the interment. The pendant's composition and manufacture technique are consistent with Great Lakes copper-working traditions of the first century BC, suggesting long-distance exchange networks active in the Chesapeake region during the late pre-contact period. The pendant was recovered face-up, suggesting intentional placement rather than incidental inclusion.*

She stopped.

*Intentional placement rather than incidental inclusion.*

She looked at that phrase for a long time. It was accurate. It was the correct archaeological inference from the orientation data. And it was also the most thoroughly emptied sentence she had ever written, because what she knew — what the chain of names told her, what Nora had carried for sixty-two years, what Waya and Kaya and Tessa and Matta and Saya and Lena and Ida had each held and handed forward — was that the pendant had been placed by a woman who had buried her daughter and had no other place to put the grief, and that the placement had been intentional in a way that the word *intentional* could not begin to hold.

But she kept going. She wrote the osteological section.

*Osteological analysis of Specimen 18CH-447-088 indicates an adult female of mature age, based on pelvic morphology and cranial suture fusion. Bone preservation is moderate, with significant fragmentation in the post-cranial elements. Dental wear patterns are consistent with a diet of ground grain and fibrous plant material. No perimortem trauma is evident. The interment is supine, extended, with the head oriented south-southwest toward the high ground above Mattawoman Creek.*

She wrote the chain-of-custody section, which was the driest section and the one she could write fastest, because it was pure documentation: who had touched the specimen and when, what containers had been used, what the transfer records showed, Adaeze's initials at 15:05 on the day of recovery, the acid-free archival bag, the Tyvek tag with the accession number in permanent marker, the metric scale in the photograph, the GPS coordinates logged to four decimal places.

She wrote the contextual summary, which connected the burial to the broader site chronology, situating Stratum Seven within the sequence she had spent eleven months documenting, from the glass bead in Stratum One down through the colonial document and the ceramic pipe bowl and the shell gorgets and the copper pendant's secondary context and the antler figure, each layer a chapter in a story that the report described as *a multi-component site with evidence of continuous cultural use spanning approximately two millennia.*

*Continuous cultural use.*

She had used that phrase in the Patuxent report and the Nanjemoy assessment and the Section 106 consultation she had filed in March, and it had always felt like the right phrase — precise, defensible, exactly what the evidence showed — and now it felt like a translation of something into a language that could not carry the original meaning. The mound was not a *multi-component site with evidence of continuous cultural use.* The mound was a place where her family had been burying their dead and saying their names for two thousand years, and the chain of those names ran from Nora back through Ida back through Lena back through Saya and Okwi and Tessa and Matta and Waya all the way to a woman whose name Nora knew and would not say on the phone — who had told Tayak plainly that the right place for that name was not a telephone line but the ground itself, or the right proceeding, face to face — and Tayak was at the end of that chain, and the report she was writing called that woman *the primary interment* and called the pendant *Artifact 18CH-447-051* and called the act of burial *an intentional depositional event* and called the chain of names nothing, because the chain of names was not in any category the report recognized.

She kept writing.

She wrote the section on historical concordance, which was where she had to be most careful, because this was where the report came closest to saying what she knew. She used the phrase *historical and genealogical concordance* four times. She cited the 1697 petition and the Calvert patent boundary description and Nora's affidavit — she cited the affidavit as a primary source document without describing its contents, because the affidavit's contents were Nora's to disclose, not hers. She cited the oral tradition record as *supplementary evidence consistent with the material findings* and she noted that the oral tradition *identifies a founding burial at the site's deepest layer associated with a copper pendant* and that this identification *is concordant with the archaeological evidence recovered in Stratum Seven.*

*Concordant.*

She had looked up the etymology of that word once, years ago, in a usage argument with a colleague. It came from the Latin for *hearts together.* The discipline had taken a word that meant hearts together and used it to mean *these two things match without us having to say why.*

She wrote the concluding section.

*The evidence recovered from Stratum Seven at Site 18CH-447 is consistent with the site's identification as a Piscataway ancestral burial ground with continuous use from approximately 100 BC to the late colonial period. The material evidence, radiocarbon chronology, and documentary record are mutually reinforcing and support the site's significance under NHPA Section 106 and NAGPRA provisions. The osteological evidence is consistent with a direct ancestral relationship to contemporary Piscataway Conoy people, as represented by the affiliated tribal authority.*

She stopped typing.

*Consistent with a direct ancestral relationship.*

She read the sentence again. Then she read the whole report from the beginning — the header, the stratigraphy, the radiocarbon dates, the artifact typology, the osteological analysis, the chain of custody, the historical concordance, the conclusion — and she read it the way Slade would read it, and the way Voss would read it, and the way the OFA reviewer would read it, and the way a judge would read it if it came to that.

It was flawless.

Every number was right. Every inference was supported. Every claim was hedged to exactly the degree the evidence required and no further. There was nothing in it that Slade could attack on methodological grounds. There was nothing in it that Voss could use to argue bias. There was nothing in it that a hostile reviewer could call speculative or personal or insufficiently rigorous. It would survive peer review. It would survive a FOIA request. It would survive whatever Pruitt's lawyers threw at it.

It would also give them nothing.

Not nothing — it would give them the evidence, properly documented, properly contextualised, properly sourced. It would give them Stratum Seven and the radiocarbon dates and the pendant and the osteological analysis. It would give them *concordance* and *consistency* and *association.* It would give them a case.

What it would not give them was Nora.

It would not give them the sixty-two years Nora had carried the name of the woman in the ground. It would not give them Ida, who had received the name in a kitchen smelling of woodsmoke and old coffee with her hands flat on the table. It would not give them Lena, who had inherited the petition and the name-list and the incomplete eastern boundary from Saya and had spent her life trying to close the gap. It would not give them Saya pressing her inked palm to the colonial assembly's record, or Okwi burying the oilskin-wrapped copies in the mound before his death, or Tessa standing alone on the mound's face after her brother walked past her on the road without stopping. It would not give them Matta selling pipe bowls to Hendricks for eleven years to buy the cover that let him keep what the cover was supposed to replace. It would not give them Waya wearing the pendant for twenty years and then giving it to Kaya with the names attached, because the names were the point, not the object.

It would not give them the chain.

And without the chain, the woman in Stratum Seven was *consistent with* and *associated with* and *concordant with* — a body of evidence, correctly catalogued, properly curated, available for study by qualified researchers upon written request to the affiliated institution.

Not someone's ancestor. Not Nora's ancestor. Not Tayak's ancestor.

Not the woman who had pressed her hands flat on a small rise above the river terrace two thousand years ago and said a name into the earth and started something that had lasted until tonight, when Tayak sat in a canvas shelter forty feet from the mound with the generator running and the coffee cold and the laptop screen throwing pale light on her face, reading back a report that described that woman's burial with technical precision and complete, institutional silence about what it meant.

The generator hummed in the corner of the shelter. Outside, a car passed on the road — Dawes doing his evening rounds, probably, the truck's headlights sweeping briefly across the canvas. The creek made its small sound in the dark.

*Section I: Stratigraphic Context*

The layer depths came back to her without effort — she had read them so many times that the numbers had the quality of a practised recitation, like the chain of names. Stratum Seven at minus one point four meters below datum. Compact, undisturbed matrix, reddish-brown silt-loam with charcoal inclusions at the eastern margin. No intrusion from above. The profile wall had been clean, the layers distinct, the kind of stratigraphy that made you slow down and look twice because it was too clear, because ground that old and that undisturbed made you feel the weight of what you were about to do to it.

Burial position — she checked the photograph on her phone, the one she had taken before Wren had photographed it officially, the one she had taken because she had needed to look at it alone first — supine, head oriented south toward the high ground, arms at the sides, hands open.

*Hands open.* She had written that in her field notes and then crossed it out because it was not a measurable observation. Now she wrote it back in and left it.

*Section II: Contextual and Relational Disclosure*

She typed the heading and then she sat for a moment with her hands in her lap, looking at it.

Slade would read this. Voss would read this. The Trust's compliance office would read this. The OFA would read this. Every person who wanted to dismiss the excavation's findings would read this section and find in it the thing they had been waiting for: the lead archaeologist's admission that she had a personal stake in the outcome. They would call it bias. They would say the entire eleven-month record was contaminated. They would say the chain of custody was compromised not by anything Tayak had done to the artifacts but by what she had known while she was doing it.

She knew all of that. She had been turning it over since the moment she had understood, standing in the trench with the pendant in her gloved hand, what she was holding and whose it had been.

She began to type.

*The following disclosure is made voluntarily, without institutional instruction, and is not required by the applicable reporting protocols. It is made because the author of this report has determined that omitting it would constitute a material misrepresentation of the evidentiary record — not of the physical evidence, which is documented in Section I with full methodological transparency, but of the relational context in which that evidence was recovered. The author has determined that a report which accurately describes the stratigraphy and osteology while concealing the author's knowledge of the oral record associated with the burial would reproduce, in technical language, the same erasure that the colonial documentary record performed for three hundred years: the separation of physical remains from the living chain of people and knowledge to which those remains belong.*

She read that paragraph back. It was too long. She left it.

*The oral tradition held by Nora Moyaone, age eighty-two, of Billingsley Road, Charles County, Maryland, connects the burials documented in Site 18CH-447 — and specifically the primary burial in Stratum Seven — to a direct ancestral line extending to the present day. She stopped. Her hands were steady on the keys. She was surprised by that.

*The decision not to halt the excavation was made on the following grounds: the excavation permit was under active challenge by the state planning office; a halt would have triggered the suspension clause in the permit conditions, surrendering site control to the state and ending the excavation permanently; the physical evidence — the pendant, the osteological assemblage, the stratigraphic record — would have been unavailable for documentation, and the site would have been designated under the Colonial Landmark framework, which would have foreclosed any future tribal claim to the ground. She was aware that this was an argument and that arguments could be challenged. She left it in.

*The oral chain held by Nora Moyaone traces the following line of transmission, as the author understands it from direct conversation with Nora Moyaone and from the affidavit prepared by Nora Moyaone in 1962 (copy attached as Appendix D):*

She typed the names she knew. Nora. Then she wrote the sentence she had been building toward since she had opened the new document.

*The author of this report is a direct descendant of the individual interred in Stratum Seven of Site 18CH-447.*

She read it back. Twelve words. She had spent eleven months in the ground to earn the right to write them. She left them exactly as they were.

*This connection was not known to the author at the commencement of the excavation. It became known to the author during the excavation, through the oral chain held by Nora Moyaone. The author discloses it now, in full, because concealment would be the same act the colonial record performed when it described the high ground adjacent to Mattawoman Creek as a boundary marker in a land patent without naming the people whose dead were buried there. The colonial record had the physical location. It did not have the names. This report has both. The author declines to omit one in order to protect the other.*

The generator made its sound. The creek made its sound. Somewhere beyond the canvas, the mound sat in the dark, forty feet away, its eastern face open to the night air, the profile wall covered with a plastic sheet weighted at the corners with sandbags.

She was typing fast now, the way she had written her dissertation chapters in the last week before the deadline, when the argument had finally clarified and there was nothing left to do but get it down before it shifted. Her shoulders still ached. She did not stop.

She wrote about the chain of custody — the pendant's journey from the conservation suite log to Adaeze's bench, the specimen bags and Tyvek tags and the metric scale in the photographs, the chain-of-custody entry signed by Wren at fifteen-oh-five — and she wrote that the physical chain of custody was unimpeachable and that the relational chain of custody, the oral one, was equally unimpeachable, and that the two chains, the institutional and the human, had converged in the trench this afternoon for the first time in two thousand years, and that this report was the record of that convergence.

She wrote: *The author anticipates that this disclosure will be used to challenge the report's objectivity. The author notes that the alternative — a report that documents the physical evidence while concealing the author's knowledge of its human significance — would be a document that achieves the appearance of objectivity by performing the same act of erasure that the colonial record performed. The author does not consider that a defensible form of objectivity.*

She wrote a final paragraph in Section II that she had not planned.

*Nora Moyaone has carried the name of the woman in Stratum Seven for sixty-two years. She received it from Ida, who received it in a kitchen on a winter morning, hands flat on a table. The name has not been written down. It has been held in a living person's memory, passed from keeper to keeper, through every generation in which the colonial record insisted there was no one left to hold it. The name will be disclosed to the tribal authority and to the affiliated institution in a separate proceeding, at a time and in a manner determined by Nora Moyaone. It does not belong in a field report. It belongs to the person who has carried it. This report acknowledges its existence and its authority without presuming to contain it.*

She stopped typing.

The clock in the corner of the screen said eleven twenty-three. She had been writing for ninety-one minutes since she had opened the new document.

She did not read Section II back. She had made herself a rule at the beginning: write it without stopping and do not reread it before you send it, because if you reread it you will start to hear Slade's voice reading it, and Voss's voice, and the Trust compliance officer's voice, and you will begin to sand it down, and what you will produce in the end will be a document that protects you without saying anything true.

She went to the beginning of the document and read the title one more time.

*Site 18CH-447 — Stratum Seven Excavation Report and Contextual and Relational Disclosure*

She added her name to the author line: *Dr. Tayak Moyaone, Principal Investigator, Site 18CH-447.*

She added the date: *November 14, 2024.*

She saved the document. The filename was *Stratum7_Report_FINAL.docx.* She did not use a version number. There would not be another version.

She attached it to an email. The recipient line had three addresses: the Trust compliance office, the tribal council's general inbox, and Marcus's direct address, because Marcus would need to read it before anyone else did and she did not want him to find out from the compliance office.

She wrote the subject line: *18CH-447 Stratum Seven Report — Final — Please Read in Full.*

In the body of the email she wrote four sentences: *Please find attached the final excavation report for Stratum Seven of Site 18CH-447. The report contains a second section, clearly headed, that discloses a personal connection to the site that I became aware of during the excavation. I am disclosing it now because not disclosing it would be wrong. I am available by phone.*

She read the four sentences back. They were enough.

She moved the cursor to the Send button. Her hand was steady. She thought, briefly, of the woman in the ground forty feet away — hands open, head toward the high ground, the pendant worn smooth at the cord-hole from decades of use before it was laid beside her — and she thought of Nora on Billingsley Road with the name she had carried for sixty-two years and would not say on the phone, and she thought of Marant saying *your feelings are not data, Tayak,* and she thought: no. But they are evidence. They are the evidence that the data cannot carry by itself. They are the part that survives when the institution loses the file.

She clicked Send.

The email disappeared from the outbox. The screen was quiet. The generator hummed. The creek made its small sound in the dark outside the canvas.

She sat for a moment with her hands in her lap, looking at the empty outbox, and she felt something she did not have a technical term for — not relief exactly, not fear exactly, but the particular sensation of having done something that could not be undone, which was also the sensation of having finally done something real.

Then she thought: *Slade will read this in the morning.*

The cursor blinked in the empty outbox.

She had not moved her hands from her lap. The laptop screen was the only light in the shelter — the overhead fluorescent she had switched off an hour ago because it made the words look clinical, made everything look like a form to be processed — and in the screen's glow her hands looked pale against her field trousers, which were still dirty at the knees from the trench.

She thought about the recipient line.

Three addresses. She had been certain about three addresses when she typed them. Now she sat with the sent email gone into the dark and she thought: *Dana Osei.*

The Washington Post reporter had texted Kimi twice and called once. Tayak had told Kimi not to respond yet. That had been the right call then. It might still be the right call. But Slade would read the report in the morning and forward it up the chain within the hour, because that was what methodical people did, and the chain above Slade included people who had already decided what Site 18CH-447 was worth, and those people had lawyers, and the lawyers had a thirty-day clock, and twenty-two of those days were already gone.

She pulled the laptop toward her and opened a new email.

The address was in her contacts because Kimi had forwarded the original text exchange: *dosei@washpost.com.*

She sat with the cursor in the To field and the body of the email blank and she thought about what Nora had said — the words Tayak had heard from Nora's own mouth and had understood only later were older than Nora, traveling the same chain as the names: *The record is not the thing. The thing is the thing. But the record is how the thing survives you.*

She typed: *Mr. Osei —*

She stopped. She deleted it. She typed: *Dana —*

She stopped again. She did not know him. She typed: *Mr. Osei —* again and left it.

*My name is Dr. Tayak Moyaone. I am the principal investigator at Site 18CH-447 in Charles County, Maryland. I have this evening submitted a final excavation report for the site's deepest stratum to the Trust compliance office and the Piscataway tribal council. The report contains a disclosure of personal connection to the site that I expect will be used to challenge its findings. I am writing to you because I want the report to exist in a place that is not a compliance inbox before that challenge begins. I am attaching it here.*

She attached Stratum7_Report_FINAL.docx.

*I am not asking you to publish anything tonight. I am asking you to read it. If you have questions, I will answer them. My phone number is below.*

She read it back. It was not elegant. It was not the kind of prose that looked good in a newspaper. It was the kind of prose that meant what it said, which was the only kind she had left in her at eleven thirty-four on a Thursday night with the creek running outside and the generator burning through its last quarter-tank of diesel.

She typed her phone number. She sent the email.

Then she sat back and looked at the canvas ceiling of the shelter, which was stained at the north seam where rain had found a gap in the summer, a brown tide-mark the shape of nothing in particular, and she thought: *four addresses.*

The generator coughed once and steadied.

She thought of Nora's kitchen — the smell of it, old woodsmoke and something sweet she had never identified, the oval photograph on the wall of the woman in the dark dress with her hair pulled back, the grandmother who had never discussed the mound, who had taken Tayak to the Mattawoman creek bottom to pick watercress and taught her to leave the roots so the plant would return, and had never named the lesson, had only done it and let Tayak watch. She thought of Nora saying *I will tell you the name when you are standing in the right place.* She thought of the trench this afternoon, the trowel-scrape and the smell of iron-rich subsoil and the pendant lying face-up in the earth, the cord-hole worn smooth.

She was standing in the right place now. She had been standing in it since four-thirty this morning.

She picked up her phone. It was eleven thirty-seven. Too late to call. She opened a text to Nora and typed: *The report is sent. Four places. I didn't reread it.* She thought about adding something else and did not. She sent it.

The read receipt appeared after forty seconds. Nora was awake. Of course she was awake. She had been awake for sixty-two years waiting for this particular night.

Three dots appeared. Tayak watched them.

The dots stopped. No message came.

Then, after a long moment, a single word: *Good.*

Tayak set the phone face-down on the field table beside the specimen tray — the tray still held the Tyvek evidence tags from this afternoon's documentation, each one filled in with permanent marker in her own handwriting, artifact numbers in sequence, the chain of custody unbroken from the ground to the conservation suite to the report to the four inboxes now holding the report in the dark — and she looked at the laptop screen, which had gone to its screensaver, a slow rotation of field photographs she had set up in the first week of the excavation and had not changed since.

The current photograph was from stratum three. The shell gorget, in situ, with the metric scale beside it and her own gloved hand at the frame's edge for context. The photograph was eleven months old. The gorget was older than the colonial record of this county. The mound was older than the gorget. The woman at the bottom of it was older than the mound as anyone had understood it, and her name was older than any document Tayak had ever held, and it was sitting tonight in Nora Moyaone's memory on Billingsley Road, intact, waiting for the right proceeding.

Tayak had not put the name in the report. She had said the name existed and that it was held by a living person and that it would be disclosed at a time determined by that person. That was the right call. She was almost certain it was the right call.

She was less certain about Dana Osei. She would know in the morning whether that had been the right call.

She closed the laptop. The shelter went dark.

She sat for a moment in the dark with the sound of the creek and the generator and the particular silence of a site at night, which was different from any other silence she knew — not empty but accumulated, forty feet of accumulated time pressing gently upward through the clay, through the stratigraphy, through the careful grid of excavated squares, through the tarp she had weighted down over the open trench before she came inside. The woman in stratum seven had been in that ground for two thousand years. She would be there in the morning.

Tayak pulled off her field boots without untying them — a habit Marant had broken her of and that she had resumed the moment Marant was no longer watching — and she lay down on the cot in her field clothes and looked at the dark canvas ceiling and thought: *Slade will read it at eight. Voss will read it by nine. Marcus will have read it by six because Marcus reads everything by six.*

She thought: *the tribal council will read it before the hearing.*

She thought: *Dana Osei might read it tonight.*

She thought: *Nora already knows what it says.*

Outside, the creek ran over its stones in the dark, carrying whatever the ground gave it, the way it had always done, the way it would do tomorrow when Slade opened his email and read the subject line and felt, she imagined, the particular sensation of a document that could not be unfiled.

She closed her eyes.

She did not sleep for a long time, but she was still.

The generator cut out at midnight.

She heard it go — the cough, the sputter, the sudden weight of silence — and lay still for three seconds waiting for it to restart. It did not restart. The shelter was fully dark now, the laptop screen cold, the specimen tray invisible on the field table two feet from her head.

She sat up. Put her feet on the floor. The linoleum was cold through her socks.

She found her field boots by feel and pulled them on without lacing them, the way Marant had spent a season trying to cure her of, and pushed open the shelter door.

The air outside was colder than she expected. Late November cold, the kind that had no ambiguity in it. The sky above the tree line was very clear and very full of stars, the Milky Way a pale smear from southeast to northwest, and the site was dark in every direction — the access road, the equipment shed, the weighted tarp over the open trench, the creek she could hear but not see running over its stones thirty yards east. No generator hum. No light from the shelter. Just the site as it had been before she arrived eleven months ago, before the permit, before the grid stakes, before the first trowel-scrape.

She stood on the shelter steps and let her eyes adjust.

The mound was a low shape against the darker tree line, the way it always was at night — not dramatic, not imposing, just a rise in the ground, a place where the earth had decided to be slightly more than itself. She had walked its perimeter so many times in eleven months that she could have done it blind. She had done it blind, more or less, on the mornings she arrived before first light and went straight to the trench without a headlamp because she needed to think without seeing.

She went down the steps and walked toward the trench.

The tarp was still weighted at its corners with the sandbags she had placed before going inside. She crouched and lifted the near edge and looked down into the dark. She could see nothing. She could smell it — the iron-rich subsoil, the particular mineral cold of a deep excavation, the faint organic note that she had learned to associate with the lowest stratum, the one that had taken eleven months to reach and two hours to document and one sitting to report. The smell was the same smell that had been rising from that ground for two thousand years, through every season, through every government that had claimed this county, through every survey and patent and partition and sale and court order and suspension notice. The ground had kept it all and given back only this: a smell, a pendant, a cord-hole worn smooth, a name held in one woman's memory on Billingsley Road.

She let the tarp down.

She stood up. The grass under her boots was stiff with cold. She walked to the mound's southern face — not the excavated section, not the grid, but the undisturbed face where the turf was intact and had been intact since before anyone in this county had thought to record what they were doing to the land around it.

She crouched. Then she knelt. The cold came through her field pants immediately, damp and direct.

She pressed both palms flat to the earth.

The grass was cold and slightly wet and the soil beneath it gave slightly under her weight, the way soil does when it is alive — when there are roots in it and organisms in it and two thousand years of accumulated decision pressing upward from below. She could feel the give of it through her palms, through the calluses she had built up over eleven months of trowel work, through the skin that had held every artifact in this site before the Tyvek tags and the specimen trays and the conservation suite took them over.

She did not say the name.

Nora had not said it on the phone. She had told Tayak plainly: the name belonged to the right place, the right proceeding, face to face. Tayak had written in the report that the name would be disclosed at a time and in a manner determined by Nora Moyaone. She had meant it when she wrote it. She meant it now.

But she could press her hands flat. She could do that much.

She stayed like that — palms against the cold turf, the give of the earth beneath them, the dark above and the dark below and the creek running somewhere to her right. She did not count the time.

When she stood, her knees were wet and her palms were cold and there was soil on the heel of her right hand where she had pressed harder than she meant to. She looked at it in the dark and could not see it but she could feel it — the specific grit of Charles County subsoil, the same material she had been cataloguing for eleven months, the same material that had held the pendant and the gorget and the pipe bowl and the glass bead and the document and the body of the woman whose name she had not said.

She wiped her hand on her field pants.

She stood on the ground above the deepest layer and looked at the sky for a moment. The stars were indifferent, which was the right thing for stars to be. The creek ran over its stones. An owl called once from the tree line and did not call again.

She thought: *Slade will open his email at eight.*

She thought: *I don't know what happens after that.*

That was true. It was the truest thing she had thought all night, truer than the report, truer than the four addresses, truer than the careful language she had used and the careful language she had chosen not to use. She did not know what happened after eight o'clock tomorrow morning. She had spent eleven months trying to control what happened next, and she had sent a report tonight that gave up that control entirely, that handed the next move to four people who would each read the same document and reach four different conclusions about what it meant and what it required them to do.

She was not afraid of that. She was something else — not calm exactly, but settled, the way the ground was settled, the way the mound had been settled for two thousand years under every weather that had come at it.

She walked back to the shelter. She pulled the door shut behind her. She lay down on the cot in the dark and laced her boots properly, both of them, in the dark, by feel, because Marant had been right about that even if she had not listened, and then she lay back and looked at the dark canvas ceiling and listened to the creek.

In the morning, Slade would read it.
