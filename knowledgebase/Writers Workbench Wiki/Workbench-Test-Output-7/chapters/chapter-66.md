The designation packet had been sitting on the right side of his desk since two-fifteen, and it was now almost six.

Slade knew the exact time because the building had gone quiet in that particular way—the HVAC cycling down a register, the rolling chairs and hallway voices gone, the overhead panels still burning their cool white light over a floor that no longer needed them. He had not moved the packet. He had not opened it. He had pulled it from the manila folder once, noted the blue ink on the signature line at the bottom of page thirty-one, and put it back.

The top sheet read *Colonial Landmark Designation — Site 18CH-447, Charles County, Maryland*. Beneath the title, in the smaller font the template used for descriptive subheadings: *Early colonial period settlement, first European proprietors, pioneer families of the lower Patuxent corridor.*

He had written that language himself, eight months ago, before the spring survey. Before he had read the Calvert patent. Before he had gone back to the records room and traced the 1683 Dent instrument through seven transfers to the deed in Liber 27, page 314, where his great-grandfather's name sat in faded type next to a purchase price of eight hundred dollars.

The sentence about pioneer families was still there on page one. He had not revised it.

His phone was face-down beside the keyboard. When it buzzed and turned itself over, the contact photo that came up was three years old—Elena at the bay, sunburned, laughing at something off-camera, her hair pulled sideways by the wind. He looked at the photo for one full ring.

He picked up.

"Hey, Dad."

Two words, and he already knew. Not from the words themselves but from the deliberate steadiness under them—the same steadiness she'd had at sixteen when she came home from a Model UN conference and told him she wanted to be a lawyer, not because lawyers made money but because someone had to know the rules well enough to use them for the right people. He had said *that's a good reason* and meant it, and had not known then what kind of law she would find.

"Hey," he said. "You're working late."

"I'm at home. The back bedroom." A pause. "Did you watch the press conference?"

The monitor on his left still had the browser tab open—he had not closed it. The freeze-frame of the young woman at the podium, the petition text behind her, the Channel 7 reporter in the navy blazer standing with her microphone angled and patient. He had watched it twice, once in the morning and once after lunch, with the sound off the second time.

"I did," he said.

"I thought you might have." Another pause, shorter. "I need to tell you something."

The toner-and-coffee smell of the office felt suddenly closer. He leaned back in his chair and looked at the ceiling, at the long fluorescent panel directly above his desk, and then back down at the designation packet on his right.

"All right," he said.

The line was quiet for a moment—not a dropped signal, just Elena gathering herself, which she only did when what came next mattered.

"I've been offered a position," she said. "With the Piscataway tribal legal team."

He did not say anything.

"NAGPRA work, mostly. Some Section 106 consultation. There's a federal recognition petition that's been in progress for four years and they need someone who knows aboriginal title." A brief pause. "I know the case. I've been watching it for two years."

The designation packet sat at the edge of his vision, the blue signature line on page thirty-one invisible from this angle but present the way a splinter is present even when you're not touching it.

"When did they offer it?" he said.

"Three weeks ago. I told them I needed time to think."

Three weeks ago. Before the press conference. Before Kimi Moyaone stood at a podium in Charles County and read a three-hundred-year-old petition into a Channel 7 microphone. He filed that away without comment.

"And now you've thought," he said.

"I watched the press conference this morning." Her voice was careful—not soft, but careful, the way she handled documents she wasn't sure were stable. "The woman reading the petition. The 1697 one."

"Kimi Moyaone."

"You know her name."

"It's my file, Elena."

A small sound that might have been acknowledgment or might have been something else. "She read the whole thing. I have a copy of the petition in my case files—I've had it for eight months. The language about ancient possessors." A pause. "It's good law, Dad. It's old law, but it's good."

"I know what it says."

"Have you read it? The actual petition, not the summary."

The question landed with precision. Not an accusation—she had her mother's instinct for leaving space around a thing rather than driving directly into it. But it was not a casual question either.

"Yes," he said. "I read it in the state archives. Three weeks before the press conference."

The silence that followed was different from the earlier pauses. Those had been Elena organizing her thoughts. This one was both of them standing on opposite sides of something and neither of them moving.

"The Calvert patent," Elena said finally. "The 1687 boundary description."

"Yes."

"It uses the burial ground as a survey marker."

"Yes."

Another silence. He could hear, faintly, the sound of her moving in the back bedroom—a chair shifting, or her setting something down on the desk she worked from on Tuesdays.

"Dad." Not an accusation. Not a question either. Just his name, said the way she said it when she was being precise about something that mattered.

"I know," he said.

"The petition," he said. "The 1697 one. The language about the mound—it's specific."

He understood then what she was doing. She was not asking permission. She was not asking for his blessing. She was telling him what the position was, what she had been watching, what she had already decided—and she was leaving a question in the air, the same kind of question she had always left in the air, not *may I* but *do you understand why*—and waiting to see whether he would answer it honestly.

A pause. Not the organizing kind. The kind where she was deciding whether to trust the question.

"I think I should," she said. "I know the case better than anyone they're likely to find. The Piscataway recognition petition has been in progress for four years and the legal framework needs someone who's worked aboriginal title in federal court." Her voice was careful but not uncertain. The silence that followed was brief.

"I already have, Dad." Her voice had settled—the way a room settles after a door closes. "I called the team this morning. Before the press conference. I wanted to tell you myself."

He sat with that for a moment. She had called the team before she called him. She had made the decision and then called him not to ask permission but to tell him—and to give him the chance to be honest about what he knew and when he had known it, which was a different thing, which was something he had understood only gradually and appreciated too late and understood completely now.

"Elena," he said.

"I know what you're going to say."

"I don't think you do."

A pause.

"Then tell me," she said.

He looked at the designation packet. He looked at the monitor, at the freeze-frame of Kimi Moyaone's hands on the podium. He looked at the ceiling panel, which gave him nothing.

"I read the petition," he said. "I read the Calvert patent. I read the 1683 Dent instrument." He stopped. "I know what the title chain says."

The silence on the line was absolute. No background sounds from her end now, no chair shifting, nothing. Just the slight compression of a cellular connection holding two people in place.

"How long have you known?" she said.

"About the farm?"

"Yes."

He thought about lying. Not a serious thought—he had not lied to Elena since she was eleven years old and he had told her the truth about why her grandmother was not at Christmas that year, because she had asked him directly and she deserved a direct answer. He had held to that since.

"Okay," she said. Just that.

"Okay," he said.
