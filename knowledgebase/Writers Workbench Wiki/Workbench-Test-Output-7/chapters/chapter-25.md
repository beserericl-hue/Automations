The pipe bowl sat in his left hand like a sleeping thing.

Matta turned it once, then again, feeling the smooth red clay warm under his thumb. He had shaped it himself from creek-bed clay, fired it slow in a banked coal bed, and worked the surface down with a river-smoothed piece of chert until it held no mark of the tool. The English constable, if he found it, would see a craftsman's piece. Nothing sacred in the shape. Nothing to read.

That was the point. That had always been the point.

He set the bowl on the bench and took up the deer hide. A good piece, scraped thin enough to fold flat, cured with the doe's own brain and smoked until it went the color of old honey. He had been saving it. He had not known for what until three days ago, when the constable's horse stood at the edge of the settlement and the man's voice carried across the cold air, reading from a paper he held at arm's length as though it smelled bad.

*Any vessel, instrument, or object held for purposes of Indian rite or ceremony shall be surrendered to the county magistrate upon demand.* The constable had read it twice, the second time slower, as though the people gathered in the yard were hard of hearing rather than simply not answering.

Matta had stood at the back of the group with his hands at his sides and watched the man's horse shift its weight from foot to foot in the mud.

Now the hide lay open on the bench. He folded one corner up over the pipe bowl and worked the leather around it, tight but not crushing, pressing out the air.

Behind him, the doorway held a different quality of cold than the room. The outside cold, moving, carrying the smell of woodsmoke from the Hendersons' chimney three fields over, and beneath that the damp-leaf smell of the creek bottom. He did not turn around. He had heard Tessa's moccasins on the threshold, the soft scuff of her stopping.

She did not speak. He did not offer her anything to respond to.

The two figures lay side by side on the bench's far end. He had made them from a single piece of red maple, cut from a branch that had fallen across the path to the spring. He had taken that as a sign worth heeding. He split the branch and worked each figure from one half, so they came from the same wood, the same tree, the same morning. Their faces were simple: a nose, a brow-ridge, the suggestion of eyes. He had drilled the eye sockets with a copper awl and pressed in two glass trade beads, blue-white, the color of winter sky. In the firelight they caught and held whatever light was near.

He rubbed his palms together over the cold ash in the small clay dish at the bench's edge, coating them gray, then took up the first figure. The ritual ash settled into the carved lines of the face. He held the figure for a moment. Not praying aloud, not yet. That would come at the mound. He laid it against the pipe bowl inside the hide and began folding the second layer.

The prayer sticks were last. He had tied the bundle himself over three evenings, working by firelight with Tessa in the other room. Seven sticks of peeled alder, the bark stripped and the wood dried to pale yellow, each one sharpened at the lower end. He had wrapped the upper ends with a strip of blue wool strouding cut from a trade blanket. The same blue the English liked, the color of their coats, which he had always found a useful irony. Between the sticks he had tucked a small knot of dried sweetgrass, braided by his wife's hands before she died, and a single turkey bone, hollow, light as a thought. The bundle smelled of smoke and something sweeter beneath it. The sweetgrass still held its scent after two seasons, faint and dry and clean.

He set the bundle alongside the figures and began wrapping the whole parcel in the outer layer of hide, folding the edges over and lashing them with a length of twisted sinew. His hands knew the work. He had wrapped things for burial before. His wife. His son. Objects that needed to go into the ground because the ground was the only place left that the English had not yet thought to search.

They would think of it eventually. He knew that. He was not under any illusion about the permanence of what he was doing. But *eventually* was not tonight, and tonight was what he had.

The lashing pulled tight. He ran his thumb along the knot, a flat close hitch that would not slip, and set the finished parcel on the bench in front of him.

He was aware of his hands. The knuckles were thick now, the skin loose over them, the veins raised and branching. Old man's hands. He had not thought of himself as an old man for very long. The thought still surprised him sometimes, the way a step in a familiar house could surprise you if someone had moved the threshold.

The fire in the small clay hearth had burned down to coals. The room was warm enough, but the warmth was ending. He would need to go soon, before the moon moved too far west, while the settlement was still quiet.

He heard Tessa shift her weight in the doorway. The metal trade brooch at her collar caught a thread of firelight and threw it back, a brief flash, gone.

He picked up the parcel and felt its weight. Not heavy, not light. The weight of what it was.

He still did not turn around.

He went out past her without a word, and she stepped aside to let him through.

The night came at him all at once: the cold, the dark, the smell of the creek bottom rising from the low ground to the south. He stood on the packed earth outside the door for a moment, letting his eyes find what they could. The sky above the tree line was hard and clear, the stars close and white, the Milky Way laid across the dark like ash raked over coals. To the north, where the road ran, there was nothing. No light, no movement. Only the sound of the creek and the dry tick of a branch settling somewhere in the oaks.

He started walking.

The path to the mound ran behind the kitchen garden and then along the edge of the old cornfield, where the stalks had been cut and the ground was broken and uneven underfoot. He had walked it so many times that his feet found the way without his thinking about it, stepping over the place where a root crossed the path, angling left where the ground began to rise. The parcel was under his left arm, held against his ribs. He could feel the shape of it through the hide. The hard knob of the pipe bowl. The angles of the figures. The slight give of the prayer bundle at the center.

Twenty paces back, Tessa's moccasins were nearly silent on the dry leaves. He could hear her only when she crossed a patch of exposed root or when the wind dropped and the night went still enough. He did not look back. He had told her: twenty paces. Close enough to witness, far enough to say she had been walking. She had not argued. She had simply nodded, and the fact that she had not argued told him she understood exactly what he was asking of her.

He was halfway across the cornfield when the dog started.

It came from the east. The Harmon farm, a quarter mile off, where a yellow dog with a torn ear had barked at him twice before in the years he had been making this walk. He stopped. The dog barked three times, hard and certain, then twice more with less conviction, then once, and then it stopped. In the silence after, he heard the faint sound of a door, a man's voice saying something short and flat, and then nothing. He stood with his weight on his back foot and counted his own breaths. The cold worked into the gap between his collar and his neck. The parcel pressed against his ribs.

When he was satisfied, he walked on.

The path left the cornfield and entered the trees, and the dark thickened. The oaks and hickories closed overhead and the starlight came through only in pieces, small pale patches on the ground between the roots. He slowed. His moccasins found the soft duff between the roots by feel, the ground giving slightly under each step, the smell of old leaves and damp earth rising around him. A branch brushed his shoulder and left a cool, mossy dampness on his sleeve.

He heard the horses before he saw anything.

A sound from the road. The road ran north of the tree line, perhaps two hundred yards, and the sound came through the trees as a muffled, rhythmic strike. Two horses at a walk, unhurried. He stopped again and crouched, bringing himself lower, though there was no one on the road who could see him through the trees in this dark. Old habit. The sound came closer, leveled, passed, and faded north. He waited until he could no longer hear it, and then waited a little longer.

Behind him, Tessa had stopped too. He could feel her stillness the way you can feel another person's held breath in a quiet room.

He stood and walked on.

The mound rose out of the dark ahead of him as a denser shadow against the tree line, a low swelling of earth that the forest had grown around but not over. The old oak at its crest stood apart from the other trees, its crown wider, its roots running down the southern face in thick ridges that broke the surface like knuckles. He had known this mound since he was a boy. His grandmother had brought him here and told him what it was and what it held, and her grandmother had told her, and the telling went back further than anyone he had ever spoken to could trace. He had never asked how far back. It had not seemed like a question that needed answering.

He went around to the northern face, where the oak's roots did not reach, where the ground was softer and the slope gentled out before it met the flat. He had dug here before. He knew the soil: a hand's depth of dark humus, rich and loose, then a harder layer of old fill, compacted and faintly lighter in color, and below that the earlier deposits, the things placed here by people who had not known his name and whose names he did not know.

He set the parcel down on the ground beside him and took the digging stick from where he had tucked it through his belt at his back. The stick was arm's length, fire-hardened at the tip, the grip worn dark and smooth. He pressed the point into the earth and felt it give.

He worked without a torch. A comet had been visible for three nights running, low in the southwest, its tail a faint smear above the tree line, and he had used it to navigate the open ground. Here under the oak it gave him nothing. He worked by feel.

The digging stick broke the humus layer cleanly. He lifted the loosened earth out with his hands, setting it to his right in a careful pile, keeping the layers separate the way his grandmother had taught him. Dark on dark, pale on pale, so the filling would go back in the right order. The soil was cold and slightly damp and it packed under his fingernails and into the creases of his knuckles. He did not mind it. He had spent forty years with his hands in clay and earth. This felt like the same work, only quieter.

A foot down, the digging stick rang differently. A harder note, almost a knock, the old fill compacted by time and weight. He switched to the iron blade. A short, wide thing, more a scraper than a knife, that he used to clean pipe-clay from the grooves of his molds. He had carried it so long that the handle had taken the shape of his grip. He worked the blade along the edge of the hole, cutting rather than stabbing, and the pale compacted earth came away in thin slabs that he stacked to his left.

He was perhaps a foot and a half down when he smelled it. Faint, slightly rancid, the smell of old fat and something underneath it like tar or pine smoke. He stopped and set the blade aside and pressed his fingers flat against the bottom of the hole.

There. A slight tackiness. A waxy resistance.

He did not dig further. He had known it was there. His grandmother had told him, and her telling had been exact: *below the second layer of old fill, above the charcoal band, you will find a sheet that does not rot.* But knowing a thing and touching it were different. He sat back on his heels and held his breath for a moment. The oilskin pulled at his fingertips when he lifted them, reluctant, as though it had grown into the earth around it. A flake of dark, greasy soil came up stuck to his middle finger and he wiped it on the ground beside him.

He had been told not to disturb it. He did not intend to.

He placed his objects above it.

He unwrapped the parcel on the ground beside the hole, folding the hide back one layer at a time. The pipe bowl came free first. The red clay bowl he had shaped and fired himself, smooth from years of handling, the inner chamber darkened with use. He turned it in his hands once, feeling the familiar weight of it, the slight roughness at the base where he had left the clay's natural texture. Then he rubbed his palms on the cold earth beside the hole, a deliberate gesture, the way his grandmother had shown him, and lowered the bowl into the ground.

He spoke the first words barely above breath. The Piscataway came to him the way it always did in ceremony, not thought-through but arriving. The phrases his grandmother had spoken over him when he was small, the ones he had spoken over his own children, the ones that addressed the pipe as a living thing and asked it to carry what it had carried in life into the ground and hold it there. His voice was a low continuous sound, almost no louder than the wind moving through the oak above him.

Then he paused. His grandmother had taught him this too: that the things placed in the mound were placed in the company of those who had come before, and that courtesy required acknowledgment. He spoke the name of the woman at the third depth, the one whose dark-fired bowl had rested at her right shoulder since before the English arrived in numbers, the one whose name was old even in the earliest tellings, its full meaning partly lost. He said it carefully, the way his grandmother had said it, noting aloud that it might not be the name she had called herself. He said it once, and the cold air took it, and he was satisfied.

The two carved figures came next. He had made them years before the hiding became necessary. Small human shapes, barely the length of his palm, cut from a piece of maple that had come down in a storm and seasoned for two winters in the rafters of his workshop. Their faces were simple: a raised nose, drilled eyes, each one fitted with a small piece of white shell he had traded for in the fall. He had rubbed them with red ochre before the last ceremony, and the color had settled into the grain of the wood so that his fingers came away faintly reddish even now, even after months. He could smell the ochre faintly, a metallic earthy smell, dry and mineral, mixed with the bear grease he had used to seal the wood.

He spoke over each figure separately. The ceremony named them not as objects but as persons. The word he used was the word for *those who stand with us*, and he asked that they continue to stand with the people even in the ground, even in the dark, even when no one living knew their names. His voice did not waver. He had been afraid, coming out here, that his voice might waver. It did not.

The prayer bundle was last. He had tied it himself three weeks ago, when the cough had told him he did not have much time. A dozen sticks, peeled and sharpened at one end, the upper ends wrapped in a strip of blue wool strouding he had cut from his own legging. Trade cloth, but it was what he had, and he had decided that what mattered was the intention carried in the wrapping, not the origin of the cloth. The sticks were bound together with sinew and tied through with a single turkey feather and two small shells. He had smoked the bundle over a fire of sweetgrass and sage before he wrapped it in the hide, and even now, outdoors in the cold, it held the smell. A faintly sweet, resinous warmth that he could catch when he turned the bundle in his hands.

He held it for a moment before he spoke. The sticks represented the names he had kept. Not all of them, not the ones in the ledger, but the old names, the ceremonial names, the ones that did not appear in any English record and never would. He had spent the last three weeks trying to decide whether to write them down somewhere. He had decided against it. Writing them would have meant trusting paper, and paper trusted the wrong things. The bundle was better. The bundle could be carried, could be held, could be spoken over. The bundle asked to be remembered in the right way.

He spoke the last ceremony over it. His voice, at the end, was so quiet that he was not sure he was making any sound at all. He was moving his lips, breathing the words rather than speaking them, and the words went into the cold air and the cold air took them. He lowered the bundle into the hole and set it beside the pipe bowl and the two figures, arranging them the way he had arranged things at every ceremony he could remember: pipe at the center, figures to either side, bundle at the foot.

He filled the hole with both hands, pressing the pale compacted earth back in first, then the dark humus on top, working carefully, feeling for air pockets and pressing them closed. When the surface was level he pressed both palms flat against it and held them there for a moment. The earth was cold under his hands. He could feel the slight give of it, the way it accepted the pressure and held the shape of his palms briefly before the cold stiffened it again.

He sat back. His knees ached. The cough was sitting in his chest the way it always did when he had been out in the cold air too long. A tight, waiting pressure, not quite pain. He breathed carefully, keeping his breaths shallow and even, not giving it anything to catch on.

Behind him, at the tree line, Tessa had not moved.

He stood slowly, using the digging stick to push himself upright, and turned to face her. She was a darker shape against the dark trees, her red wool wrap registering only as a deeper shadow, her face a pale oval. He could not read her expression at this distance. He did not need to.

He spoke quietly, just above a whisper, and he spoke in Piscataway first and then again in English, because he needed her to have it in both languages. Needed it to live in her the way it lived in him. In the old words and in the new ones, doubled, so that if one failed her the other might hold.

"Northern face," he said. "Below the first oak's roots, where the slope levels. Arm's length from the stone that sits proud of the surface. You know the stone, the flat one, gray with a white streak. Two hands north of that stone. Two hands' depth below the surface."

She was still.

"Say it back," he said.

She said it back. Her voice was steadier than he expected. *Northern face. Below the first oak's roots. Arm's length from the flat gray stone with the white streak. Two hands north. Two hands down.*

"Again."

She said it again.

He nodded. He picked up the parcel hide from the ground and folded it and tucked it under his arm. The iron blade went back into his belt. He scattered the excess earth he had set aside, a handful here, a handful there, pressing it into the ground with his foot so that the surface looked undisturbed, or as undisturbed as he could make it in the dark.

"When it is safe," he said, "you will know where to find them."

She came a few steps closer. He could see her face now. Young, the planes of it sharp in the cold, her breath a small cloud between them. The carved shell gorget at her collarbone caught a thread of comet-light and released it.

"How will I know when it is safe?"

He looked at her for a moment. The cough moved in his chest and he held it down.

"You won't," he said. "You'll have to decide."

She did not answer that. He had not expected her to. It was not the kind of thing that had an answer.

He looked once more at the ground where he had dug. It was dark and level, the surface barely distinguishable from the earth around it, the old oak's roots running past it on either side like arms held open. In a season the moss would come back. In two seasons you would not find it without the stone.

"Come," he said.

He walked back into the trees, and she followed, and the mound settled into the dark behind them.

The path back to the settlement ran along the ridge, then dropped through a stand of beech where the ground was softer and the leaves had not fully dried. His moccasins picked up the damp and he felt the cold seep through the stitching along his left sole. He had meant to regrease them before coming out. He had forgotten.

Behind him, Tessa's footsteps were quiet but not silent. The soft scuff of buckskin in dry leaves, then nothing when she hit the beech duff, then the creak of a root under her weight. She was good in the dark. Better than he had been at her age. He had taught her some of it and the rest she had come to on her own, the way the useful things always came. Through necessity, not instruction.

He stopped at the top of the ridge. Not for her. For himself. The cough had moved again and he needed a moment to breathe it back down. He stood with one hand on a hickory trunk, the bark rough and cold under his palm, and looked up through the bare branches at the sky.

The Milky Way ran clear from the south horizon to the north, a broad pale road of light that he had navigated by his whole life. Low in the southwest, the comet hung where it had been hanging for three weeks now. A smear of cold brightness with a faint, brushed tail, as though someone had drawn a finger through wet ash. He had watched it every night since it appeared. He did not know what it meant. He had stopped believing that not knowing the meaning of a thing excused him from attending to it.

Tessa came up beside him and stopped. She looked where he was looking.

"It was there last night too," she said.

"It has been there every night."

She was quiet for a moment. "My mother said it meant a change was coming."

"Your mother was not wrong about many things."

The comet did not move. Nothing moved. The forest was still in the way it went still when the temperature dropped hard. The insects long since gone, the small mammals tucked in, the only sound the faint tick of cooling wood somewhere in the canopy above them.

He turned away from the sky and started down the ridge.

She fell in a few steps behind him, not beside him. That was right. They had not come out together and they would not return together. Not visibly, not in a way that anyone watching from a doorway might connect. He had explained this to her before they left and she had not argued. She understood the shape of concealment. Not just the physical part, the separate paths and the dark clothing, but the deeper part. The way you had to arrange your life so that the important things happened in the margins of the visible one.

That was what he had tried to give her. Not the objects. Not even the location. The habit of mind that would let her use both.

At the bottom of the ridge the path narrowed between two fallen oaks, their trunks silver-gray with age, and he turned sideways to pass between them. The parcel hide under his arm caught on a branch and he freed it without stopping. His hands were still chalky with the pale compacted earth from the mound's lower layer. He could feel it in the creases of his knuckles, drying now, pulling the skin tight.

He stopped again at the edge of the settlement's cleared ground, where the trees thinned and the first of the winter-dark garden plots began. From here he could see the low orange line of a banked fire through the gap between two houses. No one moving. The settlement was asleep, or performing sleep, which was sometimes the same thing.

He waited.

Tessa came up behind him and stopped at the tree line, not stepping out. He could feel her there without turning.

"Matta," she said. Her voice was low, barely a breath.

He turned.

She was standing just inside the shadow of the last oak, her red wrap darker than the darkness around her, her face catching a faint edge of the comet's light. She was looking at him the way she had looked at the ground after he had filled the hole. Not afraid, not quite, but with the particular stillness of someone who has just understood the full weight of a thing they agreed to carry before they knew how heavy it was.

He waited for her to say what she needed to say.

She did not say she was afraid.

He did not say he was sorry for leaving this to her.

Those were true things, both of them, and they both knew it. Saying them would not have changed anything except to make the parting harder. He had lived long enough to know which true things needed to be spoken and which needed only to be acknowledged in the silence between two people who were paying attention.

"The stone with the white streak," she said. "I know it."

"I know you do."

"And if the oak falls? If the stone moves?"

He had thought about this. "The oak will not fall soon. It has been there since before my father's time. And stones do not move on their own." He paused. "But if they did, you know the face. You know the depth. You know what is above it and what is below. You have enough."

She looked at him for a moment. The comet's faint light lay across the top of her head, catching the gorget at her collar. A brief, cold flash, there and gone.

"What if I decide wrong?" she said. "About when it is safe."

He thought about that honestly, the way she deserved.

"Then you decide wrong," he said. "And you live with it. Or you decide not to decide, and you live with that instead." He shifted the folded hide under his arm. "There is no path here that does not ask something of you. I would have made it easier if I could."

She nodded. Not agreement, exactly. Acknowledgment.

He turned back toward the settlement. "Go around by the creek path," he said. "Come in from the east side. Wait until the fire dies down."

"I know," she said.

He did know that she knew. He had said it anyway, the way you said things to people you were about to stop being able to say things to. Not because they needed the information, but because the saying of it was its own kind of ceremony. A last small proof that you had been paying attention to each other.

He crossed the cleared ground alone, his moccasins quiet on the frost-hardened earth, the parcel hide under his arm, the cough sitting in his chest like a held breath. The comet hung low and steady over the tree line behind him. He did not look back at it.

The fire through the gap between the houses had burned down to a flat red glow, barely enough to see by, casting no shadows worth the name. He went to his door and stood for a moment with his hand on the frame, feeling the faint warmth leaking out through the gaps in the planking.

Inside, someone was breathing in sleep.

He stood there long enough to feel the cold settle fully into his shoulders and his knees, and then he went in and pulled the door closed behind him. The night outside went on without him. The mound dark under its recovered earth. The comet moving its slow arc toward the horizon. Tessa somewhere in the trees, making her way around by the creek path, carrying everything he had just given her and already deciding what to do with it., -

Morning came gray and without ceremony, the way winter mornings did in that country. Light arrived not as a single moment but as a slow, reluctant brightening that turned the frost on the window-gap from black to white and left everything the same color as cold ash.

Matta was already at his bench.

He had slept three hours, maybe four. The cough had woken him before the light, and he had lain still for a while listening to it subside, then gotten up rather than lie there waiting for it to come back. He had built the fire up from the banked coals, heated water, drunk it plain. His knees ached from the digging. He had not let himself think about the mound, or Tessa, or the comet hanging over the tree line. He had thought instead about the pipe bowl.

The factor's name was Hendricks. He came through the Port Tobacco road four times a year with a cart and a list, and he paid in coin when he had it and in credit when he did not, and he had been buying Matta's pipe bowls for eleven years without once asking who had taught him to carve or where the clay came from. This suited Matta well. Hendricks wanted bowls that the English would buy. Smooth, symmetrical, with a clean draw and no smell of ceremony. Matta made them, and Hendricks sold them in Annapolis and wherever else he went, and the coin or the credit kept Matta's household fed through the seasons when the English were watching more closely than usual and it was better not to draw attention by other means.

The clay was the same red creek-bed clay he had always used, dug from the bank below the mill where the cut face stayed damp even in dry summers. He had three pieces roughed out and waiting on the bench. Rough cylinders, each about the size of his closed fist, the corners already knocked off with a chisel. He picked up the nearest one and turned it in his hands, feeling for the grain, the small flaws, the places where the clay wanted to crack and the places where it would hold.

His hands knew this work the way his feet knew the path to the creek. He did not have to think about it.

He set the piece in the notched block that held it steady, picked up the iron-tipped drill, and began.

The drill made a dry, grinding sound, low and rhythmic, the kind of sound that filled a room without filling it and became, after a few minutes, simply the sound of the morning. Red dust fell in a thin line onto the bench. He blew it away, checked the angle, continued. The cough came once, briefly, and he paused until it passed, then went on.

Outside, the settlement was waking. He could hear it through the planking. A child's voice, quickly hushed. The soft knock of a door. Someone's feet on the frozen ground, going quickly somewhere. Normal sounds. Sounds that meant no one was watching this particular door on this particular morning, which was all he needed them to mean.

He worked the drill in short, even strokes, letting the tool do the work rather than forcing it. Forcing split the clay. You had to find the pace the piece preferred and stay in it. Do that, and it would open for you. Push it, and it would crack and you would have a ruined bowl and a wasted morning. He had learned this the hard way, twenty years ago, and had not had to learn it again.

The bore deepened. He checked it with the tip of his smallest finger, feeling the smoothness of the walls, the roundness of the bottom. Hendricks wanted a clean bowl. No rough patches that would catch burning leaf and char unevenly. Matta ran his finger around the interior three times, feeling each small irregularity, then picked up the curved scraper and addressed them one by one.

His hands were steady.

He noticed this. He had expected them to shake, or to feel different somehow. Heavier, or lighter, or marked in some way by the night's work. They did not. They were the same hands that had held the pipe bowl and the carved figures and the bundle of prayer sticks. The same hands that had pressed the pale compacted earth back down into the hole, tamping it firm, making the surface look as it had before. The same hands that had touched Tessa's arm once, briefly, before he turned toward the settlement. The same hands that had been making pipe bowls for Hendricks for eleven years. They knew what they were doing this morning, and they did it.

He shaped the exterior next. The slight taper toward the base, the flat platform where the stem would seat. Hendricks liked a particular angle on the platform, thirty degrees or thereabouts, which he had explained once with a sketch in the dirt and which Matta had reproduced exactly ever since without needing the sketch again. He worked the angle with a flat file, checking by eye, correcting a degree or two, checking again.

The comet would be gone from the sky in a few weeks. He had heard this from a man who had spoken to a man who had spoken to a minister who had said the comet would pass and not return. Matta did not know if this was true. He had not thought much about the comet until last night, and now he thought about it differently. Not as a sign, exactly, but as a kind of witness. It had been there when he carried the objects to the mound. It had been there when he filled the hole. It had been there when Tessa stood in the shadow of the last oak and asked him what she should do when she decided wrong.

He blew a line of clay dust off the bench and did not think about the comet anymore.

The bowl was taking shape now. He held it up toward the window-gap, where the gray winter light came through in a thin, flat sheet, and turned it slowly. The walls were even. The bore was clean. The platform angle was right. He set it back in the notched block and took up the fine-grained river stone he used for smoothing, working it in small circles over the exterior, feeling the surface move from rough to fine to nearly polished under his palm.

This was the part of the work he liked best. Not the shaping, which required attention and decision, but the smoothing, which required only patience and a steady hand and let the mind go quiet. He had done his best thinking in this part of the work, over the years. He had worked out the accounts in his head during the smoothing. The names, the debts, the old boundary lines that existed nowhere in English writing but that he carried in the same part of himself where he carried the words to the ceremony. He had worked out what to tell his granddaughter, and when, and how much she could hold at one time, during the smoothing. He had worked out most of what he knew about staying alive in this particular time and place during the smoothing, while his hands moved in their small, steady circles and the clay grew warm and the room smelled of cold water and red dust.

He smoothed the bowl for a long time. Longer than it needed. The cough came again and he stopped, waited, breathed carefully through it. It was worse in the mornings. He had noticed this for six weeks or so. The way it gathered overnight and had to be worked through before the day could properly begin. He was not worried about it yet. He had had coughs before. The body had its complaints and mostly they passed.

When the bowl was done he set it on the shelf with the two others he had finished the previous week. Three bowls, enough for Hendricks's next visit. He covered them with a piece of linen, not for ceremony, just to keep the dust off, and straightened up from the bench, feeling his back register the night's digging and the morning's hunched work in equal measure.

He looked at his hands. The red clay dust had settled into the same creases where the pale mound-earth had dried the night before. He could not tell them apart now. The work of concealment and the work of craft, mixed together in the lines of his palms, indistinguishable from each other, the way they had been for most of his life.

He went to the fire and held his hands over it. Not for warmth exactly, but for the feeling of it. The small, reliable heat, the ordinary smell of woodsmoke, the red light moving across his knuckles.

Outside, the settlement went on with its morning. Someone was splitting wood. A dog was barking at something in the creek bottom, the sound carrying flat and clear in the cold air. Somewhere a woman was singing, low and without words, the kind of sound you made without knowing you were making it.

He stood at the fire for a while, listening to all of it., -

Six weeks later, the fever came. It came fast, the way fevers did in that season, and it did not leave. By the fourth day he could not sit up. By the seventh he could not speak clearly.

He had meant to tell his granddaughter everything he knew. The names, the debts, the old boundary lines, the full account he had kept in his head for forty years. But the fever came before he was ready, and when it came he had to speak fast, and some of what he said came out in the wrong order, and some of it he did not reach before the words left him. He had thought he had more time. He had always thought he had more time.

He died on a morning very like this one. Gray, cold, the frost white on the window-gap. The three finished pipe bowls still on the shelf under the linen, waiting for a factor who would come and find them and pay for them and carry them away without knowing or caring who had made them or what other work those same hands had done.

His granddaughter sat with him at the end and held what she could of what he gave her. It was not everything. She knew it was not everything. She held it anyway, the way you hold water in cupped hands. Carefully, knowing some will be lost, trying to move fast enough that most of it reaches where it is going., -

Tessa stood at the mound alone.

She stood there for a long time, long enough for the cold to work through her wool wrap and settle into her shoulders, looking at the ground that looked like every other piece of ground on that hillside. The same leaf litter, the same exposed roots, the same pale compacted earth barely visible where he had smoothed it back. The stone with the white streak was where he had said it would be. The oak was where he had said it would be. Everything was exactly as he had described it, and she was the only living person who knew what was below it, and she had no one left to ask whether it was safe yet, and she was not ready to decide, and she was going to have to decide anyway.

She stood there in the cold and the quiet and understood for the first time what he had meant when he said there was no path here that did not ask something of her.

She decided not to decide yet.

She turned and walked back through the trees toward the settlement, her moccasins quiet on the frozen ground, carrying everything he had given her. The mound held what it held. The winter went on.
