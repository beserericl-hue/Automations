The document lay in its evidence bag on the tailgate of her truck, and Tayak stood over it the way she stood over anything she did not want to put down.

Morning had come in cold and white, the sky the color of old bone. The soybean stubble in Dawes's field caught the low light and threw it back flat and without warmth. Somewhere to the east a crow was working through its complaints, the same three syllables repeated until they stopped meaning anything. Tayak had been at the trench since before six, had drunk her coffee standing up, had not eaten. The document had come out of the ground yesterday and she had not slept more than two hours for thinking about it.

She did not touch the bag. She had photographed it from four angles in the early light, had updated the field log with the morning's conditions — *overcast, 3°C, light wind from the northwest, no precipitation* — and now she stood with her hands in her jacket pockets and looked at the thing she had found.

Laid rag paper, her notes said. Iron gall ink, oxidized to a deep brown at the edges where the oilskin had let moisture in over three centuries. She had held it to the light with nitrile gloves and seen the chain lines running through it like the memory of the loom that made the cloth that made the paper. 1697, the date in the upper right corner in a hand that was not Saya's — a clerk's hand, formal and practiced — and below it, in a different hand, the words that had kept her awake: *we the Piscataway people of this place, being ancient possessors of this ground.*

Ancient possessors. Judge Notley's phrase, lifted and used by someone who knew its legal weight.

She heard the gravel before she saw the vehicle. A low, steady crunch, unhurried, the sound of something moving at the pace of someone who is not lost and is not in a hurry because they already know where they are going. She looked up.

A dark SUV came along the farm road from the county blacktop, moving through the gap in the tree line where Dawes had left the gate open. Maryland plates. A small emblem on the door she could not read at this distance but did not need to.

Her first thought was Marcus. Her second thought, arriving immediately behind the first and canceling it, was that Marcus drove a dented F-150 with a cracked left taillight and would have called ahead.

She pulled her hands from her pockets.

The SUV rolled to a stop twenty feet from her truck and sat for a moment with the engine running, as if the driver were finishing a thought or a sentence into his phone. Then the engine cut. The door opened.

The man who got out was somewhere in his mid-fifties, trim in the way of someone who runs and considers it a discipline rather than a pleasure. He wore a soft-shell jacket with a small embroidered seal on the left chest — she caught the word *Maryland* and below it something in Latin she didn't try to read — and dark cargo pants tucked into clean field boots that had seen real mud before but not recently. An ID badge on a lanyard swung once as he stood and then settled against his chest. He carried a zippered portfolio under one arm and a ruggedized tablet in his other hand.

He looked at the trench first. Then at her truck. Then at her.

"Dr. Moyaone." Not a question.

"Yes," she said.

He crossed the distance between them at the same unhurried pace he'd driven in. "Garrett Slade. Maryland Historical Trust, State Historic Preservation Office." He held out his hand and she shook it. His grip was measured — firm enough to be serious, not firm enough to be a statement.

She had known his name for two years. He was the reason she had read Section 9(c) three times. She had been hoping, in the way you hope against things you already know, that he would not come until she had more time.

He looked down at the evidence bag on her tailgate. "Is that it?"

"That's it," she said.

He did not reach for it. That was the first thing she noticed. He looked at it the way she had looked at it — with attention, not appetite — and then he looked away, back at the trench, at the grid stakes, at the profile drawing Wren had taped to the side of the equipment box.

"Good setup," he said. "Who did the stratigraphy profile?"

"My graduate assistant."

"It's clean work." He meant it. That was the second thing she noticed, and it was worse than the first, because a man who noticed clean work was a man who had done it himself, and a man who had done it himself was harder to dismiss.

He set the tablet on her tailgate, not on the evidence bag, six inches to the left of it, and unzipped the portfolio. The pages inside were organized in labeled sections. She could see the tabs from where she stood: *Permit*, *Disclosure*, *Statute*, *Forms*. He had been organized before he left Annapolis. He had been organized the night before.

"I appreciate you being on site this early," he said. "I know you've had a long twenty-four hours."

"I'm always on site this early."

He nodded as if she'd said something useful. He pulled a single page from the *Permit* section and held it out to her. It was a printout of her own permit — she recognized the header, the permit number, the date — but he had highlighted a paragraph about two-thirds of the way down in yellow. The ink of the highlighter was still slightly waxy-looking, the way it looks when it's recent.

She took the page. She did not need to read it. She had read Section 9(c) the morning the permit arrived, had read it again after Voss's call, had read it a third time at two in the morning with a flashlight in her sleeping bag. But she read it again now, because he was watching and because she wanted him to see her read it carefully, the way you want a poker player to see you count your chips.

*In the event that documentary materials of significant historical value are recovered in the course of survey activities, the Principal Investigator shall notify the Maryland Historical Trust within twenty-four hours of recovery. Such materials shall, upon request of the Trust, be transferred to state custody for analysis and conservation, pending determination of final repository placement.*

"The university filed the required disclosure yesterday afternoon," Slade said. "I received it at four-seventeen." He paused. "I was on the road by six this morning."

Four-seventeen. She thought of Voss. She thought of the call she had not returned last night, the voicemail she had listened to twice and then set her phone face-down on the sleeping bag.

"You drove down this morning," she said.

"It's not far." He said it without apology. "The document you recovered — the 1697 petition. Based on the disclosure summary, it meets the threshold under the clause. Age, rarity, direct association with a documented land claim and a named Indigenous community." He reached back into the portfolio and produced a second page. "I pulled the statute language as well, so you'd have it in front of you."

She did not take that page.

He set it on the tailgate beside the tablet, smoothed it flat with one hand against the morning wind, and weighted the corner with the portfolio's spine.

"I'm requesting transfer to state custody," he said. "For analysis and conservation. The document will be logged, housed in acid-free materials, climate-controlled storage — eighteen degrees, forty percent humidity. Chain of custody maintained throughout." He said *chain of custody* the way doctors say *prognosis*: precisely, and with the understanding that precision is its own kind of pressure. "You'd receive copies of all analysis. Full access for publication."

The crow to the east had stopped. The wind moved through the soybean stubble with a dry, papery sound, the same sound the document had made when she first unfolded it from the oilskin three centuries after someone had wrapped it.

"No," Tayak said.

Slade looked at her. His expression did not change, which told her he had expected this.

"The document came out of a burial context," she said. "It was interred deliberately, wrapped in protective material, placed in association with human remains and cultural objects. That makes it part of the burial assemblage. NAGPRA applies."

"NAGPRA covers human remains and associated funerary objects." He said it without heat. "A paper document —"

"Placed intentionally in a burial mound, in direct association with the interment, by a member of the community whose ancestors are buried here." She kept her voice level. "That's associated funerary object language. You know it is."

He was quiet for a moment. He looked at the evidence bag again — the document inside it, the laid rag paper she had held to the early light and seen the chain lines running through like the memory of a loom.

"The tribe doesn't have federal recognition," he said. Not unkindly.

"The tribe has a pending petition with the OFA. It's been active for fourteen years. And NAGPRA's affiliated tribe standard doesn't require federal recognition — it requires a demonstrated cultural relationship with the materials. Which this document establishes on its face." She paused. "The words *we the Piscataway people of this place* are in the first line."

Slade reached into his jacket pocket and took out a pen. Not a tablet stylus — an actual pen, the kind that clicks. He clicked it once, opened the portfolio to a blank page at the back, and wrote something. She watched him write. He wrote the time first — she could see the colon between the digits — and then her name, and then something else she couldn't read at this angle.

"I want to be clear that I'm not disputing your reading of the document," he said, still writing. "I'm noting the request and the refusal." He looked up. "For the record."

"Note it."

He wrote another line. The pen made a small, precise sound against the paper, quieter than the wind.

"Dr. Moyaone." He clicked the pen closed. "I have a daughter who does tribal rights work. She's out in New Mexico — federal Indian law." He said it without looking away. "She would tell you I'm not your enemy here."

Tayak looked at him. He held the look without flinching, and she believed him — she believed he believed it — and that was the most dangerous thing about him.

"The document stays with me," she said. "Under the excavation permit, as Principal Investigator, I retain custody of all recovered materials pending analysis. Section 6(a). You can check your tab."

Something moved across his face — not surprise, not irritation. Recognition, maybe. He had read Section 6(a) too.

He closed the portfolio. He picked up the tablet and tucked it under his arm. He did not pick up the statute page he'd weighted against the wind; he left it on her tailgate, its corner lifting slightly in the cold air, as if he expected her to read it again once he was gone.

"I'll note the refusal as custody retained by the Principal Investigator pending further determination," he said. "I'll also note that you cited NAGPRA affiliation and burial context provenance as grounds."

"That's accurate."

"I'll be in touch." He said it the way people say *the weather may change*: as a statement of fact about the future, not a threat, not a promise, just a thing that was going to happen.

He walked back to the SUV at the same pace he'd walked over. She watched him go. He did not look back at the trench, or at the evidence bag, or at her. He got in, started the engine, and sat for a moment — finishing a thought, or beginning one — and then he turned the vehicle around in the farm track and drove back toward the gap in the tree line.

The gravel sound faded. The crow started up again to the east.

Tayak looked down at the statute page on her tailgate. The wind had folded the corner back. She smoothed it flat and read the line he had underlined in a different color — blue, not yellow, done at a different time:

*shall, upon request of the Trust, be transferred to state custody.*

*Shall.* Not *may*. Not *should*.

She picked up the page, folded it once along its center crease, and put it in her jacket pocket. Then she picked up her field notebook — red cover, Carver's rule, easiest to find in a site bucket — and wrote the time, the temperature, the name, and the exact words of the refusal she had just given, and the exact words he had used to receive it.

She wrote it all down before she could think about what *shall* meant in a statute, and what it meant that he had driven two hours without calling first, and what it meant that he had left the page behind.

She was still writing when she heard Wren's boots on the gravel behind her.

She didn't turn. She wrote the last sentence — *custody retained by PI per Section 6(a); Slade acknowledged, did not contest* — and capped the pen.

"He's gone?" Wren said.

"He's gone."

Wren came up beside her and looked at the tailgate. The evidence bag was still there, the document inside it, the laid rag paper with its chain lines and its iron gall ink going brown at the edges. Wren looked at it the way you look at something you're glad is still in the room.

"What did he want?"

"Transfer of custody. Section 9(c)." Tayak slid the field notebook into her jacket pocket, next to the statute page. "He wanted me to hand it over for state analysis."

"And?"

"I didn't."

Wren was quiet for a moment. She had her green canvas jacket zipped to the chin and her hands in her pockets, and she was watching the gap in the tree line where Slade's SUV had gone. The gravel had settled. The crow had stopped.

"He didn't argue," Wren said. It wasn't a question.

"No."

"That's not good."

"No," Tayak said. "It isn't."

She picked up the evidence bag. The document shifted slightly inside it — she could feel the faint weight of the paper through the plastic, three hundred and twenty-seven years of laid rag and iron gall and someone's careful hand — and she held it still.

The thing about Slade was the notebook. Not the tablet, not the ruggedized state-issue hardware with its encrypted sync and its timestamped GPS fields. The notebook. Slim, green, the kind you buy at a drugstore or carry in a chest pocket for years. He'd opened it to a page that already had writing on it — she'd seen that much — and he'd added three lines in the unhurried hand of a man who writes things down because writing them down is the last step in a process that began somewhere else, somewhere she hadn't been present for.

She had watched him write her name. She had watched him write the time. She had not been able to read the third line.

That was the line she kept thinking about.

She set the evidence bag down. She pulled out her phone and called David Soto.

He picked up on the second ring, which meant he was at his desk, which meant she had caught him before lunch.

"Tayak." His voice had the particular warmth of someone who is genuinely glad to hear from you and immediately aware that you are not calling socially. "I heard you found something."

"I need to know what a logged refusal does to my permit."

A pause. Not long — David was fast — but long enough that she knew the answer was not the one she wanted.

"Where are you?"

"Field site. Slade just left."

Another pause, shorter. "Tell me exactly what he said."

She told him. She kept it tight: the green notebook, the statute citation, the permit clause, the transfer form he had not unfolded, the three lines he had written while she stood there. She told him about *shall* in blue ink and the yellow highlighting that had been there first, and what it meant that he had gone back a second time. She did not tell him about the partial name at the bottom of the third page. That was not what this call was about.

When she finished, David was quiet for a moment. She could hear him breathing and, faintly, the sound of Annapolis traffic through his office window — a city sound, orderly and indifferent.

"Okay," he said. "Here's where you are."

She waited.

"The logged refusal goes into the compliance database tonight. Slade enters it, it's time-stamped automatically, it backs up to the state server. After that it's a record. Doesn't matter what you do next — the record exists."

"What does the record trigger?"

"Nothing automatically. But it gives him grounds to file a formal transfer request. If he files, the Trust is required to open a mandatory review of the permit."

The crow had started up again, somewhere in the treeline east of the mound. She turned her back to it.

"And the review routes through his office."

"His office manages the review, yes." David's voice was careful now, the way it got when he was about to say something accurate that he wished were not accurate. "He doesn't decide it. There's a review panel. But he controls the timeline, and he controls what gets flagged as a complicating factor."

"How long can he hold the timeline?"

"Pending resolution of a compliance dispute? Sixty days. Possibly ninety if he argues the document's condition requires specialist assessment before transfer can be safely refused."

She did the math. Thirty-eight days to Garrett Wills's bulldozers. Sixty days minimum on a mandatory review. The numbers did not overlap in any direction she wanted.

"The permit freezes during the review?"

"Excavation activities are suspended pending resolution. Yes."

She looked at the truck. Through the rear window she could see the field kit's latch, the padlock she had bought at the hardware store in La Plata, the small combination she had set to her grandmother's birth year because she had been sentimental and tired the day she bought it.

"What's a counter-filing?"

"A formal objection to the transfer request, filed before the review opens. It doesn't stop the review, but it changes the review's scope — the panel has to consider your objection as part of the determination. It buys time and it forces them to address your argument on the record."

"What do I need to file one?"

David took a breath. "A legal basis for retained custody. Usually that's a tribal cultural property claim, a NAGPRA consultation request, or a memorandum of understanding with the Trust that predates the transfer request." He paused. "Do you have any of those?"

She knew the answer. She said it anyway, because saying it was how you made yourself understand the actual shape of the problem.

"No."

"The tribal affiliation hasn't been formally recognized by the federal government," David said, and his tone was not unkind — it was the tone of a man who had explained this particular wall to people he respected, more than once, and had not stopped hating the wall. "Without recognition, a NAGPRA claim doesn't attach. The MOU would require the Trust to agree to it, which they won't do while a compliance review is pending. And a cultural property claim without the legal standing to back it —"

"Slade would note the claim and proceed."

"He would note it and proceed," David said. "Yes."

She watched a gust move through the dry soybean stalks at the field's edge, a low, papery sound like something being turned over slowly. Dawes had left them standing after harvest, the way some farmers did — the stalks were pale and rattled when the wind hit them, and they had been rattling all morning, a sound she had stopped hearing until now.

"So the document is a liability," she said. "As long as it stays in my custody without a counter-filing."

"That's the correct reading."

"And I have no counter-filing ready."

"No."

She pressed her thumb against the folded statute page in her jacket pocket. The paper had gone warm from her body heat, which seemed wrong — a thing that cold should stay cold. "If I transferred it voluntarily. Before he files the formal request."

"Then there's no compliance record. The logged refusal still exists, but there's nothing to review if you've already complied. He'd have to close the case."

"And I lose the document."

"You lose physical custody. You could request access for research purposes. The Trust would likely grant it, given your permit."

*Likely.* She noted the word. David was precise; he did not say *likely* when he meant *certainly.*

"Or," he said, and she heard him choose the word carefully, "you could file something that creates a parallel record. Not a counter-filing in the legal sense. But something that establishes your claim on the document's significance before he files. Something that makes it harder for the review panel to treat this as a straightforward compliance matter."

"What kind of something?"

"That depends on what the document actually says." A pause. "Tayak. What does it say?"

She thought about *We the Piscataway people of this place.* She thought about the handprints in ochre, pressed at the bottom of the petition in 1697 by people who had understood that ink alone might not be believed. She thought about four letters in a different hand, written after the petition was complete, by someone who had needed to add something the petition had not said.

*Sioſ—*

"It's a land rights petition," she said. "1697. Filed with the provincial assembly. It references specific ground — this ground, this mound — as Piscataway burial territory. There are names on it. Handprints."

David was quiet for a moment that felt different from the previous ones.

"That's not a routine colonial document," he said.

"No."

"If it directly references the site —" He stopped. She could hear him thinking, the particular silence of someone rearranging what they knew. "That changes the research value argument. That changes it significantly. A document that explicitly names the site as Piscataway burial ground, filed as a legal petition, in 1697 — that's not just historically significant. That's potentially material to a land claim. To a recognition filing."

"I know what it's material to."

"Then you know why Slade wants it in state custody."

She did know. She had known since she watched him photograph the evidence bag with his phone, the small practiced motion of a man documenting what he intended to take. The petition was not just a historical artifact. It was evidence. It was the kind of evidence that, in state custody, could be studied and cited and referenced in reports that the state controlled, and that, in her custody, could be studied and cited and referenced in a recognition filing that the state did not control.

The difference was who got to decide what the document meant.

"I need to call Nora," she said.

A pause. "Nora first?"

"Nora first."

David was quiet for a moment. She could hear him deciding whether to say what he was thinking. He decided against it. "Call me when you've talked to her. And Tayak — document everything from this morning. Every word he said. Your field log, timestamped, before you do anything else."

She said she would and ended the call.

The crow had gone quiet again. The soybean stalks rattled once and stopped. Somewhere south, so far away it was more vibration than sound, a truck was moving on the county road — the ordinary world, going about its business, indifferent to the thirty-eight days and the fifteen days and the sixty days and the way they stacked against each other like stones in a wall she had not built but was going to have to climb.

She opened her field log to a clean page. At the top she wrote the date, the time — 11:47 a.m. — and the weather: *Overcast, 8°C, light wind NW.* Then she wrote Slade's name, and the statute citation, and the three lines he had entered in his green notebook, and the word *shall* that he had underlined in blue ink on a day before today, when he had gone back to the page and decided it was worth marking twice.

She wrote it all down. She wrote it the way Marant had taught her — clearly, without editorializing, in the plain declarative sentences that would mean the same thing to a stranger reading them in fifteen years as they meant to her now, in this cold field, with the petition locked in the kit behind her and no counter-filing ready and Nora's number already pulled up on her phone.

Then she looked at what she had written, and she thought about the partial name at the bottom of the third page, and she thought about what it meant to add your name to a document that was already complete — to write yourself into the record after the record had been made, because the record alone was not enough, because you needed to be there too, because four letters were better than none.

She thought: *whoever you were, you understood this problem.*

Then she called.

The tent smelled of damp canvas and the iron-cold air that had come in off the field all morning. Tayak sat on the camp stool with the evidence bag on the folding table in front of her and did not open it.

The bag was standard — four-mil clear polyethylene, sealed with a tamper-evident strip, her initials and the date written across the seal in permanent marker. Through the plastic she could see the acid-free folder, and through the folder, barely, the outline of the document inside. Three hundred and twenty-seven years of paper, folded once, wrapped in oilskin by a man whose name she did not know, buried in ground that had remembered it when everything else had been stripped away.

She did not open it.

Nora had listened without interrupting, which was the only way Nora listened. When Tayak finished, there was a long silence — not the silence of someone gathering words but the silence of someone who had already gathered them and was deciding which ones to use.

"He drove down without calling," Nora said.

"Yes."

"That means he already knew you'd say no." Another pause. "He came for the log entry."

Tayak had thought the same thing and had not said it aloud until now. Hearing it from Nora made it more solid — the kind of solid that stays with you.

"He said he has a daughter who does tribal rights work," Tayak said. "Out in New Mexico."

"Elena Slade." Nora's voice was flat, not unkind. "I know who she is. She argued the Pamunkey consultation case two years ago — she was brought in as outside counsel, flew up from Albuquerque for the hearings." A pause. "She's good. She's also not him."

"That's what I thought."

"Tayak." Nora's voice changed register — not softer, but more direct, the way it had been in the kitchen the first time Tayak had come to her with the permit paperwork spread across the table between them. "The name I've been holding. The one at the bottom of the chain."

"Yes."

"I said I would say it at the base layer. In person."

"I know."

"I'm saying it now." A breath. "Not because of the state. Because of what you found." Another pause — shorter, deliberate. "Her name was —"

And Tayak heard it. Two syllables, a weighted pause before the second that changed the meaning from a common word to something that belonged only to one person and one place. She did not write it down. She held it the way Nora had held it — in the body, not on paper.

"Say it back," Nora said.

Tayak said it back.

"Again."

She said it again.

"Good." A long exhale. "That's the first one. The one who started it. The one who pressed her palms to the ground and spoke the name." A pause. "She would have understood exactly what that man came for this morning."

Tayak looked at the evidence bag. Through the plastic, through the acid-free folder, the faint outline of the petition's third page, the bottom margin where the partial name had been written in a different hand.

*Sioſ—*

"Nora," she said. "There's a partial name on the document. Bottom of the third page. Different hand than the main text. It starts *Sioſ—* and then the paper's gone."

A silence.

"Say those letters again."

Tayak said them.

The silence that followed was different from the ones before it — longer, with a quality she could not name, the quality of someone standing very still in a room where something has shifted.

"I don't know that name," Nora said finally. "But I know someone who might." A pause. "There's a woman I should call. She holds names I don't. Older ones. Names that came down a different line." Another pause. "I'll call her tonight."

"Nora —"

"Tonight," Nora said. "Not tomorrow."

Tayak was quiet. Outside the tent, the wind moved through the soybean stubble, that dry papery sound. The crow had not come back.

"The state is going to file a formal transfer request," Tayak said. "David thinks within fifteen days. When they do, the excavation suspends."

"I know what a suspension means."

"I need a counter-filing. I don't have one ready."

"No." Nora's voice was steady. "But you have the name now. And you have what's written in that margin." A pause. "Two forms of keeping. Okwi understood that. So did whoever wrote those four letters."

Tayak looked at the evidence bag. She thought about Okwi pressing two oilskin-wrapped copies into the earth at the base of the mound, operating on the principle that two forms of keeping are better than one. She thought about the petition already complete, the handprints already pressed in ochre, and someone picking up a pen anyway and writing themselves into the margin.

"Get the document photographed," Nora said. "Everything. Then call the woman I'm going to tell you about. Her name is —"

She gave a name. Tayak wrote it in the field notebook, on the same page as Slade's log entry, below the weather and the time and the word *shall* underlined in blue.

"She lives up near Waldorf," Nora said. "She doesn't like phones. Go in person."

"When?"

"Tomorrow morning. Early." A pause. "Tayak."

"Yes."

"You said it right. The first time." A breath. "She would have been glad of that."

The line went quiet. Not dead — Tayak could still hear the small sounds of Nora's kitchen, the tick of the woodstove, the faint creak of the chair — but quiet in the way of a conversation that has finished what it needed to do.

Nora made a sound that was not quite acknowledgment and not quite dismissal — the sound of someone who has given what was asked and considers the thanks unnecessary.

Tayak came out of the tent into the cold air and found Wren standing at the tailgate with the camera already in her hands.

"I heard you in there," Wren said. She did not say what she had heard. "Do you want me to start now?"

"Yes." Tayak set the evidence bag on the acid-free paper. "Full coverage. Macro on the chain lines. And the bottom margin of page three — as close as the lens will go."

The chain lines appeared: faint parallel shadows running through the laid rag paper, the ghost of the mold the papermaker had used, the memory of a loom three centuries gone. The ink had gone brown in places, the iron gall oxidizing slowly through the fibers, eating its own record. But the words were there. *We the Piscataway people of this place.*

Wren began to photograph. The shutter sound was small and precise in the cold air, quieter than the wind.

"Tayak," Wren said, without looking up from the viewfinder.

"Yeah."

"The third page." Wren tilted the document carefully, holding it by the edges. "There's a partial name here. Bottom margin, different hand than the body text. I noticed it yesterday but I didn't —" She stopped. "I should have said something yesterday."

"You're saying it now."

Wren looked up. Something in Tayak's voice had changed and Wren heard it. She did not ask what had changed.

"Photograph it," Tayak said. "Macro. As close as the lens will go."

"Already on it."

The camera clicked again. The tractor sound had faded south. The crow had gone quiet.

Tayak put her hand in her jacket pocket and felt the statute page there, folded once along its center crease, *shall* in blue ink pressed against her palm. She felt the field notebook on the other side, the page with Slade's log entry and the name below it — the name she had said twice and would not write down, the name that was now held in two places instead of one.

She thought about what Nora had said. *Two forms of keeping.* She thought about Okwi at the base of the mound, pressing two wrapped copies into the earth, and about whoever had picked up a pen after the petition was finished and written four letters into the margin because the petition alone was not enough.

She thought: the problem has always been this. Not the state, not the statute, not the word *shall* underlined in blue. The problem is that every form of keeping is also a form of loss. Paper rots. Names thin. Ground gets sold. The only answer anyone had ever found was to keep in more than one way at once — to press your palms flat and speak the name and also write it down and also bury it and also pass it to the next person and also, when the time came, say it again in a cold field in November to a woman who would say it back.

She watched Wren photograph the partial name. The camera clicked once, twice, three times.

"Got it," Wren said.

"Good." Tayak looked at the document — the handprints in faded ochre at the bottom of the petition, pressed there in 1697 by people who had understood that ink alone might not be believed. "Bag it back up. Then I need you to make a copy of the full log for the university archive and a second copy for me."

"Two copies," Wren said.

"Two forms of keeping," Tayak said.

Wren looked at her. Then she nodded, once, and began.
