The dark before dawn had its own weight. Waya's daughter lay on her sleeping mat and felt it pressing down through the bark roof, through the pole frame, settling into the house the way cold water settles into a hollow.

She had not slept. Not truly. She had lain still for hours, listening to the fire tick down to coals, and beneath all of it she had been aware of the cedar box beside her mat the way you are aware of a thorn working through leather — not pain yet, but a pressure that demands attention.

The pendant was inside the box. She did not need to open it. She had held it enough times that her hands knew its weight: cool and dense, slightly rough where the hammer marks crossed the surface, smooth at the center where years of skin had burnished it. She had worn it herself for thirty years, after her mother stopped wearing it and before she understood what she was going to have to do.

Today she would put it in the ground.

She had known it for a month. The knowing had arrived quietly, the way the decision to stop fighting a river current arrives — not as a choice but as a recognition that the choice had already been made, somewhere deeper than thought. The pendant had come far enough. It had traveled from the lake country, from the hands of the man who brought it south along the river corridor, through how many hands she could not say before it reached her mother. It had been worn by enough people. It was time for it to go into the earth, into the mound, into the layer that would hold it alongside the others.

She sat up.

Her hands trembled slightly as she reached for the box — they had trembled since last winter, a fine tremor that came and went with the cold. She did not try to still them. She pressed one hand flat against the cedar lid and felt the grain under her palm, the familiar dry sharpness of the wood's own oils. Her mother had made this box. Waya had made it for the pendant and for her daughter in the same motion, without explaining that was what she was doing, the same way her own father had made it for Waya when Waya was fifteen and first shown the pendant and told what she would one day be responsible for.

She knew the names she would speak. She had practiced them in order, quietly, in the evenings when the house was hers alone. She could say them without hesitation now: the woman at the bottom of the chain; the woman after her; the woman after that; her mother's mother, who had worn it longest; her mother Waya, who had passed it to her. She could say all of them. She had said them enough times that the names felt like a path, each one leading to the next.

But the chain did not reach the beginning.

She knew that. She had always known it. Somewhere before the oldest name she could say, there was the man who had carried the pendant south from the lake country — who had walked or paddled through territories she had never seen, who had arrived at some fire she could not picture, and traded the copper piece for something she did not know. He had a name. He had a mother and a place he called home and a reason for traveling so far. She did not know any of it. She did not even know which people he came from, only that he had come from far away, from the direction of the great water to the north, and that the copper had come with him.

If she buried the pendant today and did not speak his name, the chain would have a gap in it. The memory would be broken at its root.

She had turned this over for a month. She had not found a way through it.

Outside, something moved in the brush at the edge of the house yard — a deer, probably, or a raccoon working the night's last hours before the light drove it back into the trees. She heard the soft displacement of leaves, then silence.

She stood and wrapped her mantle around her shoulders. The deerskin was soft from years of use, worn almost through at the left shoulder where she had carried water in her younger years. The tremor in her hands steadied slightly once she was upright and moving. She found her moccasins by touch and pulled them on, feeling the familiar give of the tanned hide.

She picked up the cedar box.

It was lighter than it felt. That was always the strangeness of it — she expected weight and got lightness, and then the weight arrived later, in her chest, where it had no business being.

She went to the door.

The sky outside was the color of charcoal, the eastern edge barely beginning to differentiate from the dark above it. The mound was a ten-minute walk through the trees, up the slope to the terrace above the creek. She had walked it so many times that her feet knew the path without her eyes — where the root crossed the track, where the ground softened near the seep, where the last stand of hickory opened onto the cleared slope and the mound came into view.

She stood in the doorway for a moment. The air smelled of damp leaves and the faint mineral cold of the creek below. Somewhere in the oaks a woodpecker knocked twice, stopped, knocked again.

She would speak the names she knew. She would say the trader's name as she understood it: *the one who came from far away*. It was not his name. She knew that. But it was what she had, and she had decided — not peacefully, not without grief — that it would have to be enough.

She was not sure it would be enough.

That was what she carried with her out of the house and into the dark, alongside the cedar box: the fear that a name she did not know could undo everything she was about to do. That the pendant would go into the earth with a broken chain attached to it, and the ground would know.

She started up the path.

---

The grey came in slowly, the way it always did this time of year — not arriving so much as accumulating, the darkness thinning by degrees until the shapes of things became possible again without being certain. She sat on the flat stone beside the path's edge. The cedar box was in her lap. She had not meant to sit. She had meant to walk straight to the mound, to do the thing cleanly and come home before the household woke. But her feet had stopped at the stone and she had sat down, and now she understood why.

She was not ready with the names.

She set the box on the stone beside her and pressed both hands flat on her thighs. The cold of the air settled against her face and the backs of her hands. She could see her breath, just barely, in the grey light.

She began.

The oldest name — the woman at the bottom, the one who had started the mound when the mound was only a small rise above the river terrace and the forest came close on all sides. She said it carefully, with the pause before the second syllable that her mother had told her was important, that changed the meaning from a common word to a name. This name she had learned slowly, in the careful, slowed-down way her mother used when she was passing something she was afraid of losing.

Then the next name. Then the next.

Her mother's mother, who had worn it longest — a woman with hands that never stopped moving, who had worn the pendant at every ceremony she could remember, who smelled of woodsmoke and the dried mint she tucked into the folds of her mantle in summer. Gone now many years. Her name was among the easier ones, still warm in the telling.

Her mother's name came last in the chain before the trader. Waya. A woman she had stood beside at this mound more times than she could count, who had pressed her small hand flat against the grass and said *feel the difference* and waited until she felt it. Who had passed the pendant to her keeping with the same unhurried deliberateness with which she did everything. Who had died in the late summer, after a short illness, and been buried near the mound as she had asked, and whose name had been added to the chain after her death.

She said all the names aloud, quietly, not quite a whisper. The sound of them in the cold air made them more solid. That was why you spoke them. The name in your head was only a thought, but the name in the air was a fact.

Then she arrived at the trader.

She sat for a moment without speaking.

The sky had shifted while she worked through the names. The eastern edge was pale now, not yet pink but no longer charcoal — the color of birch bark in shadow. A thrush started up somewhere in the oaks, a single phrase, then stopped. A second thrush answered from farther down the slope.

She said, quietly: *The copper man.*

She listened to it. It named the object, not the person. It could have described anyone who worked copper, anyone who wore it. It did not reach him.

She said: *The one from the north.*

Better. It placed him. But the north was large. It pointed in a direction without arriving anywhere.

She said: *The one who came from far away.*

She stopped.

She said it again.

The third time she said it, something changed — not in the words but in her relationship to them. They stopped feeling like an apology and started feeling like a description. He had come from far away. That was true. It was the truest thing she knew about him, the fact that had survived every thinning and every gap in the chain — not his name, not his people, not the route he traveled, but the distance. The distance had been passed down. Someone had thought the distance was the important thing, and they had been right, because it was all that remained.

She said it a fourth time, and a fifth, and then she stopped counting and simply said it until the phrase lost its strangeness and became part of the sequence — until it sat between the oldest name she knew and the silence before the beginning the way a stone settles into the bed of a still creek, with a small displacement of water and then stillness.

*The one who came from far away.*

She tried to picture him. She could not. She got a figure without a face, walking through forest she had never seen, carrying something wrapped in hide. She got the idea of distance — days and days of travel, territories not his own, fires where he was a stranger. She got the copper itself: the way it felt in her hand when she held the pendant, cool and dense, slightly rough where the hammering had left its marks, the greenish tinge at the edges where years of skin and air had worked on it. He had carried that. He had brought it here, to this river, to these people, and traded it for something she would never know, and then he had gone back the way he came or not come back at all.

She did not know which.

She picked up the cedar box and stood.

The chain was what it was. She had the names she had been given and a description, and the description would have to carry the weight of everything before it. She had decided this inside the house, in the dark, and she had thought she was at peace with it. She was not entirely at peace with it. But peace was not the requirement. The requirement was to do it correctly, with attention, without flinching from what she did not know.

She said the full sequence once more, standing, the cedar box held against her chest with both hands: the oldest name, then the next, then the next, then her mother's mother, then her mother Waya, then the one who came from far away — said in that order, moving from the beginning toward the present, so that the pendant would go into the earth knowing the full length of what had held it.

The thrush started again. This time it did not stop.

She turned toward the path.

---

The path to the mound ran east along the edge of the settlement, past the fire ring and the drying racks and the place where her grandfather used to sit in the mornings to watch the river. She walked it in the half-dark, the cedar box against her chest, her moccasins finding the packed dirt by feel — the path was worn deep enough that her feet knew it without her eyes.

The fire ring was cold. The ash had gone gray and settled flat in the night air, and the stones around it were dark with old smoke. She did not look at it long. Her grandfather had sat on the flat-topped stone to the ring's south side every morning for as long as she could remember, wrapping his mantle around his shoulders and watching the light come up over the water. The stone was empty. It had been empty for two seasons. She did not stop walking.

The drying racks stood beyond the ring — four of them, lashed hickory poles, the crossbars hung with strips of venison gone dark and stiff in the night chill. The smell of them reached her before she was past: salt and smoke and the faint sweetness of fat beginning to render out. She breathed through it and kept moving.

Past the racks the path narrowed, threading between two old oaks whose roots had lifted the ground into ridges she stepped over without thinking. The canopy above her was not yet light enough to show leaves — she moved through shapes, darker against dark, the trunks massive and patient on either side. Somewhere above her a woodpecker struck once, twice, then stopped.

The mound came into view as the path curved left and the trees thinned.

She always forgot, between visits, how the mound looked in low light. In full day it was simply a rise in the ground, grass-covered, perhaps twice her height at its crest, unremarkable to anyone who did not know what it held. In the hour before sunrise it was something else — the pale grass caught whatever faint luminescence came from the sky and gave it back, so the mound seemed to hold a different quality of darkness than the trees around it. She had tried once to explain this to her mother, who had listened carefully and then said only: *Yes. It does that.*

She stopped at the base and stood.

The cedar box was heavier than it should have been for its size. She had noticed this inside the house, in the dark, and she noticed it again now — the density of it, the way it settled into her arms as though it had opinions about being carried. The pendant inside was not large. She had held it in one hand for thirty years. But the box felt like it held more than the pendant, which she supposed it did.

She looked at the mound's crest. The grass there was shorter, kept down by something she had never identified — weather, or foot traffic from the ceremonies, or simply the nature of the soil underneath, which was not the same as the soil around it. Her mother had told her the mound was four layers deep. Her grandmother had told her mother, and her great-grandmother had told her grandmother, and somewhere before that the counting had begun, though she did not know when. Four layers. Four episodes of opening and closing, four times someone had come to this place with something that needed to be kept and had parted the earth and placed it and covered it again. She was going to make it five.

She set the cedar box down at the mound's base, in the grass, and went to find the digging stick.

She had left it there two days ago, leaning against the oak at the mound's eastern edge, wrapped in a piece of bark to keep the dew off the tip. The tip had been hardened in fire — she had done it herself, turning the point slowly in the coals of the evening fire until the wood darkened and the surface sealed. It was her mother's stick originally; this one she had made herself from a straight-grained piece of ash, the length of her arm from elbow to wrist, the tip narrowed and hardened and the upper end wrapped in a strip of soft hide for grip. Her hands trembled slightly as she unwrapped the bark, but once she had the stick gripped properly the tremor steadied. She ran her thumb along the point. Still sharp. Still dry.

She carried it back to the mound and stood for a moment looking at the place where she would open it.

The fifth layer would go at the crest, above the others. That was the structure — each layer above the last, each episode of burial adding height to the mound so that it grew with its contents, the way a family grows with its dead. She knew where to open it because her mother had shown her the spot: slightly left of center, where the soil was darker, where previous openings had left the ground a little looser than the rest. She had knelt there once with her mother and pressed her palm flat against the grass and felt the difference — the way the earth gave slightly under her hand, the way it breathed differently. *Here,* her mother had said. *Always here. So the layers stay true.*

She knelt now and pressed her palm flat.

The grass was wet with dew, cold against her skin. Beneath it the soil was firm but not hard — it had rained three days ago, and the moisture had worked its way down through the grass roots into the upper layer. She could smell it: damp earth, mineral and slightly sour, with something older underneath that she could not name, a deeper smell that came from what was already inside. She had smelled it before, at the ceremonies, when the smoke elder opened the ground briefly to speak into it. She had never been the one to open it herself.

She pressed harder. The soil yielded a little. She felt, or thought she felt, the faint resistance of what was below — not the objects themselves, which were too deep, but the compaction of all the previous closings, the weight of all the earth that had been placed and settled over generations. Four closings. She was going to make it five.

She took the digging stick in both hands and set the tip against the soil at the edge of her palm print.

She pushed down slowly, leaning her weight into it, and felt the tip enter the earth. The soil was damp enough to part cleanly — no crumbling, no resistance at the surface, just a clean entry, the stick going in to the depth of two fingers before she felt it meet something firmer underneath. She worked the stick in a small circle, widening the hole, then pulled it out and moved it a finger's width to the right and pushed again. She worked this way across a span the width of her two hands together, opening the surface in a line, the digging stick making a low scraping sound against the soil that was very loud in the quiet.

The thrush had stopped.

She set the stick aside and used her hands.

Her fingers went into the loosened soil and she scooped it out carefully, setting it to her left in a pile she would use to close the layer again. The soil was cold and slightly gritty — sandy loam mixed with something finer, a pale silt that left a gray film on her skin when she rubbed her fingers together. Below the first few inches the color changed: darker, almost black, with a faint sheen of moisture. She worked more carefully here, her movements slower, her fingers feeling for changes in texture rather than pushing through.

She found the first layer's boundary by feel before she saw it — a change in the soil's resistance, a slight stiffening, and then under her fingertips a different texture altogether: paler, more compact, with a faint grittiness that was different from the sandy loam above. She stopped and looked. In the growing light she could just make out the color difference — the dark upper soil and the pale layer beneath it, a thin line of distinction running across the width of her opening like a seam. The pale layer was the mound's first closing. Everything below it was older than anything she had been told by name.

She sat back on her heels and looked at what her hands had opened.

The hole was perhaps the width of her shoulders and the depth of her forearm. The pale layer was visible at the bottom, clean and distinct. Above it the dark soil she had removed sat in a pile to her left, still holding the shape of her scooping — rounded at the edges, with the drag marks of her fingers visible in it. The air coming up from the opening smelled of old rain and something deeper: a mineral cold, a stillness, the smell of things that had been enclosed for a long time.

She thought of the four layers below her. Her mother had described them once, in the way she described important things — not all at once, but piece by piece, over years, as she was ready for each part. The oilskin bundle at the topmost layer, the hide gone dark and slightly tacky with age. The pipe bowl below that, its stone dark with charred residue, finger-polished at the rim. The antler figure below that, the carved lines fine as a hair. And at the deepest place, the oldest name she knew — the woman at the beginning of the chain, the one she had said first in the sequence, whose object she was now going to place above.

She reached for the cedar box.

She lifted the lid. The pendant lay on the piece of soft hide she had wrapped it in — a flat copper plate, lenticular, the size of her palm, its surface dull brown and green where the years had worked on it. The suspension hole at the top was smooth from use, the cord long since gone. She had replaced the cord herself with a new one, twisted dogbane fiber, slightly rough against her fingers — not because the pendant needed it for burial, but because it seemed wrong to put it in the ground without one, as though the hole at the top implied a cord the way a door implies a threshold.

She lifted the pendant out of the box and held it in both hands.

The copper was cool against her skin. Dense. She could feel the slight roughness of the hammer marks on its surface, the places where the metal had been worked and worked until it thinned and flattened and took this shape. The greenish tinge at the edges was cold under her thumbs. She turned it once, slowly, looking at both faces. They were not identical — one side was slightly more worked than the other, the surface a little smoother, as though it had ridden against skin for years and years and the friction had polished it. She had worn it smooth herself, or helped to. Thirty years of wearing left a mark.

She thought of her mother Waya. She thought of her mother's hands on it — sure, unhurried, the hands of a woman who had spent her life holding things carefully and knowing when to let them go. Waya had worn it for thirty years before her, and her mother's mother before that, and back through the chain to the woman at the beginning, the woman who had started the mound when it was only a small rise above the river terrace and the forest came close on all sides.

She lowered the pendant into the opening.

She set it carefully on the pale layer at the bottom of the hole, face up — the smoother face, the face that had ridden against skin. She centered it as well as she could by feel, adjusting it with her fingertips until it lay flat and still. Then she sat back and looked at it for a moment. The pale soil around it, the dark walls of the opening above, the pendant lying at the boundary between what was old and what was older — a small copper plate, dull and green-edged, with a cord it didn't need threaded through a hole worn smooth by use.

She began to cover it.

She used her hands first, cupping the dark soil from the pile to her left and lowering it into the opening carefully, letting it fall in small amounts rather than dropping it. The soil went in slowly, covering the cord first, then the edges of the pendant, then the center, until only the suspension hole was visible, a small dark circle in the dark soil. She covered that too. Then she pressed the soil down with her palms, gently, feeling it compact under her weight, the surface smoothing under her hands.

She took more soil and added it, building the layer back to its original depth, pressing and smoothing as she went. The soil was cold and slightly gritty and it left gray on her palms and worked under her fingernails and she did not try to keep her hands clean. This was the work. This was what the work felt like.

When the level was right she stopped pressing and simply held her hands flat against the surface for a moment.

Below her palms, four layers deep, the oldest name she knew. Above her palms, the fifth layer, just closed. The pendant was in the ground. The chain was in the ground. The trader was in the ground, or as much of him as she had been able to carry — not his name, not his face, not his people, but the distance, the fact of far away, the truest thing that had survived.

She lifted her hands.

The surface looked almost undisturbed. She would replace the grass — she had cut two pieces of turf carefully before she began, setting them aside in the dew — and by midday the opening would be invisible to anyone who did not know where to look. She reached for the first piece of turf and set it back into place, pressing the edges down, tucking the grass roots into the soil. The second piece went beside it. She pressed both flat with her palms, working the seams together, and then sat back and looked.

The mound looked as it had always looked.

She was cold. She had not noticed the cold while she worked, but now that she had stopped she felt it in her hands and her knees and the back of her neck where the morning air moved through the trees. The sky to the east was pink now — a thin line of it above the treeline, the color of the inside of a shell, pale and precise. The thrush had started again somewhere below her.

She picked up the cedar box and the digging stick and stood.

The mound was five layers deep.

She stood and looked at it for a long moment — the pale grass, the slight rise of it against the pink sky, the seams of the replaced turf already invisible in the low light. She had the feeling she always had at the end of something that could not be undone: not regret exactly, not relief, but a kind of settling, the way a stone settles into water and the water closes over it and is still.

She said the sequence one last time. Quietly, standing, facing the mound: the oldest name, then the next, then the next, then her mother's mother, then her mother Waya, then the one who came from far away. The full length of what she knew.

Then she turned and walked back down the path toward the settlement, carrying the empty cedar box against her chest, her moccasins dark with dew, the digging stick under her arm, the smell of the opened earth still on her hands.

The smoke from the morning fires was rising above the treeline. Someone was already awake.

The settlement was waking. She heard it before she saw it — the knock of a pot against stone, a child's voice asking something in the particular tone that means *now*, a dog's low complaint from somewhere near the river path. The smoke columns she had seen from the mound path were thicker now, and a third had appeared above the eastern houses, pale against the brightening sky.

She walked through the outer ring of the settlement without stopping. Her moccasins were heavy with dew and the mud at the path's edge clung to the seams where hide met sole. She did not try to avoid it. The mud was cold and the ground was soft from the night's damp and her footprints pressed in cleanly and filled slowly with water behind her, the way footprints do in ground that is still deciding what shape to hold.

Her own house was at the settlement's southern edge. She had built it there deliberately, many years ago, after her mother died — close enough to walk to the mound in the dark, far enough that the smoke from her fire went in a different direction than her neighbors'. The door-flap was tied shut. She had tied it herself before leaving in the dark, before the stars had shifted, before the first birds.

She stopped at the door.

The cedar box was in her left hand, her fingers curled around it out of habit, the way you carry something long after it has stopped being heavy. The box was light — had always been light, even when the pendant was inside — but it felt lighter now in a way that was not about weight. She turned it once in her hands. The cedar smell was still there, faint and dry, the smell of the wood's own oils working against rot. Her mother had made the box. She had not thought of that until now. Waya had made it for the pendant and for her daughter in the same motion, without explaining that was what she was doing, the same way her own father had made it for Waya.

She stood at the door a moment longer.

The mound was behind her — she could not see it, the settlement's eastern houses were between her and the tree line, and the hickory closed the path — but she knew the direction of it the way she knew the direction of the river without looking, a kind of knowledge that lives in the body's balance rather than in the eyes. It was there. It would be there when she went inside. It would be there when she woke tomorrow and the day after. The turf seams would be gone by the next rain. The roots would knit together in the dark. The pendant would lie in the fifth layer, in the dark and the cold and the mineral quiet, and it would lie there past her life and past her children's lives and past the lives of people she could not yet imagine, people who would speak differently and eat differently and perhaps not know this hill's name, and it would still be there, and the names sealed into the act of its burial would still be there with it, held in the earth the way the earth holds everything it is given: without preference, without forgetting, without end.

She had not ended the pendant's story. She understood that now, standing at her door with the empty cedar box in her hand and the dew drying on her moccasins and the cold still in her chest from the long kneeling. She had moved the story somewhere safer than memory. Memory thinned. Memory was carried by people, and people died, and some of them died before they had finished the telling, and some of them died with the names still inside them, and what was inside them went with them into the ground. The pendant would not go that way. The pendant would stay. Whatever came after — drought, sickness, departure, the slow wearing-away of everything she knew — the pendant would stay in the fifth layer and the fifth layer would stay in the mound and the mound would stay on the river terrace above the creek, and if someone came who knew how to look, who knew what they were looking at, who knew that a layer of pale subsoil over a layer of dark loam meant a deliberate act and not an accident of water — that person would find it. She did not know who that person would be. She did not need to know.

She had done something that could not be undone. That was exactly what she had intended.

She untied the door-flap and went inside.

The house was dim and cool, the banked fire down to a low red pulse at the hearthstone's center. The smell of last night's food was still in the air — dried fish and the faint sweetness of cooked chenopodium — and underneath it the older smell of the house itself, smoke and hide and the particular smell of packed earth that has been lived on for years and holds the warmth of bodies in its surface even in the cold. She set the cedar box down beside the hearthstone. It made a small sound against the stone, a flat, quiet sound, and then it was still.

She crouched at the fire.

Her hands trembled slightly as she reached for the bark, but once she had it the tremor steadied. The coals were good — she had banked it well before she left, and the heat was still there, waiting. She took a handful of dry bark from the pile beside the door, the inner bark of hickory split fine, and she laid it over the coals in the way she had learned before she could name what she was doing: loosely, so the air could move through, so the heat could find the fuel without smothering. Then she added a piece of split oak, not too large, and another, and she waited.

The bark caught. The small flame moved across it the way fire always does — quickly, as if it had been waiting — and then the oak began to darken at its edges and the smoke changed color and the warmth began to spread outward from the hearthstone in the slow, reliable way of morning fires.

She sat back on her heels and watched it.

Outside, the settlement sounds continued: the pot against stone, the child's voice, the dog. Someone was splitting wood — she could hear the rhythm of it, two beats and a pause, two beats and a pause, the sound of ordinary morning work. A pair of geese went over, loud and low, their wingbeats audible for a moment before the sound crossed the treeline and was gone.

The cedar box sat beside the hearthstone. Empty. She would keep it. It was a good box, well made, and cedar lasted, and there would be other things that needed keeping. She was not sure how many mornings remained to her — the tremor in her hands had worsened since the summer, and she knew what her mother's last year had looked like, and she was not afraid of knowing — but the box would outlast her, and that was as it should be.

The fire was steady now, the oak burning clean, the warmth reaching her hands and her face. She spread her fingers toward it. Her palms were still gray with soil — the deep soil, the cold mineral soil from the fifth layer — and in the firelight the gray looked almost like ash, almost like paint, almost like something deliberate. She did not wash it off yet. She let the fire warm her hands and looked at the soil in the lines of her palms and thought: *I have been inside the mound. I have touched what was kept there. I have added to it and sealed it and spoken the names over it.*

She turned her hands over, gray palms upward, and held them open toward the fire, and the morning came in around the door-flap in thin bright lines, and the work of the day was still ahead of her, and she let it come.
