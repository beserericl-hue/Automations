The newsletter came through at 11:47 p.m., which was exactly the kind of hour the tribal council chose for its digital correspondence. Not deliberately, Kimi knew. Marcus drafted things after his other work was done, after the construction company calls and the BIA correspondence and whatever else filled his days, and by the time he hit send it was always nearly midnight.

She was on her couch with her laptop balanced on her knees and a half-eaten bowl of rice going cold on the cushion beside her. The apartment was the kind of small that Silver Spring called cozy and charged fourteen hundred a month for: one window looking onto a fire escape, a kitchen she could touch both walls of if she stretched, a bathroom with a showerhead that ran warm for exactly four minutes before deciding otherwise. On the wall above her desk she had pinned a paper map of Charles County, the kind the county sold at the courthouse for three dollars, and she had marked the mound site with a red thumbtack. The thumbtack had been there for two months, since Tayak had told her, quietly, over the phone, that the permit was coming.

She opened the newsletter.

*Site 18CH-447. Phase I Cultural Resource Survey. Excavation Permit Issued.*

Then a paragraph of language so dense it seemed designed to repel reading. *Pursuant to Section 9(c) of the Maryland Historical Trust oversight clause, as revised in the 2019 permit framework applicable to sites where the affiliated tribal entity has not achieved federal recognition status, the Principal Investigator, Dr. T. Moyaone, is authorized to conduct systematic subsurface investigation of the above-referenced site, subject to co-supervision protocols for any human remains removal as specified under Section 4(b).*

Kimi read it twice. She set the laptop on the cushion next to the rice and looked at the ceiling, which had a water stain in the shape of nothing in particular.

*Where the affiliated tribal entity has not achieved federal recognition status.*

That phrase. That single subordinate clause, doing so much work, quietly, in the middle of a sentence about permits. Fourteen years of Renata's filing. Twenty-two years of Marcus's council work. Her grandmother's grandmother's stories, carried forward in the dark, told at kitchen tables and bedsides and in cars on the way to funerals. All of it, in the eyes of the Maryland Historical Trust, reducible to *has not achieved.*

She got up and went to the window. The fire escape was wet from the afternoon's rain, and the streetlight below caught the water on the metal steps and made them look briefly valuable. A delivery truck moved through the intersection. A man walked a dog that was pulling hard toward something invisible on the far curb.

Kimi was twenty-six. She had built her channel, *Piscataway Rising*, over four years out of nothing: a camera, a used microphone bought from a film student, and the conviction that if you showed people something true and specific enough, some of them would care. Not all of them. Not even most. But enough. The logo was a heron standing in the Potomac, photographed from the Accokeek shore one October morning when the light was doing something extraordinary. She had eighty thousand followers and had never been called by a Washington Post reporter or a Maryland Historical Trust officer or a Bureau of Indian Affairs representative. But eighty thousand people had chosen, for whatever reason, to keep listening.

She looked at the map on the wall. The red thumbtack.

Her phone was on the desk. She picked it up, turned it over, set it down. Picked it up again.

The thing she knew about visibility was that it guaranteed nothing. She had posted about the NAGPRA dispute in Baltimore, the storage facility, the accession number representing grave goods whose owners the spreadsheet did not name. She had posted about the recognition petition and the BIA's response letters, which used language so careful and so empty that reading them felt like being handed a closed fist. She had posted about the language revival work, Tayak's voice on a recording reading Piscataway words from the margins of a cookbook their grandmother had left behind. Some of those posts had done well. Some had gone nowhere. She had learned not to predict which would be which.

But there was something different about the mound.

She knew it the way she knew when a story was real. Not performed, not curated, but actual. Tayak had been to the site. Tayak had found something: a cobalt glass bead, trade-era, and had not told Kimi what she thought it meant, which meant she thought it meant something significant. The permit was issued. The bulldozers for the Route 301 connector were forty-eight days out. The tribal entity had not achieved federal recognition status.

She opened the camera app.

The apartment's overhead light was harsh, so she turned it off and used the lamp on her desk, which threw a warmer, more directed light across her face and left the wall behind her in shadow. The map was visible but not legible, the red thumbtack a small point of color in the dark. She had learned this from trial and error: the overhead light made her look like she was being interrogated. The desk lamp made her look like she was telling you something important in a room where the rest of the world had gone quiet.

She looked at herself in the small circle of the front-facing camera. Her hair was loose, which she almost never did on camera. She usually wore it back, professional, controlled. She left it loose. She was wearing a t-shirt that said nothing. She had not rehearsed.

She pressed record.

"My cousin is an archaeologist," she said. "She has a permit to excavate a burial mound in Charles County, Maryland. The mound has been there for two thousand years. The highway has been planned for three. I want to tell you what that means."

She paused. Outside, the delivery truck had gone. The street was quiet.

"The permit language says our tribe has not achieved federal recognition. That phrase is doing a lot of work. What it means, practically, is that the state has more say over our ancestors' ground than we do. What it means historically is that we were here before the state existed, and the state's response to that fact is a subordinate clause."

She was not reading anything. She had not written this down. It was coming out of the same place the frustration always came from, the place that had been full since she was old enough to understand what *has not achieved* meant when applied to people who had been somewhere for two thousand years.

"There is a bead in that mound," she said. "A cobalt glass bead from the colonial period. My cousin found it in the first stratum. It has been in the ground for three hundred years waiting for someone with the right to look at it. My cousin has that right. She earned it, and she carries it, and she is in Charles County right now with a trowel and a field notebook doing the work that nobody else was going to do."

She stopped. She looked at the map behind her without turning around, as if she could feel the thumbtack.

"I don't know what else is in that mound. My cousin doesn't know yet either. But I know that in forty-eight days, if nothing changes, the bulldozers for the Route 301 connector are scheduled to break ground on the western flank of that site. And I know that the language of the permit that is supposed to protect it contains, in its third paragraph, a clause that limits our authority over our own dead."

She let that sit for a moment.

"So I'm going to keep talking about this. Every time there's something to say, I'm going to say it. Because the other option is silence, and silence is what got us the subordinate clause."

She pressed stop.

She watched it back once, sitting on the edge of the desk with the lamp still on, the room still quiet. Her voice was steadier than she felt. Her hair was loose and her t-shirt said nothing and behind her the map was a dark shape with a single point of red.

She did not call Tayak. She told herself she would in the morning, after she posted, after she saw how it landed.

She added a caption, *The mound has been there for two thousand years. The highway has been planned for three*, and posted it at 12:23 a.m. Then she put her phone face-down on the desk and went to bed. The lamp stayed on because she had forgotten to turn it off, throwing its warm directed light at the empty chair and the map on the wall and the red thumbtack marking a place in Charles County where her cousin was sleeping in a tent forty-eight days from the end of something.

She could not sleep.

She lay on her back with the phone on her chest and told herself not to pick it up. She picked it up.

Forty-seven views. Then fifty-one. She put it down.

The lamp was still on. She had meant to turn it off before she got into bed and had not, and now the light fell across the foot of the mattress in a warm stripe. She stared at it and thought about the caption she had written. *The mound has been there for two thousand years. The highway has been planned for three.* She had written it in twenty seconds and she thought it was the best thing she had written all month, which was either a good sign about the caption or a bad sign about the month.

Seventy-three views.

She set the phone face-down and put her arm over her eyes.

The problem with the first take had been her hands. She had not realized she was moving them until she watched it back. Not expressively, just restlessly, fingers curling and uncurling against the edge of the desk, the way she did when she was thinking through something she had not finished thinking. It made her look uncertain. She was not uncertain. She had done a second take and kept her hands flat on the desk in front of her, which felt artificial until she watched it back and saw that it looked like stillness, like someone who had decided something and was telling you the decision. She had used the second take.

She lifted her arm. The phone said ninety-one views.

She had edited it to ninety seconds because ninety seconds was the length of something people finished. Longer and they left at the forty-second mark, which she knew from four years of watching her own analytics with the particular intimacy of someone who has learned to read rejection in increments. Ninety seconds meant the whole argument landed before anyone could decide they were busy. She had cut the pause after *subordinate clause*. The original had been four seconds, which was too long for a phone screen, though in the room it had felt exactly right.

One hundred and twelve views.

She got up and turned the lamp off and got back into bed. The room was fully dark and she could still see the afterimage of the red thumbtack when she closed her eyes, a small bright point that faded slowly.

She had not called Tayak.

She had thought about it. She had picked up the phone three times during the editing and put it down each time, and the reason she told herself was that it was late, nearly one in the morning, and Tayak was in Charles County in a tent and probably asleep, and waking her up to say *I'm about to post a video about your excavation* was not the kind of courtesy that helped anyone. The other reason, the one she did not say aloud, was that Tayak would have said wait.

Tayak always said wait. Tayak said wait the way Marcus said wait, the way the BIA process said wait, the way forty-eight days from now the bulldozer operator would wait exactly as long as it took to start the engine and not one second longer. Kimi had been watching people wait for eleven years and she was done with it as a strategy.

She picked up the phone. One hundred and fifty-four views.

Something was happening. Views at one in the morning meant shares, meant someone with a larger account had pushed it somewhere, meant the algorithm had caught a signal she had not expected. She opened the shares tab. A woman in Albuquerque with twelve thousand followers had reposted it with the caption *This is what erasure looks like in real time.* A man in Winnipeg. A heritage account she had followed for two years that had never interacted with her content before. The comments were coming in slowly, one every few minutes, and she read them all: *Sharing this. I didn't know this was happening. The permit language is doing so much work here. This is exactly the kind of thing that never gets covered. What can we do?*

She did not know what they could do. She had not thought past the posting.

One hundred and eighty-eight views.

She thought about the third take, which she had not used. In the third take she had said everything she said in the second take and then kept going, past the planned ending, into something she had not meant to say aloud. Something about their grandmother Nora, about the way Nora told stories that were really instructions for how to keep living after you had lost the thing that was supposed to make living make sense. She had stopped herself mid-sentence and pressed stop and watched it back and it was too much, too personal, the kind of thing that belonged in a different video or no video at all. She had deleted the third take.

But she had meant every word of it.

Two hundred and three views.

She put the phone on the nightstand with the screen facing up so she could see it without picking it up, which was not the same as putting it away but was as close as she was going to get tonight. The numbers changed every minute or so. She watched them change. Two-eleven. Two-nineteen. Two-twenty-four.

She thought: Tayak is going to be angry.

She thought: Tayak is going to be angry and she will be right to be angry and I am not sorry.

She thought: the permit language says *has not achieved federal recognition* and someone in Albuquerque is reading that right now at one in the morning and understanding for the first time what those words mean when you apply them to people who were on the land before the state had a name.

Two hundred and thirty-one.

She closed her eyes. The afterimage of the thumbtack was gone. She could hear the street outside, a car, someone's music briefly audible and then not. The room was dark and cool and the phone's screen cast a faint rectangle of light on the ceiling that shifted each time a notification came in, a small pulse, almost rhythmic.

She thought: I will text her in the morning. I will text her before she sees it from someone else.

She thought: she will see it from someone else.

She thought: I know.

Two hundred and forty-seven views, and Kimi fell asleep with the phone still lit on the nightstand, its screen a pale square in the dark, the numbers climbing quietly past two-fifty, past two-sixty, past the point where she would have been able to tell herself it was just a small thing she had done, a private thing, something that might not matter.

Sleep came in shallow pulls. Kimi surfaced twice, once when a truck downshifted on the street below, once for no reason she could name, and both times she reached for the phone before she was fully awake, the gesture already a reflex.

At 6:47 she stopped pretending.

She sat up, pushed her hair back with one hand, and looked at the screen.

Fourteen thousand, two hundred and eight views.

She read it three times. The number didn't change shape. She put the phone down on the blanket, picked it up again, and went to the shares tab.

The heritage account, *@IndigNationsMedia*, three hundred thousand followers, the one she had followed for two years without ever getting a response to a comment, had reposted at 4:23 in the morning with a caption that read: *A Piscataway burial mound in Charles County, Maryland is forty-eight days from a bulldozer. A tribal archaeologist is racing the clock. Watch this.* Below it, two thousand and eleven shares. Below that, a thread she could not read fast enough.

She sat with that for four minutes. She knew it was four minutes because she watched the clock on the nightstand tick from 6:48 to 6:52, and for those four minutes she let herself feel it. The specific warmth of having done a thing that worked. Having aimed at something and hit it. Having spent years watching the recognition process grind forward one deferred filing at a time and finally, finally moved something herself, with her own hands, in a single night.

Then her phone buzzed with a listserv notification.

The tribal council listserv. The one she was on as a community member, not as staff, which meant she saw what the council members saw but couldn't send to it. The notification was a forwarded email, Marcus's name in the sender line, the subject heading: *FWD: Press inquiry. Washington Post.*

She opened it.

The original email was from someone named Dana Osei, staff reporter, Washington Post, sent at 6:31 that morning. Fifteen minutes ago. It read:

*Mr. Redcloud. I'm a reporter at the Washington Post covering Indigenous land rights and cultural heritage. I came across a video posted last night describing an ongoing excavation of a Piscataway burial mound in Charles County, Maryland, and I wanted to reach out for comment before I begin reporting. Specifically, I have questions about the permit conditions, the tribal recognition status, and the timeline related to the Route 301 connector project. Would you or a council representative be available to speak today? I'm happy to work around your schedule.*

Kimi read it twice.

Her stomach dropped the way a floor drops. Not a sensation of falling but of the ground simply not being where she had expected it.

She got out of bed. Her feet found the cold hardwood and she stood in the middle of the room in her socks and the oversized Howard sweatshirt she slept in and she read the email a third time, as if the third reading might produce a different sender or a different subject line or a different reason that a Washington Post reporter had Marcus Redcloud's direct email address at 6:31 in the morning.

It didn't.

She went to the kitchen and filled the kettle and set it on the burner and stood with her back against the counter while the element ticked toward heat. The DMs. She had not looked at the DMs. She went back to the phone and opened them, forty-three, now forty-six, and she scrolled without reading because she was looking for one name and it wasn't there. Tayak had not messaged her. Tayak had not called. Tayak was in Charles County in a field and it was 6:53 in the morning and either she had not seen the video yet or she had seen it and was deciding what to say, and Kimi did not know which of those was worse.

The kettle began to hiss.

She made tea she didn't want, standing at the counter with the mug between both palms, and she thought about what she had said in the video. She had been accurate. She had the council newsletter open on her laptop while she recorded, the exact Section 9(c) wording on screen, and she had read it aloud and explained it carefully and she had not embellished and she had not guessed. Every fact in the video was a fact. The forty-eight days were a fact. The recognition status was a fact. The mound was a fact. Tayak's name she had not used. She had said *a tribal archaeologist* and not the name, and she had not shown the site location, and she had not said anything about the bead.

She had not said anything about the bead.

That was the thing she kept coming back to. The bead was Tayak's. The bead was the council's. The bead was evidence in a recognition filing that had been years in the making, and Kimi had known that and she had kept it out of the video and she had thought that was enough. She had thought the line was there and she had not crossed it.

But Dana Osei at the Washington Post had questions about the permit conditions and the timeline and the recognition status, and Marcus Redcloud had those questions in his inbox at 6:31 in the morning, and Kimi had put them there.

She set the mug down on the counter harder than she meant to. Tea sloshed over the rim and spread across the tile in a slow brown arc.

She got a cloth and wiped it up and stood looking at the clean counter and thought: call Tayak. Call her now, before Marcus does, before the reporter files anything, before the council listserv erupts, before any of the forty-six DMs turn into something she can't manage. Call her and say *I did this and I know you're angry and I need you to hear me out.*

She picked up the phone.

She put it down.

She picked it up again and opened the video. Fourteen thousand, six hundred and twelve views now. The comment section had moved past the pace she could track, new lines appearing every few seconds, most of them supportive, some of them asking questions she couldn't answer, one of them a link to a 2019 Maryland Historical Trust report on Route 301 that she had not known existed and that she now needed to read immediately.

*What can we do?* someone had written, three hours ago, and below it twenty-seven replies, none of which she had seen. She scrolled through them and found two people who had already emailed the Maryland Historical Trust directly, one person who had tagged a congressional staffer, and one person who had found the developer's public permit filing and posted the project timeline with a thread explaining what a Section 106 consultation was and why it mattered.

She had not asked anyone to do any of that. She had not coordinated any of it. It had simply happened, the way things happened when you put the right information in front of people who had been waiting for something to do with their anger.

She thought: this is not a small thing anymore.

She thought: it wasn't a small thing when she posted it. She had known that. She had told herself it was a small thing because that was the only way she had been able to press the button.

Her phone buzzed. Not a notification. A call.

Marcus.

She looked at his name on the screen for two full rings. On the third she answered.

"I saw it," she said, before he could speak.

A pause. She could hear him breathing, the particular quality of his silence that meant he was choosing words rather than reaching for them.

"Dana Osei," he said. "Washington Post."

"I know."

"She emailed me at six thirty-one."

"I know. I saw the listserv forward."

Another pause. "Kimi." His voice had the flat, careful weight of a man who had decided not to say the first thing he thought of. "Has Tayak called you?"

The question landed like a stone in still water.

"Not yet," Kimi said.

Marcus said nothing. The silence was its own answer. The silence of a man who knew that *not yet* was doing a great deal of work in that sentence, and who was going to let her sit with it.

"I didn't use her name," Kimi said. "I didn't show the site. I didn't say anything about the bead."

"I know what you didn't say."

"The permit language is public record. The forty-eight-day timeline is in the project filing. I didn't reveal anything that wasn't already, "

"Kimi." Not sharp. Quieter than sharp, which was worse. "I'm going to call Tayak in ten minutes. I wanted to speak to you first."

"Nothing. You say nothing to Dana Osei. You say nothing to anyone until I've spoken to Tayak and we've decided together what the council's position is." A pause. "Can you do that?"

"Good." He didn't say anything else for a moment. She heard him shift, a chair, maybe, or a car door. "The video is good, Kimi. Fourteen thousand, eight hundred and thirty-one views. The number changed while she watched it.

She thought about Tayak in Charles County, in the field, already up. Tayak was always up before first light, had been that way since they were children. "Tayak."

Outside, a car started somewhere on the street, and the sound of it pulling away left the morning quieter than it had been. The tea was cold. She didn't make more. She stood in the kitchen and waited, without quite knowing what she was waiting for. Marcus to call back, maybe, or the number to stop climbing, or the feeling in her chest to resolve itself into something she had a name for.

It didn't. It sat there, unresolved, with the shape of Tayak's voice inside it.

Her phone buzzed. Not a call. A notification, the video, another share. Then another. Then three in quick succession, the small percussion of a thing that had found its own momentum now and would not stop because she had put the phone face-down.

Kimi looked at the notifications for a long moment.

Then she went to get her coat.

The email arrived at 11:47 a.m.

She saw it on her laptop when she got back from the coffee shop on Calvert Street, still in her coat, her fingers cold from the walk. She had gone out to think somewhere that wasn't her kitchen, had sat for forty minutes over an Americano she barely touched, and had come home having decided nothing except that she needed to sit down somewhere that had a proper chair.

The subject line read: *Kimi Moyaone.*

Just her name. No punctuation, no question mark. Marcus had forwarded it without a word, not a greeting, not an explanation, not even a period after her name. Just the forward, and her name in the subject line like an accusation he was handing off.

She read the original email below.

*Ms. Moyaone. Dana Osei, Washington Post, Indigenous affairs desk. I came across your video this morning and I have a few questions I'd like to put to you or to a tribal representative. Specifically: Who authorized the excavation at Site 18CH-447? What is the current federal recognition status of the Piscataway Conoy Tribe? And is it accurate that the Route 301 connector project poses a direct threat to the site within the next forty-eight days? I'd welcome a call at your earliest convenience.*

Kimi read it twice.

She took her coat off slowly, laid it over the back of the chair, and sat down.

The questions were not what she had expected. She had expected enthusiasm, the kind of email that began *incredible story* and ended with a request for more footage. She had expected someone who had watched the video and wanted to amplify it, who would ask her to explain the mound in plain language, who would let her tell the story the way she had told it to the camera.

Dana Osei had not watched the video and wanted to amplify it.

Dana Osei had watched the video and started making calls.

*Who authorized the excavation.* That was not a question you asked if you only knew what the video contained. The video said nothing about authorization. It said burial mound, it said Piscataway ancestors, it said forty-eight days. It did not say permit, it did not say university, it did not say tribal council vote. It did not say anything that would lead a reporter to ask about authorization unless the reporter had already spoken to someone who knew about the permit.

Kimi sat with that for a moment.

Someone else had been talking. Someone had seen the video, or heard about it, and had called the Post, or called someone who called the Post, and had said enough that Dana Osei now knew to ask the right questions. She thought about Voss. She thought about Slade. She thought about the permit and the oversight clause Tayak had mentioned, the one that gave the state a hand on the wheel, and she thought about how quickly a story could turn from *burial mound threatened by highway* to *unauthorized excavation triggers permit review.*

She had not made that story. But she might have opened the door for it.

Her hands were on the keyboard. She had typed *Dear Ms. Osei* before she stopped.

She looked at what she had written. She deleted it.

She typed: *The excavation is permitted through the Maryland Historical Trust under a Phase I Cultural Resource Survey. Dr. T. Moyaone of, *

She stopped again.

She did not know what Tayak wanted her to say. She did not know what Marcus wanted her to say. She did not know whether answering this email would help or detonate something, whether Osei's questions were the beginning of coverage that would save the mound or the beginning of a story that would hand Slade and Voss a procedural problem to exploit. She did not know whether Tayak had already seen the forward. She did not know whether Tayak was going to call Marcus the moment she did, or whether Marcus was already on the phone with someone in Annapolis, or whether the email had been sitting in Marcus's inbox for two hours while he decided what to do with it and had finally just sent it to her because she was the one who had started this.

She deleted the second draft.

The cursor blinked in the empty reply field.

Outside, a pigeon landed on the windowsill and stood there for a moment in the flat November light, its throat feathers catching a color that was almost green, and then it left. The windowsill was bare again. The light didn't change.

Kimi closed the reply window.

She pulled up Osei's byline: three pieces on the Post's site, two on Muscogee water rights in Oklahoma and one on a NAGPRA dispute at the Smithsonian that had taken four years to resolve. She copied Osei's email address into a new document, then her name, then the timestamp on Marcus's forward. She added the subject line. She saved the document in a folder she labeled *Press* and then sat looking at the label for a moment before she changed it to *Contacts* instead.

She did not know whether she had helped or hurt. That was the truth of it, and it was not a comfortable truth. It had the particular quality of truths that do not resolve on their own, that sit in the chest and wait, patient as sediment, for something to happen that will tell you which kind of mistake you made, or whether it was a mistake at all.

Fourteen thousand people. Closer to seventeen now; she had checked on the walk home. Seventeen thousand people who had not known, who now knew. A reporter at the Washington Post who was asking the right questions about a mound that forty-eight hours ago had existed only in a permit database and a tribal council meeting and Tayak's field notes.

She had done that.

She had also, possibly, handed someone a lever.

Kimi pulled up a blank email, addressed it to Tayak, and typed: *Marcus forwarded me something. You need to see it if you haven't.*

She attached the forward. She did not add anything else. She sent it before she could revise it into something longer and more explanatory, because longer and more explanatory was what she did when she was trying to manage how someone felt about her, and she was not going to do that. Not with Tayak. Not today.

She closed the laptop.

The document with Osei's contact information was still open in the background, saved, waiting. She would need it again. She did not know yet whether that would be a good thing or a bad one. She suspected it would be both, at different times, in ways she could not yet see clearly enough to prepare for.

She got up and made fresh tea, because the morning had been long and it wasn't yet noon, and because standing at the kettle gave her hands something to do while she waited for Tayak to call back. Or not call back, which was also a kind of answer, and one she was beginning to understand she might have to learn to live with.
