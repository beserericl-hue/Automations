The field notes on her laptop screen needed checking before she could call it a morning. Tayak had been parked at the gate since six forty-five, engine off, the windows fogging at the edges where the April air met the glass. She had a thermos of gas-station coffee going cold in the cupholder and a red-covered field notebook open on the passenger seat, and she was cross-referencing yesterday's context sheets against the digital log when the email came in.

She almost didn't look.

Priya's name in the preview. Priya sent her things at odd hours — cricket scores, geology department gossip, links to papers she thought Tayak should read. Tayak clicked it open because her eyes had drifted from the context sheet and Priya's name was there.

*Did you know about this?*

One line. No greeting.

Below it, a forwarded message from a Research.gov automated sender, the kind of notification the NSF portal generated when a proposal moved from internal routing to submitted. The subject line read: *NSF Archaeology — Chesapeake Trade Networks and Ancestral Land Use Patterns, Site 18CH-447, Charles County, MD.*

Tayak read the subject line again.

She opened the attached PDF.

The cover sheet was three pages, formatted in the NSF's standard layout — boxes ruled off in thin black lines, sections labeled in small caps. Her eyes went straight to the PI field.

**Principal Investigator: Elliot Voss, Ph.D.**
**Sponsoring Organization: University of Maryland**
**Project Title: Chesapeake Trade Networks and Ancestral Land Use Patterns: A Systematic Investigation of Site 18CH-447**
**Submission Date: April 14, 2024, 4:17 p.m.**

She sat very still.

The truck cab smelled of damp soil from her boots and the faint petroleum sweetness of the waterproofing she'd worked into her jacket the night before. Through the windshield, the gate to the field was a grey shape in the morning mist, the padlock catching a little light. She looked at it for a moment, then looked back at the screen.

She scrolled to the Project Description.

Her data was on page two.

Not similar data. Not data gathered independently from the same county. Hers — the artifact catalog numbers she had assigned, her own alphanumeric sequence, the one she'd built in the field log starting with 18CH-447-001 for the blue glass bead. The numbers ran down the methodology section in a clean column. Voss had formatted them into a table. He'd given the table a caption: *Preliminary artifact inventory, Phase I survey, Charles County site complex.*

He had not put her name anywhere on the document.

She scrolled back to the cover sheet and checked the co-PI field. Blank. She checked the senior personnel section. Voss, and two of his own graduate students, their names listed with biographical sketch page numbers.

She read the Project Summary.

The language was careful and familiar — too familiar. The phrase *unbroken occupation sequence* appeared in the second paragraph. She had used that phrase in her preliminary field assessment, the forty-three-page report she had submitted to the department in February. She had chosen it deliberately, knowing its legal weight, knowing that Slade's office would read the report. She had not published it. She had not posted it online. It was an internal document, submitted through the department's research reporting system, accessible to the department chair and the grants office.

Accessible to Voss.

She closed the PDF and opened it again, because the first time she had read it she had moved too fast and she wanted to be sure she had not misread the PI field or the co-PI field or the senior personnel section.

She had not misread them.

The submission timestamp was 4:17 p.m. on April 14th. She looked at her phone. Today was April 17th. He had submitted three days ago, and she had not known, and she would not have known if Priya hadn't seen it — Priya, who must have been running a keyword search on the NSF awards database, looking for something else entirely, and found this instead.

Tayak picked up her phone and looked at Priya's message again. *Did you know about this?*

She typed: *No.*

She deleted it. She typed: *When did you find this?*

She sent that instead, because she needed to know how long the listing had been visible before Priya saw it, and because she needed to do something with her hands.

Then she plugged her phone into the charger cable, set it face-down on the seat, and looked out at the gate.

The mound was forty yards past the gate, down a slope through a stand of tulip poplars that had leafed out in the last two weeks, their new leaves a yellow-green so pale they were almost white in the morning light. She could not see the mound from here. She had not been able to see it from the gate since the third week of March, when the leaves came in and closed the view. But she knew exactly where it was. She knew the slope of the ground above it, the way the water table showed in the soil color at the second stratum, the smell of the clay at depth — a cold mineral smell, like iron and old rain.

Voss had never been to the site.

He had seen the preliminary field assessment. He had seen the photographs she'd submitted with the artifact catalog. He had seen the report, and he had taken the artifact numbers, and the phrase *unbroken occupation sequence*, and the site designation, and he had built a proposal around them and submitted it to the NSF three days ago with his own name in the PI field and no mention of her anywhere in the document.

Her phone buzzed. Priya: *This morning. I was checking the NSF archaeology solicitation for my own stuff. Is this what I think it is?*

Tayak looked at the message. She thought about what Priya was asking — whether this was what it looked like, whether Voss had done what the document plainly showed he had done — and she found she had no interest in softening the answer.

*Yes,* she typed. *Don't say anything to anyone yet.*

She put the phone down.

The coffee in the thermos was cold. She drank it anyway, standing outside the truck now, her back against the driver's door, looking at the gate. The morning was damp and still, the kind of April quiet where you could hear a single crow three fields away. The tulip poplars moved slightly in a wind she couldn't feel from where she stood.

She thought about the submission timestamp. 4:17 p.m., April 14th. She had been in the field at 4:17 p.m. on April 14th. She had been crouched at the edge of the trench with a brush in her hand, working the soil back from the edge of the shell gorget's lower rim. At 4:09 p.m. she had sent the shell gorget preliminary report to the Trust. Voss had been copied as a courtesy, per the oversight clause. Eight minutes between the courtesy copy timestamp and his submission.

She filed that.

She went back to the truck, forwarded the PDF attachment to the printer at her campus office, and started the engine.

The drive to campus was forty minutes on a good day. Today, the Route 301 corridor through Waldorf was backed up past the big-box strip where a Walmart and a Lowe's faced each other across six lanes of stopped traffic, and she sat in it for twenty minutes watching the brake lights and the roadside oaks dropping pollen across the hoods of the cars in front of her. She had the NSF cover sheet in her memory now — the PI field, the blank co-PI field, the artifact numbers in their column, the phrase *unbroken occupation sequence* in the Project Summary — and she turned it over the way she turned an artifact over in her hands, looking for what she had missed.

She had not missed anything. The document was what it was.

By the time she reached campus and parked, she had decided two things: she was going to pick up the printout, and then she was going to the Office of Research Integrity.

The printer in her office had done its job. Three pages of NSF cover sheet, the Research.gov logo in the header, the date and time in the footer: *April 17, 2024, 9:52 a.m.* She picked them up and squared the edges against the desk and looked at Voss's name in the PI field one more time, in print, on paper, where it could not be a screen error or a misread.

Then she folded the pages once and put them in her jacket pocket and went to find a copy shop.

The print shop on Charles Street in La Plata was a narrow room between a nail salon and a tax preparer, and it smelled of warm toner and the faint chemical sweetness of lamination fluid. A ceiling fan turned slowly overhead. The woman behind the counter had a name tag that read *Bev* and was eating a sandwich from a paper bag, and she did not look up when Tayak came in.

"USB or email?" Bev said.

"Email."

Bev slid a card across the counter with the shop's print address on it. Tayak sent two files: the NSF cover sheet she'd forwarded from her campus printer queue before she'd left the parking lot, and the cover page of her own preliminary field assessment — forty-three pages of work, but she only needed the cover, the one that showed her name in the PI field, the site designation 18CH-447, and the date: *March 3, 2024*. Six weeks before Voss had submitted his proposal. Six weeks and eleven days, to be exact.

She paid for two pages, color, standard weight. Bev printed them without comment and handed them across.

Tayak took both sheets to the narrow ledge along the window that served as a work surface, between a rack of lamination pouches and a hand-lettered sign advertising passport photos. She laid the two pages side by side.

On the left: her cover page. *Principal Investigator: Dr. Tayak Moyaone. Site Designation: 18CH-447. Charles County, Maryland. Submitted to the Maryland Historical Trust, March 3, 2024.*

On the right: Voss's NSF cover sheet, printed from Research.gov, the logo small and blue in the upper left corner, the footer showing the system timestamp — *April 14, 2024, 4:17 PM EDT*. *Principal Investigator: Dr. Elliot Voss. Sponsoring Organization: University of Maryland. Project Title: Chesapeake Trade Networks and Ancestral Land Use Patterns: A Systematic Investigation of Site 18CH-447.*

Her site number. Her designation. Her site.

She took out her phone and photographed both pages together, flat on the ledge, her own shadow falling across the lower right corner. Then she photographed each page separately. The phone stamped the time on each image: *9:58 a.m., April 17, 2024.* She checked that the timestamps were visible in the image metadata. They were.

Bev had gone back to her sandwich.

Outside, Tayak sat in the truck with the engine off and both pages on the passenger seat and a red pen she'd taken from the field kit in the door pocket. She read the NSF cover sheet again. Then she turned to the Project Summary, which Priya's forwarded message had included as an attachment — the full three-page summary, not just the cover — and she read it slowly, the way she read stratigraphy profiles, looking for the place where one layer ended and another began.

She found the first theft at line seven of the second paragraph.

*The site presents a stratigraphic sequence consistent with unbroken occupation from the late Woodland period through the early Contact era, with artifact assemblages indicating continuity of ceremonial practice across at least four distinct depositional episodes.*

She had written that sentence. Not a sentence like it. That sentence. She had written it on the evening of March 28th, sitting in the truck with a headlamp on, after they'd pulled the shell gorget from the third stratum. She remembered the exact word she'd debated — *continuity* versus *persistence* — and she had chosen *continuity* because it was the word the OFA recognition framework used, and she had wanted the language of the field report to align with the language of the petition. She had not published it. She circled the sentence in red. The pen made a dry sound on the paper.

The second theft was the artifact catalog numbers: *Catalog entries 18CH-447-003 through 18CH-447-017, inclusive, representing ceramic, shell, and organic material assemblages recovered from Strata II and III.* Her numbering system. Her catalog. The numbers she had assigned in the field, by hand, in her red-covered notebook, the way Carver had taught her. She circled them.

The third was the site coordinates. Latitude and longitude to five decimal places — the kind of precision that does not come from a map, that comes from standing at the datum point with a GPS unit and recording the reading yourself. She had stood at that point on the morning of March 4th, the day after she'd filed the preliminary assessment, and she had taken the reading three times to confirm it. She had not published those coordinates anywhere. They were in the preliminary report she had submitted to the Trust, which Voss had received as a courtesy copy because his name was on the oversight clause of the permit.

She circled the coordinates. She pressed the pen hard enough that the circle scored the paper slightly.

Then she put the cap back on the pen and looked out through the windshield at Charles Street. A woman pushed a stroller past the nail salon. A truck from a landscaping company idled at the light, hauling a trailer loaded with a riding mower and two bags of mulch. The sky was the flat white of an overcast April morning, the kind of light that made everything look slightly provisional, as though the day hadn't committed to itself yet.

She thought about Voss's voice on the phone — the careful, regretful register he used when he had already decided something and was performing the process of deciding it. She thought about the voicemail he'd left after the gorget find, the one she'd listened to twice and not returned. *Extraordinary results, Tayak. The committee is going to want to move on this. We should talk about the next steps for the report.* She had thought, at the time, that *we* was a courtesy. She understood now that it was a claim.

She photographed the circled pages. Then she photographed them again from a different angle, making sure the Research.gov header and the handwritten red circles were both in frame. She checked the focus on each image.

Her hands were steady. She noticed this the way she noticed soil color — as data, not as commentary on herself. Her hands were steady because there was work to do, and she had always been able to work. The feeling underneath the steadiness was something she would deal with later, in the same way she dealt with the cold at depth, the ache in her knees after four hours in a trench, the smell of the clay when it came up from below the water table — you noted it, you filed it, and you kept working.

She folded the pages — her cover page, his cover sheet, the circled Project Summary — and put them in the inside pocket of her jacket, where they sat flat against her ribs.

Then she called Priya.

The phone rang four times before Priya picked up.

"I was wondering when you'd call," Priya said.

"Tell me what you know," Tayak said.

A pause — the sound of a door closing, Priya putting distance between herself and wherever she'd been. "My colleague Farida sits on the NSF archaeology panel. Not as a reviewer on this one — she recused, because she knows the site. But she has access to the submitted proposals queue, and she flagged it yesterday afternoon because of the PI designation. She emailed me first, then tried to reach you directly — I think she wasn't sure how to frame it."

"What exactly did she flag?"

"The application lists Voss as sole PI. No co-investigators disclosed. But the site is an active permitted excavation with a named PI of record — you — and the proposal uses data from that permit period without listing you anywhere. Not as co-PI, not as senior personnel, not in the acknowledgments. Farida said it read like someone had stripped the attribution layer off a document and submitted what was underneath."

Tayak watched a student cross the street outside the print shop, backpack slung over one shoulder, earbuds in, moving fast. The ordinary world, going about its business.

"What time was the proposal submitted?"

"Four seventeen p.m." Priya's voice was careful now, the way it got when she was delivering field measurements she knew would change an interpretation. "April fourteenth. The cover sheet timestamp."

Tayak had sent the shell gorget preliminary report to the Trust at 4:09 p.m. that same day. Voss had been copied as a courtesy, per the oversight clause. Eight minutes between the courtesy copy and the submission timestamp. He had not waited a day, or an hour. He had already written the proposal. He had been waiting for her data to arrive.

She did not say this aloud. She filed it.

"The program?" she asked.

"Archaeology — the standard research grants solicitation. Not a DDRI, not a collaborative. A full PI-driven award. Farida said the budget was structured for two field seasons, a postdoc line, and lab costs. She didn't give me the number, but she said it wasn't small."

A postdoc line. Lab costs. Two field seasons on a site that had thirty-eight days before Garrett Slade's demolition order arrived. He had written the grant as though the site were already his — as though the permit, the preliminary data, the catalog numbers, the coordinates she had taken three times in the cold March air, were simply resources available to him, the way a library database was available to anyone with a university login.

"Tayak." Priya's voice had shifted, lower. "I'm sorry. I genuinely am."

The words landed cleanly. Priya was not a person who said things to fill silence, and she was not saying this to fill silence now. Tayak let it sit for a moment, the way you let a measurement sit before you record it, making sure you've read it right.

"Thank you," Tayak said. "For finding it. For calling."

"Farida wanted to know if she should raise it through the panel. I told her to hold until I'd spoken with you."

"Tell her to hold. I need twenty-four hours."

"She'll give you that." A beat. "What are you going to do?"

Tayak looked out through the print shop window at Charles Street, at the flat white sky above the storefronts. She had three pages of NSF cover sheet in her inside pocket with her own sentences circled in red. She had a timestamp that told the whole story to anyone willing to read it.

"I'm going to file the intake form first," she said. "Then I'll go find him."

Priya said nothing for a moment. Then: "Be careful how you do it."

"I know."

"I mean it. He has more institutional standing than you do right now. If you go in there angry, he gets to define what happened in that room."

Tayak thought about Voss's voice. The careful, regretful register. The way he said things after he had already decided them, performing the process of deciding for the benefit of whoever was listening. She was not going in angry. Anger was a variable she could not afford to introduce.

"I'm not angry," she said. It was almost true.

"Good." Priya exhaled. "Call me after."

"I will."

She ended the call, started the engine, and drove back to campus.

She had thought, on the drive, about how the conversation with Voss would go. She knew how it would go. He would be collegial. He would express concern. He would say something about miscommunication, about the oversight clause, about how he had understood the data to be shared material under the terms of the permit. He would perform the process of misunderstanding for her benefit, and she would have no record of any of it except her own word against his, and his word would carry more weight in this building than hers did. Not because he was right. Because he had been here longer.

She went to the Office of Research Integrity first.

It occupied a suite at the end of a third-floor corridor lined with compliance notices — IRB renewal deadlines, export control reminders, a laminated poster about responsible conduct of research with a photograph of two people in lab coats looking at a computer screen. The carpet was the particular shade of institutional grey that suggested it had been chosen to hide everything. A water stain near the baseboard had been painted over but not quite concealed.

The outer office had two desks. One was empty. At the other sat a man in his late thirties with close-cut hair and a lanyard bearing a photo ID, working through something on a dual-monitor setup. He looked up when she came in.

"Can I help you?"

"I need the formal complaint intake form," Tayak said. "For misappropriation of unpublished research data."

He blinked. Not surprised, exactly — she had the sense he had heard stranger things in this room — but recalibrating. "Are you faculty here?"

"I'm a PI on an active permitted excavation. The site is in Charles County. The preliminary data from that permit period appears in an NSF proposal submitted April fourteenth under a different PI's name."

She watched him process this. He had the careful stillness of someone who had been trained to hear things without reacting to them.

"Okay," he said. "Have you spoken with your department chair about this?"

"I'm documenting that I came here today, at this time, and asked for this form."

A pause. He glanced at the empty desk, as though the person who usually sat there might materialize and take this off his hands. They did not.

"The standard process," he said, "for something like this, is to start with the department chair, and then if the matter isn't resolved at that level —"

"I understand the standard process. I'm not asking you to open an investigation. I'm asking for the intake form. The form that documents that I came here and made this request. I would like that form, and I would like to fill it out, and I would like you to timestamp it when I hand it back."

He looked at her for a moment. She looked back. The fluorescent light above the empty desk hummed at a frequency she could feel at the back of her teeth.

He opened a desk drawer and produced a two-page form printed on white copy paper, the university seal at the top, a series of boxed fields below it: *Nature of Complaint. Date of Discovery. Name of Respondent. Supporting Documentation Attached.*

He set it on the counter between them with a pen.

"There's a chair," he said, gesturing toward a row of seats along the wall.

"Thank you."

*Misappropriation of unpublished preliminary research data.* She wrote it in full. *NSF proposal submitted April 14, 2024, 4:17 p.m., listing E. Voss as sole Principal Investigator on Site 18CH-447, Charles County, Maryland, an active excavation for which the undersigned holds the permit of record and produced all preliminary field data cited in the proposal.*

It was enough to keep moving.
