The fire had been burning since midday. By the time Waya reached the edge of the plaza it had settled into a low, steady pulse, the kind of fire that would last all night whether anyone fed it or not. She could smell the cedar smoke from fifty paces, sharp and clean above the heavier smell of the crowd: sweat, tanned hide, the ghost of boiled corn from a hundred cook pots. The plaza was full. That alone told her something had already shifted.

She found her place near the eastern edge of the fire ring, between Maka and old Siku, where she had stood since she was old enough to stand without her mother's hand. Maka was grinding something small between her fingers, dried sumac from the smell, and did not look up. Siku's eyes were already fixed on the far side of the fire, where the men from the river households had clustered together in a way they did not usually cluster. Waya followed her gaze and found nothing she wanted to see.

Her father was not there yet.

She had three nights of his voice in her head already. He had not been speaking to her. He had been speaking to the walls of their house, to the sleeping mats, to the clay-daubed post beside the door that he touched every morning out of a habit so old it had become invisible. The green corn rite. His voice had gone flat and tight the way it did when he was building toward something. They ate the first corn without fasting. Without the smoke. Without the songs. She had kept her eyes on her work and said nothing, because she had learned years ago that her father's rehearsals were not conversations. They were preparations. You did not interrupt a man sharpening a blade.

He was right about the fasting. She knew it because she had been at the green corn gathering herself and had watched the younger men eat before the purification fire was even lit, laughing at something at the edge of the crowd, their faces bare of paint. She had felt the wrongness of it the way you feel a stone in your moccasin: not crippling, but constant, impossible to ignore. The songs had been shortened. The elder who led the smoke ceremony had paused twice in the middle of the words, and she had seen his eyes go uncertain, reaching for something he had not said aloud in long enough that it had begun to blur.

Her father had seen it too. He saw everything.

What he had not seen, or had seen and chosen not to hold, was the reason the young men were laughing. They were laughing because no one had taught them not to. Because the man who had known the full planting songs had died two winters ago and no one had sat with his knowledge long enough to carry it forward whole. Because the drought had broken three people's confidence in the rites before it broke their water jars. You could not shame a man into remembering words he had never been given.

Waya knew this. She had thought it through in the dark of the house while her father's voice moved through the walls like weather. She had assembled her argument the way she assembled anything, carefully, testing each piece before she set it down. She had arrived at the same place she always arrived: she was right, and she could not say so.

Not to Sokan. Not here.

Across the fire, she saw him enter the plaza from the northern path. He had painted his face for this. Red ocher along the jaw and cheekbones, the line sharp enough that he must have had someone else draw it, because his hands had begun to tremble slightly in the mornings, a thing he did not acknowledge and she did not name. He wore his best mantle, the dark one with the border of worked shell that had belonged to his mother. His hair was tied back with a cord of twisted plant fiber, and the single copper ear spool he wore on his left side caught the firelight and threw it back in a brief, cold flash.

He looked, she thought, like a man who had already decided the outcome.

She watched him move through the crowd toward the fire's western edge, where the senior men stood. He did not look for her. He never looked for her at gatherings, not because he did not know she was there, but because he trusted her to be where she was supposed to be, and she had never given him reason to doubt it. That trust, tonight, felt like a weight she had not agreed to carry.

Maka leaned toward her without turning her head. "Your father came painted."

"I see it."

"Ahanu will not like it."

Waya said nothing. Ahanu would not like it, and Sokan knew Ahanu would not like it, and that was precisely why Sokan had done it. Her father did not make mistakes about what his actions would cost. He made choices about what he was willing to pay.

The fire popped. A child at the inner ring startled and was pulled back by its mother's hand. On the far side of the circle, the council men were settling into their places, not the formal arrangement of the council house with its benches and its smoke and its ranked seating, but the older arrangement, the one that preceded the council house by more generations than anyone could count. The plaza gathering. Open sky. Everyone present, not just the men with the right to speak inside.

That was her father's doing too. She had understood it as soon as she heard where the gathering would be held. Inside, the rules of order protected Ahanu. Outside, in the plaza, with the burial mound visible at the northern edge of the field and the fire between them and the sky, the weight of the old ways pressed on everyone equally.

Waya pulled her mantle closer. The evening had gone cool faster than she expected, and the fire's heat only reached so far. She could feel the packed earth of the plaza through the thin soles of her moccasins, still warm from the day's sun, giving up its heat slowly into the cooling air. Around her the crowd had gone quieter in the way crowds go quiet when something is about to happen that they have been waiting for and half-dreading in equal measure.

She looked at her father across the fire.

He was already looking at Ahanu.

Ahanu stood at the fire's eastern edge, which was where he always stood. Not because the position had been assigned to him, but because he had stood there long enough that the spot had become his by habit, and habit in a community like this carried almost the weight of law. He wore no paint. His mantle was good quality, a dark fur-trimmed wrap he kept for formal occasions, but he had not dressed for a challenge. He had dressed for a gathering, which was what he had been told this was. Waya felt the unfairness of that land on her like something physical. Her father had known. Ahanu had not.

Sokan moved to the fire's western edge and stopped. He did not ask permission to speak. In the plaza, under open sky, with the old arrangement, there was no one to ask. He let the silence build for three full breaths, she counted them, and then he began.

"The first-corn offering." His voice was not loud. It did not need to be. The plaza had gone so quiet that the insects in the grass beyond the mound were audible, a steady, indifferent sound. "Two seasons. Two seasons the first ears went into the pot before the offering fire was lit. Two seasons the smoke did not go up."

No one moved.

"The autumn burn." He turned slightly, not to face anyone in particular, but the movement drew the eye. "Left undone. The fields carry last year's sickness into this year's soil. We ask the corn to grow in ground we have not cleaned."

Waya watched Ahanu. He was listening with his face arranged in the careful stillness of a man who has decided not to react until he understands what he is reacting to. It was a good face for a leader. She had always thought so. But it was the wrong face for tonight, because tonight stillness would read as guilt.

"The burial songs." Her father's voice dropped lower, and the drop made people lean forward. "At the last three burials. Shortened. The words for the third passage, the words that open the way, not said. Not said because the man who knew them fully is gone, and no one thought to learn them from him while he lived."

That one landed differently. She felt it move through the crowd the way a stone moves through still water, the ripple going out and out. Because that one was true in a way that had nothing to do with weakness or permission. That one was grief dressed as accusation, and it was harder to refuse.

Her father paused. The fire popped, and a knot of wood shifted, and the flames rose briefly and then settled. In the new light his face was very clear, the red ocher sharp along his jaw, his copper ear spool burning cold on the left side. He looked like the men she had seen in the old stories her grandmother told, the men who stood at the edge of something and named it before anyone else would.

Then he turned the knife.

"Kotan." He said the name into the open air, and the young man at the inner ring went rigid. She could see it from where she stood, the sudden stillness of someone who has heard their name in a register they did not expect. "Kotan ate before the purification fire was lit at the green corn gathering. He laughed while the smoke elder was speaking the words."

Kotan said nothing. He was perhaps nineteen. He had a younger brother who still carried their father's name because their father had died before the boy was old enough to earn his own.

"Retu." The second name. Retu was older, perhaps twenty-three, with a wife and a child not yet walking. He had been standing near the back of the inner ring and now he took a small step backward, as if he could undo his presence. "Retu wore no paint for the autumn ceremony. He was there. He stood in the circle. But he came without paint, and he did not fast, and he ate dried meat from his own pouch during the elder's prayer."

The silence after the second name was a different kind of silence from the first. The first had been the silence of people absorbing a charge against the community. This was the silence of people watching two young men be stripped of something in public and not knowing whether to look.

Waya's hands were cold inside her mantle. She pressed them flat against her ribs and held them there.

Her father let the names sit in the air for a moment. Then he said, quietly, "These things happened. They happened in the open. They were seen." He paused. "And they were permitted."

The word fell like the last stone into still water, and she understood then exactly what he had done. He had named the drift, true. He had named the young men, true, and cruel, and unnecessary. And then he had turned both things toward Ahanu like a blade with the point at the chest. Permitted. Not happened. Not went uncorrected. Permitted. As if Ahanu had opened a door and waved the decay through.

She looked at Ahanu.

What she saw was not anger. She had expected anger, had been braced for it, had been calculating what anger would cost, how it would play, whether Ahanu had the standing to absorb a charge and respond with authority or whether the anger itself would become the story. But there was no anger. Ahanu's face held something she did not have a clean word for. He was looking at her father with the expression of a man who has been handed a wound he did not earn and does not know how to refuse without looking as though he deserved it. He could not say he had not permitted it without sounding as though he had simply failed to notice. He could not say the young men meant no harm without seeming to excuse the inexcusable. He could not say the man who knew the burial words is dead and grief is not the same as negligence without sounding as though he was defending himself against a charge he had already accepted by his silence.

He was trapped. Her father had built the trap carefully, over a long time, and set it in the open plaza where everyone could see.

Maka's shoulder pressed against hers, barely perceptible. Not a question. Just presence.

Waya kept her eyes on Ahanu's face. The copper ear spool on her father's left side caught the fire again and threw its brief cold light, and in that light she could see the burial mound at the northern edge of the field, the low dark shape of it against the darker sky, patient and indifferent to all of this.

Kotan had not moved. Retu had stopped his backward step. The crowd held itself in that particular stillness that precedes either resolution or rupture, and Waya stood inside it with her hands flat against her ribs and her argument assembled in her chest like a thing she could not set down.

Her father was right about what was broken.

He was wrong about what would mend it.

Ahanu was looking at the fire now, not at Sokan, not at the crowd, not at the two young men whose names hung in the air above the plaza like smoke that had not yet decided which way to go. He was looking at the fire with the face of a man trying to find, in the flames, something he could say that would not cost more than he had left to spend.

The fire gave him nothing. It never did.

The silence held.

Waya counted it, not in heartbeats but in the small movements of the crowd. A woman shifted her weight from one foot to the other. A child at the outer ring reached for its mother's hand and was given it without the mother looking down. One of the older men near the fire coughed once, quietly, into his fist, and then seemed to wish he had not.

Ahanu did not move.

He was still looking at the fire. The flames had dropped since her father began speaking. Someone should have fed them, but no one had wanted to step into the space between Sokan and Ahanu and be seen doing something ordinary. Now they burned low and orange, throwing less light than before, so that the faces at the inner ring were half in shadow. Ahanu's face was one of them. She could see the line of his jaw, the set of his shoulders under the feathered mantle, the way his hands rested open at his sides. He was not clenching them. She noticed that particularly. He was not clenching them.

Her father stood where he had stood throughout, straight-backed, the copper ear spool still. He had said everything he intended to say. She knew this about him: when he was finished, he was finished. He did not pace or shift or reach for more words. He simply waited, with the patience of a man who had prepared for this moment long enough that waiting felt like the easiest part.

Kotan had not looked up. He was staring at the packed earth between his feet with the rigid concentration of someone trying to become very small without moving. He was nineteen years old and his name had been spoken in front of every person he knew, and the thing he had done, eaten before the purification fire, laughed during the smoke elder's words, was not a terrible thing, not the kind of thing a person did from malice, but it had been made into a terrible thing by the telling of it, and now it would be the first thing anyone thought of when they heard his name for a long time. Waya felt something for him that was not quite pity and not quite anger. It sat somewhere between the two.

Retu had stopped his backward step. He stood very still, and his stillness was different from Kotan's. Not the stillness of a young man trying to disappear, but the stillness of a man who has a wife and a child not yet walking and who is calculating, right now, what this costs them. His face was flat and controlled in the way of someone who has decided that whatever expression he shows will be used against him.

Then Ahanu raised his head.

He did not look at her father. He looked at the fire for one more moment, his chest rising with a breath he did not hurry, and then he looked out at the circle. Not at any one person. At the circle itself, the whole of it, the inner ring and the outer ring and the people standing at the edges where the firelight thinned to nothing.

He said, "The community has heard what our elder Sokan has brought before us."

His voice was level. Not warm and not cold. The voice of a man who has decided on his register and will not be moved from it.

"These are serious words," he said. "They deserve serious consideration. They will receive it."

He paused. Not the pause of a man searching for what to say next, but the pause of a man who knows exactly what he is going to say and is choosing the moment to say it.

"The ceremonies will be discussed at the next gathering. All who have knowledge to offer will be heard. All who have concern to name will be heard." He looked once, briefly, at Kotan, not with accusation, not with absolution, just a look that acknowledged the young man's existence, and then looked back at the circle. "We will speak of these things together, as we have always spoken of difficult things. In the proper time and in the proper way."

That was all.

Waya watched her father's face. He had not moved. His expression did not change. But she saw something in his eyes, a rapid, almost invisible recalibration, and she understood that he had not expected this. He had expected anger, or defense, or a counter-charge, or the kind of formal dignity that is indistinguishable from contempt. He had not expected this. This quiet, this deliberate ordinariness, this refusal to make the moment what he had made it.

He is not going to fight you here, she thought, and she did not know whether she was thinking it at her father or at Ahanu. He has decided not to fight you here, and you cannot make him.

The gathering began to come apart the way gatherings did, not all at once but at the edges first, where people felt the release of the moment and turned to the person beside them, and the murmur of private voices started up like insects after rain. The inner ring held a moment longer, then broke. Kotan turned and walked away from the fire, not quickly, with the careful pace of someone who will not be seen to flee. Retu did not move immediately. He stood for a moment, and Waya saw his wife find him at the edge of the outer ring and come to him, and he turned to her and said something low, and she touched his arm.

The older women were moving past Waya now, two of them together, their voices close and quiet. She caught nothing of what they said. Their faces told her something but not enough, not agreement, not disagreement, but the particular expression of women who have watched men conduct the public version of a private argument and are reserving judgment on which man was right until they have spoken to each other in a place where no one is watching.

Maka's shoulder was still against hers. Then Maka said, very quietly, "He handled that well."

"Which one," Waya said.

A pause. "Both," Maka said. "In different ways."

Her father was speaking to one of the older men, Siwek, who had been at the inner ring. Siwek's face was attentive, slightly tilted, the face of a man being told something he is deciding whether to believe. Her father's hands moved as he spoke, not large gestures, just the small precise movements he used when he was making a point he considered beyond argument. He was not looking at Ahanu.

Ahanu had turned away from the fire. He was walking toward the far end of the plaza, not hurrying, his hands still open at his sides, and two men fell into step beside him. Not the urgent clustering of people rallying to a leader under threat, but the quiet companionship of men who had decided the evening was over. One of them said something. Ahanu's head inclined slightly. He did not look back.

Waya watched him go.

Half of them, she thought. Half of them read it as wisdom and half of them read it as nothing.

She could not have said which half was which. That was the thing that frightened her most, not the charge her father had laid, not Ahanu's silence, not the names of the two young men still hanging somewhere in the cooling air above the plaza, but the fact that she could look at the same faces that had watched the same exchange and could not read them. The older women's looks. Siwek's tilted head. The way the men at the outer ring had dispersed, some toward Ahanu's direction, some toward the council house, some simply toward their own homes and their own fires, and she could not tell which direction meant what.

The burial mound was still there at the northern edge of the field. She could see it now that the fire had dropped further, a low dark shape against the sky, patient as it always was. The path to it was clear in the moonlight. She thought of the untended place at the base of it, the weeds that had come up through the packed earth at the offering spot, the ritual post that leaned and had not been straightened. Her father had not named those things specifically, but they were what he had meant. They were the visible evidence of what he had said, and they were still there, and they would still be there tomorrow, and the ceremony that would set them right had not been scheduled and might not be scheduled soon enough.

He was right about that. She was right about that.

And he had used it to do something she was not sure could be undone.

Maka had moved away. Waya had not noticed when. She was standing alone at the edge of where the inner ring had been, the fire now only a low red glow at the center of the empty space. The dust of the plaza was cool under her feet. She could smell the smoke and beneath it the night smell of the fields, the green-rot sweetness of the squash vines and the drier smell of the corn, and further back the river, which was always there beneath everything if you were quiet enough to find it.

She thought: he will not forgive this.

Not Ahanu. Ahanu had chosen not to fight, and that choice would cost him something, but it was his choice and he had made it with his eyes open. She meant her father. Her father would not forgive Ahanu for not fighting. He had come here to draw blood and Ahanu had stepped aside and let the blade pass, and her father would read that as its own kind of insult, the insult of a man who will not honor your challenge by meeting it.

And the next gathering was still to come.

She stood there until the fire went to coals, and then she turned and walked home, and she did not look back at the mound, and she did not speak to anyone, and the argument she had not made moved inside her chest like something alive, something that had not died just because she had kept it quiet.

The path home was narrow and the moon was behind cloud now, so she walked by feel, her feet finding the packed earth between the corn rows the way they always did, the way they had since she was small enough that the corn stalks had been taller than her head. Her father walked ahead. Not far, three or four steps, but enough that she would have had to raise her voice to speak to him, and she did not want to raise her voice.

She watched his back. He moved the way he always moved after he had done something he believed in: a slight straightening of the shoulders, a deliberateness in each step, as if the ground were listening. She had seen it after the planting rites when he had argued for the old sequence and been followed. She had seen it after the council meeting three winters back when he had named the boundary correctly and the elder from the north settlement had finally agreed. It was not arrogance, exactly. It was the particular ease of a man who has carried a weight for a long time and has set it down.

He had not set it down. He had thrown it at someone else. But his shoulders did not know that.

A cricket sang from somewhere in the squash vines to her left. The air smelled of the green-rot sweetness of the leaves and beneath it the dry smell of the corn husks beginning to cure on the stalk, and further still the river, dark and patient at the edge of everything. She breathed it in and let it out slowly.

You named the people instead of the problem.

She had been saying it to herself since she turned away from the fire, turning it over the way you turn a stone to see what lives under it. It was true. He had stood in the inner ring and he had said, not in those words but clearly enough, these people have drifted, these people have let the old ways thin, these people have not kept faith with the ancestors. He had been right about the fact of it. The weeds at the offering spot. The ritual post leaning at the mound's base, its cord frayed and not replaced. The young men at the outer ring who had come to the gathering without paint, without the fast, without anything that marked the occasion as different from an ordinary evening. He had seen all of it and named it truly.

And then he had made the people who had drifted stand in the firelight and feel themselves named. Now they would remember being named. They would carry that into their sleeping and into their waking and into every gathering after this one, and what they would remember would not be the weeds or the post or the fast they had not kept. They would remember the feeling of standing in the firelight while her father's voice moved over them like a hand pressing down. Some of them would come back to the old ways out of shame. Some of them would not come back at all, because shame is a door that opens in two directions.

She had not said this. She had stood at the outer ring with Maka's shoulder against hers and she had not said a word.

The path curved left around a stand of elder trees, their leaves black against the sky. Her father slowed and she came up beside him, and for a moment they walked together in the dark without speaking. She could hear his breathing, steady, unhurried, the breathing of a man who has slept well and expects to sleep well again.

She thought of Ahanu's face in the firelight. The way he had held himself, hands open, not moving. The way he had let the words fall around him without answering. She had read it, at the time, as dignity, the dignity of a man who will not give a challenger the fight he is looking for. But she had also read it as something else, something she could not name cleanly: the look of a man who has already decided that the argument is happening in the wrong place and cannot be corrected there, and so has chosen to wait. Ahanu was good at waiting. She had always known that about him. It was one of the things she trusted in him and one of the things that frightened her, because a man who is good at waiting is also a man who remembers.

Her father would not read it that way. Her father would read Ahanu's silence as weakness, or worse, as contempt. The contempt of a man who will not honor your challenge by meeting it. That reading would sit in him and grow, and the next gathering would come, and the one after that.

She thought: I could tell him now. I could say, Father, they heard you. The weeds at the mound, the post that leans, they heard you name those things, and that is enough. You do not need him to answer. The answer is not the point.

She did not say it.

They came out of the corn rows and she could see the house ahead, the low dark shape of the bark-covered walls, the sapling-frame roof with its ridge pole against the sky, the faint glow of the banked hearth through the smoke hole. Her mother's house. Her father's house. The house she had grown up in, whose walls she knew by touch, whose floor she had swept so many times that she knew every hollow and rise of the packed earth.

Her father stopped at the door and turned, and in the darkness she could see the pale of his face but not his expression.

"Someone had to speak," he said.

She heard it for what it was, not a question, not an explanation. A statement offered to the dark, to her, to whatever was listening. The tone of a man who has rehearsed the sentence and is glad to finally say it aloud.

"Yes," she said.

It was the only word that did not require her to choose. It was true. Someone had needed to speak, the drift was real, the neglect was visible, the ancestors were owed what they were owed. All of that was true. Yes held all of it without holding any of the rest of it, and so she said it, and she hated the precision with which she had chosen it, the way it sat exactly in the gap between agreement and assent and meant neither.

Her father nodded once, satisfied, and ducked inside.

She stood at the door a moment longer. The moon had come back from behind the cloud and she could see the fields, the dark line of the elder trees, and beyond them, she knew it was there even if she could not quite see it, the low shape of the burial mound at the northern edge, patient as it always was. The weeds at its base were still there. The post still leaned. Her father's words were still in the air above the plaza, cooling with the fire, and tomorrow they would still be there too, settled into the people who had heard them, and she could not take them back because they were not hers to take.

She thought: Ahanu will remember I was there.

Not at the outer ring, not at the edge. There. Present. Her father's daughter, standing in the firelight while her father's voice moved over the gathering. That was what Ahanu would have seen, if he had looked. She did not know if he had looked. She had not been able to tell, from where she stood, whether his eyes had found her in the crowd before he turned and walked away with his hands open at his sides.

But he would know she had been there. Word moved in a community the way water moved, finding every low place. He would know, and he would draw the conclusion that her presence invited, and she had no words to correct it that would not also require her to say: my father was wrong in his method, and I should have said so, and I did not.

She could not say that. Not to Ahanu. Not without saying it first to her father, and she had stood at the door of that sentence and found she could not walk through it either.

She ducked inside. The hearth was low, the coals breathing orange in the dark, and the house smelled of tanned hides and dried corn and the faint sweetness of the cedar her mother burned against insects. Her father had already settled onto the sleeping platform, his back to the room. Her mother was a shape in the far corner, still, already asleep or choosing to seem so.

Waya sat down near the hearth and held her hands toward the warmth and did not sleep for a long time.

The argument she had not made was still alive in her chest. She could feel it moving, not loudly, not urgently, but with the patient persistence of something that has found no way out and has decided to wait. It would wait. It was good at waiting.

She thought: this is not over.

Not the gathering. Not her father's satisfaction. Not Ahanu's silence and the two directions it could mean. Not the weeds at the mound's base, the post that leaned, the ceremony that had not been scheduled. None of it was over. Her father believed he had finished something tonight. Ahanu had chosen not to finish it. She had stood between them and said nothing and said yes, and the space between those two things was where she now lived, and she did not yet know what it would cost her to live there.

The coals shifted. A small sound, like a breath.

Outside, the corn moved in a wind off the river, and the mound was dark, and the night held everything equally, her father's satisfaction, Ahanu's patience, the unspoken argument, the word she had chosen because it was the only one that did not cut. None of it knew yet what the next season would bring, or who would need to trust whom when it came, or whether the silence she had kept tonight would hold or break under that weight.

She sat with her hands over the coals until the warmth was gone, and then she lay down, and the dark pressed close, and she did not sleep.

She was up before her father.

The coals were ash by then, grey and cold, and the house held the particular stillness of people who have not yet decided to be awake. Waya pulled her mantle around her shoulders, deerskin, worn soft at the edges, smelling of her mother's cedar, and went out through the door without making a sound.

The air hit her face like creek water. Autumn, fully arrived now. Her breath showed white against the dark, and the plaza was empty, the fire pit a circle of black earth with a thin curl of smoke still rising from somewhere in its center, patient and purposeless. The chunkey pole stood at the plaza's edge with its shadow laid long across the ground. Someone had left a gourd bowl near the fire pit. It sat tilted on the packed earth, catching nothing.

She did not go toward the council house. She went north.

The path to the mound was not much of a path, more a habit of feet, worn into the grass over years, bending around the elder trees at the field's edge and then rising slightly where the ground swelled. She had walked it since she was small enough that the elder roots were obstacles rather than landmarks. She knew every place where the ground dipped, where the grass grew thick enough to wet her feet, where the slope began.

The mound rose from the field's edge the way a sleeping animal rises from flat ground, not dramatically, but with a weight that made the surrounding land feel lighter by comparison. In the grey pre-dawn it was darker than the sky, its silhouette low and patient. The weeds at its base were heavy with dew. She walked through them and felt the cold water on her feet and shins and did not stop until she stood at the mound's southern face, close enough to press her hand against the earth.

She pressed her hand against the earth.

It was cold. Colder than the air, colder than the plaza, holding the night in its clay the way a pot holds water. Under her palm she could feel the slight roughness of it, grass roots, small stones, the particular texture of ground that has been shaped by intention and then left to the weather for longer than anyone now living could say. Her grandmother's grandmother's grandmother. Further back than that. People who had carried someone here, laid them down, covered them, and then walked away and lived the rest of their lives knowing this place was here.

She had always found it easier to think at the mound. It was not that the ancestors answered. She did not expect that, had never expected it, had been quietly skeptical of the people who claimed to hear voices in such places. It was something simpler. The mound did not need anything from her. It did not need her to be Sokan's daughter or Ahanu's subject or the girl who had said yes when she meant something more complicated than yes. It had been here before all of them and would be here after, and it asked nothing, and in that asking-nothing she could sometimes find the shape of her own thinking more clearly than she could anywhere else.

She thought about what she knew.

Sokan was right. That was the part that made everything harder. If he had been simply wrong she could have stood in the firelight and felt only the ordinary grief of watching a father embarrass himself, and then she could have gone home and slept. But he was right. The green corn ceremony had been thin this year, the fasting shortened, the ritual bathing skipped by half the younger people, the purification fire lit without the proper preparation because someone had let the coals go cold and no one had wanted to say so aloud. She had noticed. She had said nothing. The mound path had weeds on it that should have been cleared in the spring, and the leaning post had been leaning since before the last planting, and no one had straightened it. The ancestors were owed what they were owed and were not receiving it. Her father had seen all of this clearly. His eyes were good for that kind of seeing.

And Ahanu was right. That was the other part. Shame did not restore what shame had driven away. She had watched the faces in the firelight while her father spoke, the young man near the outer ring who had not done the ritual bathing, who had stood with his jaw set and his eyes going flat as her father's words found him and named him without naming him. That young man would not be drawn back to the ceremonies by the memory of his own humiliation. He would be pushed further from them. He would find reasons why the old ways were not his ways, why the people who kept them were people he did not need to be. She had seen it happen before. The community had lost people that way, not to death but to distance, to a slow turning away that looked like indifference and was actually something more painful and more permanent.

Her father had diagnosed the sickness correctly and then prescribed the wrong medicine, and she was the only person in the community who could see both of those things at once, and she had no standing to say either one aloud.

She pressed her palm harder against the mound's cold face.

Not Sokan's daughter speaking against her father, not in public, not in the gathering, not where the words would travel the way water traveled and find every low place and settle there. She had no standing for that. She was twenty years old and she was his blood and she had been standing in the firelight while he spoke, which meant that whatever she said now would be heard through that, filtered through the image of her there, present, her face lit by the fire he was speaking across. She could not undo that. She had not moved to the outer ring. She had not stepped back into the dark. She had stood there because she had not known what else to do, and now that position was fixed, and any word she said against him would be heard as a betrayal rather than a correction, and betrayal was not what she meant.

And not a young woman correcting the leader. She had seen what happened to people who corrected Ahanu without the standing to do it, not harshly, Ahanu was not a harsh man, but they were heard differently afterward, their words arriving already weighted with the memory of the correction, already slightly suspect. She did not want to be suspect. She wanted to be useful, and useful required being trusted, and trust required not spending it on a correction she had no authority to make.

So she had said nothing. She had said yes, which was not nothing but was the shape of nothing, the word that held the space where a real answer should have been.

She had hated herself for it last night. She still hated herself for it now, standing in the cold with her hand against the mound's earth and her breath making small clouds in the grey air. But hating herself for it did not change the fact that it had been the only available word. She had looked at the other words and found them all too costly. That was the truth of it. She had not been cowardly, or she had been cowardly, but not only cowardly. She had also been calculating, which was worse. She had stood in the firelight and calculated the cost of each word and chosen the cheapest one, and the cheapest one had been yes, and now she had to live in the house that word had built.

A bird called from the elder trees. Once, then silence.

She thought: I cannot lose both of them.

Her father, who was right about the diagnosis and wrong about the cure, and who had needed her yes last night the way a man needs to feel the ground under his feet, not because it tells him anything new but because it confirms he has not yet fallen. She knew what it had meant to him, that small sound she had made in the dark. She knew what he would do with it.

Ahanu, who had walked away with his hands open at his sides, and who would know she had been there, and who would draw the conclusion her presence invited, and who might already be drawing it in whatever quiet room he sat in at this hour, if he too had not slept.

She could not lose both of them. But the way she was standing now, between them, saying nothing, saying yes, she was going to lose both of them anyway, only more slowly, and with more damage to everything she cared about in the interval.

She took her hand from the mound's face. Her palm was cold, marked faintly with the texture of the earth. She looked at it.

The sun was not yet up but the sky was lightening in the east, the darkness thinning to grey-blue above the river trees. The mound's shadow shortened slightly as she watched. Around her the field was still, the corn cut and gone, the stalks broken and lying flat, the ground between them dark with dew. Somewhere behind her the community was beginning to wake. She could hear it, faintly, the sounds of a fire being coaxed, a child's voice, a dog.

She did not know yet what she would do.

That was the honest thing, and she held it without flinching. She did not know. She was twenty years old and she was standing at the base of a mound that was older than anyone's memory, and she had a piece of knowledge that sat in her chest like a stone. Two truths that cancelled each other. Two people she could not afford to lose. One silence that was already costing her something she could not yet name. She did not know what to do with any of it.

But she knew she would have to do something. The silence would not hold. Her father's satisfaction would not hold. Ahanu's patience, the patience of someone who is waiting to see what happens next before deciding what he thinks, that would not hold either. The season was turning. The next planting would come. The ceremonies would need to be scheduled, and someone would have to decide how, and the decision would be made in the space that her father's challenge had opened up, and she would be standing somewhere in that space whether she chose to be or not.

She pressed her hand against the mound one more time. Flat-palmed, deliberate. The cold earth under her fingers. The weight of it.

I know what I know, she thought. Not to the ancestors, not to anyone. To herself. A statement of fact, the way you note the depth of a river before you decide whether to cross it.

She knew what she knew. She did not yet know what to do with it. But she would not pretend she did not know it, and she would not spend another night sitting over dead coals with the unspoken argument moving in her chest like something looking for a way out.

Whatever it cost her, one of them, both of them, something she had not yet imagined, she was going to have to find a way to say it.

She turned from the mound and walked back toward the waking community, her feet wet with dew, the cold air on her face, the bird calling once more from the elder trees behind her as if it simply had something to say and had decided not to wait any longer.
