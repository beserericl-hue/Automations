The fever had taken her husband in November of the year before last. He had been dead two winters when the spring came and did not bring Saya's strength back with it.

She sat at the shelter's entrance with the flap drawn back two fingers' width, enough to let in the grey light but not the wind. Behind her, Lena slept on the raised platform, wrapped in the wool stroud blanket they had traded three baskets of dried corn for the previous September. Before the assembly. Before the journey home alone through mud that had sucked at her moccasins like something hungry.

The petition lay across her knees. The last copy. The one her husband had not buried.

She had not meant to keep it. When he wrapped the other two in oilskin and went out to the mound in the dark, she had stood in the doorway watching the candle he carried until the trees took it. She had told herself she would burn this one. Paper was dangerous in the wrong hands, and whose hands were wrong in this country was never certain. But she had not burned it. She had folded it along its existing creases, tucked it inside the old account book her father had left her, the one with the marbled board cover and the blank leaves at the back, and she had carried it home under her coat against her ribs, where it warmed itself against her skin like something alive.

Now she read it in the grey light. Not for hope. She understood, finally and without grief, that there was no hope in this particular document. The assembly had received it. The clerk had written it in the journal with his ink-stained fingers. The president in his large chair had said *the matter will be considered* in a voice that meant the matter would not be considered, and Harwood with his comfortable coat had asked, pleasantly, whether the Piscataway constituted a recognized body capable of holding a land claim under provincial law, and the answer to that question was already decided before Saya had opened her mouth.

She read it for something else. She read it the way she had watched her father copy documents for the Jesuit. Not to understand the meaning, which he already knew, but to fix the shapes of the words in his hands, in his eyes, so that the next time he needed them they would come without effort.

*Know all men by these presents.*

She said it quietly, her breath a small cloud in the cold air.

*Know all men by these presents that we the undersigned, being the ancient possessors of the land lying along the west branch of the Patuxent River and the high ground therein described.*

Her father had taught her that phrase. *Ancient possessors.* He had found it in a ruling by a judge named Notley, dead twenty years or more, whose words had been reversed by the men who came after him but who had been copied into the record twice in the reversing, so that the language survived its own defeat. Her father had laughed at that. *They tried to bury it and made two copies,* he had said. *That is how the law works against itself.*

She had not understood what he meant when she was eleven. She understood now.

The petition went on for three pages in her own hand, the letters close and even, the lines straight without ruling because she had learned to hold a line by eye from copying her father's work. She had written it in the formal language of the colonial court. *Humbly beseeching,* Tomah had insisted, and he had been right, though the words had cost her something to write. She had known that the assembly would not read a document that did not speak in its own register. She had known that, and she had written it, and the assembly had read it and done nothing, and she had been right about all of it and it had not been enough.

She turned to the second page. The boundary description: *north from the great rock at the branch's fork, eighty poles to a marked hickory, thence east forty degrees to the old Indian path, thence following said path to the high ground where the people's dead are kept.*

She stopped.

*Where the people's dead are kept.*

She had written that herself, without Tomah's help. The assembly clerk had not questioned it. He had copied it into the journal without pausing, his pen moving steadily, and she had watched his hand and thought: *he does not know what he is writing. He does not know what that ground is. He thinks it is a description of a place. He does not know it is a name.*

Lena made a sound in her sleep. A small sound, not distress, just the noise a child makes when a dream shifts. Saya did not turn. She listened until the sound stopped and the shelter was quiet again except for the wind moving through the pine boughs on the roof.

She looked at the petition in her hands. Three pages of rag paper, the iron gall ink already browning at the edges where it had been thick. The linen tape she had tied it with had frayed at one end. In twenty years it would be yellow. In fifty it might be unreadable. In a hundred it would be ash, or dust, or nothing.

The mound would still be there.

She thought about what her husband had said the night before he buried the copies. He had said: *the paper is for them, so they cannot say they were not told. The mound is for us, so we cannot forget.* Then he had said the thing she had not answered, because she had not known how: *what do we give Lena?*

She had not answered. She had held the candle while he went out into the dark, and she had not answered.

Now, alone in the grey light with the petition across her knees, she thought she understood what he had meant. Not the document. Not the paper, which would brown and crack and be lost. Not even the mound, which she could not carry.

What she could give Lena was the knowledge of how to read a document like this one. How to find the phrase that had survived its own reversal. How to see where a boundary description named a perishable tree that might already be gone, and what that absence meant in a court. How to hear the word *waste* in a colonial grant and know that *waste* meant land the crown wanted to believe was empty, and that the belief was a legal instrument, not a fact, and that a literate person who knew the difference had a weapon the crown had not intended to give her.

Her father had given her that. He had given it to her not by explaining it but by teaching her to read the documents themselves, by sitting beside her at the table with a candle between them and saying: *sound it out. What does that word mean? What does it mean in this sentence? What would it mean if the tree described in line four were gone?*

She could do that. She could do that for Lena.

She folded the petition along its creases and held it in both hands for a moment. The rag paper was smooth and slightly cold, substantial, heavier than it looked. She thought of her father's hands on documents like this one. She thought of her husband's hands, which would not hold anything again.

Then she opened the account book and slid the petition back inside, between the marbled board cover and the first blank leaf, and she closed it and set it beside her on the cold ground.

Lena was still asleep. Outside, a crow called once and went quiet. The grey light was getting thinner, which meant the day was ending, not beginning. She had lost track of the hours. She would need to start the fire soon.

She sat for another moment with her hand on the account book's cover, feeling the slight ridge where the petition lay inside it.

*What do we give Lena?*

She knew the answer now. Knowing it and doing it were two different things, and Lena was four years old, and there was a long distance between four years old and the kind of reading Saya needed to teach her, and Saya did not know how much time she had.

She had not let herself think about that. She thought about it now, briefly, in the way she allowed herself to think about hard things. Directly, without flinching, and then she put it down.

She did not know how much time she had. That was true. It was also true that her father had not known how much time he had when he sat her down at the table with the candle between them, and he had begun anyway, and she was here.

She got up and started the fire., -

The ground thawed in early April, and that was when Saya began.

She chose a patch of bare earth near the fire pit, where the frost had pulled back and left the soil dark and workable. She found a stick the right length, not so long it was unwieldy in a child's hand, not so short it cramped the fingers, and she stripped the bark from it and smoothed the tip against a flat stone until it was blunt enough not to break at pressure.

Lena watched from the doorway of the shelter, her feet in her moccasins, her wool shift already damp at the hem from the morning grass.

"Come here," Saya said.

Lena came. She had her mother's way of moving. Not cautious, not reckless, but deliberate, as if she had already decided where each foot would land.

Saya crouched and drew a letter in the dirt. A large, slow *G*, the curve of it opening to the right.

"What is that?"

"G," Lena said. She had seen letters before. Saya had been making them for her since winter, in the ash at the edge of the hearth, in the frost on the shelter wall.

"Good. Now this." Saya added *R*, then *A*, then *N*, then *T*. Five letters in a row, each one clear.

Lena sounded them together the way Saya had taught her. Not the letter names, but the sounds. "Gr." A pause. "Grant."

"Grant," Saya said. "Say it again."

"Grant."

"Do you know what it means?"

Lena shook her head. She was not embarrassed by not knowing. That was one of the things Saya loved about her. She did not perform understanding she did not have.

"It means someone wrote a paper that said a piece of ground belonged to them. The governor signed it. The paper said: this ground is granted to this man, and to his heirs and assigns forever." Saya said the phrase the way her father had said it to her, without inflection, the way you say a thing that is true whether you like it or not. "Do you know what *assigns* means?"

Lena shook her head again.

"Neither did I, the first time. It means people he gives it to. People he chooses." Saya handed her the stick. "Write it."

Lena took the stick in her right hand and drew the *G* with her tongue between her teeth, concentrating. The curve went too wide. She looked at Saya's letters and drew it again in the dirt beside the first attempt, smaller, tighter.

"Better," Saya said.

Lena wrote *R*, then *A*. The *N* gave her trouble. The second leg came out too short. She looked at Saya's *N* and tried again.

"Grant," she said, when she had all five letters.

"Grant," Saya agreed. She took the stick back and wrote the next word. *S-U-R-V-E-Y*.

"Sur." Lena sounded the first syllable. "Survey."

"Survey. A man walked the ground with a chain and measured it and wrote down what he found. He wrote: *north forty-five degrees east, eighty poles, to a marked hickory.* Do you know what a pole is?"

"A stick?"

"A measure. Sixteen and a half feet. They used a chain of one hundred links to count them." Saya drew a horizontal line in the dirt and marked intervals on it with small notches. "The chain was this long, and they counted how many times it fit between one tree and the next. Then they wrote it down and said: this is where the boundary is." She paused. "But the hickory tree they marked. What happens to a hickory tree?"

Lena thought about it. "It falls down?"

"It falls down. Or it burns. Or it dies and rots and there is nothing left but a stump, and then the stump rots too, and then there is nothing." Saya smoothed the dirt with her palm. "And then the boundary is wherever someone says it is. The person who can read the document and say *the tree is gone and the boundary is therefore uncertain* has something to work with."

Lena was watching her with the particular attention she gave to things she found interesting, which was not the same as the attention she gave to things she thought she should find interesting. This was real attention. Her eyes steady, her hands still.

"Write it," Saya said. "Survey."

Lena wrote it. The *V* came out angular and sharp, which was not wrong, only a child's hand finding its way to the shape.

They worked through the morning. *Grant. Survey. Aforementioned. Humbly beseeching. Heirs and assigns forever. Waste and unoccupied. Metes and bounds.* Saya said each phrase and then explained it, the way her father had. Not what the words meant in isolation, but what they meant inside the document, inside the argument, inside the law that the colonists had built around themselves like a wall. *Waste* was the one that made Lena frown.

"But it isn't waste," Lena said. "We live there."

"We do."

"So why does it say waste?"

"Because if they write *waste*, they can say no one owned it before them. And if no one owned it before them, then the grant is clean." Saya looked at her daughter. "That is what the word is for. Not to describe the ground. To describe the ground as they need it to be."

Lena looked at the word she had written in the dirt, *W-A-S-T-E*, and then she dragged her foot through it. Not angry. Deliberate.

Saya did not say anything. She smoothed the dirt and wrote *survey* again, and Lena wrote it after her, and they went on., -

The names came in the mornings, before the fire was fully caught.

Saya had started it without planning to. The first morning she had simply begun speaking while Lena sat with her bowl of hominy, still half-asleep, her hair loose and tangled from the night. Saya had said the names the way she always said them inside her own head. In order, without rushing, going back as far as the names went.

Her mother's mother. Her mother's mother's mother. The woman before her whose name Saya's grandmother had given her, and the woman before that, and the woman before that, going back to the names that had no stories attached to them anymore, only the names themselves, which her father had made her memorize when she was younger than Lena was now.

Lena had looked up from her hominy.

"Who are you talking to?" she said.

"No one," Saya said. "I am saying the names."

"What names?"

"The names of the women who were here before us." Saya poured water into the pot and set it over the fire. "Eat."

Lena ate. Saya said the names again from the beginning, slower this time, so Lena could hear them as separate things. Not a sound, but a list, each name its own weight.

The next morning she did it again. And the morning after that.

By the fourth morning, Lena had stopped looking up. She just ate her hominy and listened, her spoon moving in the bowl, her eyes on the fire. She was listening. Saya could tell by the way she went still at certain names, the ones that had a rhythm to them, or an unusual sound.

On the fifth morning, Saya paused after the third name.

Lena said the fourth name without being asked. She said it quietly, into her bowl, as if she were not sure she had it right.

She had it right.

"Good," Saya said, and went on.

She did not explain why they were doing it. She did not say: *these names are what we have when the documents are gone. These names are the record that no clerk can lose and no fire can burn.* Lena was five. Explanations could come later. What mattered now was the names in the right order, said every morning, until they were as ordinary as the sound of the fire and as impossible to forget as hunger.

Her father had done the same thing. He had not called it anything. He had just said the names, every morning, in the same order, until Saya knew them without knowing she knew them. The way she knew which mushrooms were safe to eat, which she had also never been formally taught., -

In the afternoons, when the light was good, she brought Lena to the mound.

It was a short walk from the shelter. Through the stand of oaks, past the place where the creek bent south, up the slight rise where the ground was drier and the leaf mold thicker under the trees. The mound was not large. It rose perhaps to the height of a man's chest at its highest point, and it was wide enough that a child could not run across it in fewer than a dozen steps. Saya had been coming here since she was younger than Lena.

She let Lena run up the side of it the first time, because Lena was five and the slope was an invitation. She watched her daughter scramble to the top and stand there with her arms out, pleased with herself.

"Good," Saya said. "Now come down and look."

They walked around it slowly, the way Saya's mother had walked her around it. Saya pointed to the slight depression near the eastern edge, barely visible now, filled in with leaf mold and the roots of a young cedar that had seeded itself there.

"Someone is buried there," she said. "An old burial. Older than your grandmother's grandmother."

Lena looked at the depression. "How do you know?"

"The soil is darker. And the cedar chose that spot." Saya crouched and pressed her fingers into the earth at the cedar's base. The soil was soft, almost black, different from the reddish clay two feet away. "When ground has been opened and closed again, it holds differently. It breathes differently. You can learn to feel it."

Lena crouched beside her and pressed her own fingers into the soil.

"It's soft," she said.

"Yes."

"Who is it?"

"I don't know the name. It was before the names we have." Saya stood and looked at the mound's crown, where the leaf mold was thickest and the light came through the oak canopy in long, pale bars. "But she is part of the count. She is one of the ones before the ones whose names we say."

Lena considered this with the seriousness she brought to things that were genuinely new to her. Then she said: "Does she know we're here?"

Saya thought about how to answer that. She thought about what her mother would have said, and what her father would have said, and what her husband would have said. Her husband, who had been dead two winters now, whose absence was still a shape in the air beside her, like a door that was always slightly ajar.

"She knows the mound is still here," Saya said. "That is enough."

Lena accepted this. She stood up and wiped her hands on her shift and looked at the cedar tree, which was no taller than she was, its branches still thin and slightly reddish at the tips.

"Will she be here when I'm old?" Lena said.

"Yes," Saya said. "And when your daughter is old. And when her daughter is old."

Lena seemed to find this satisfying. She went back up the side of the mound, more carefully this time, not running, and stood at the top again and looked out through the oaks toward the creek.

Saya stood at the base and watched her. The afternoon light was going gold and flat, the way it did in April when the sun was still low. A woodpecker was working somewhere in the trees to the north, the sound of it carrying clean and sharp through the quiet.

She thought: *she will remember this. She will remember the cedar and the soft soil and the light. She will not remember being taught. She will only remember knowing.*

That was how it worked. That was how it had always worked. Her father had not taught her to read the documents. He had sat beside her until she could read them, and then she had always been able to read them, and she did not remember the learning, only the knowing.

She would do the same for Lena. The dirt lessons, the morning names, the mound. All of it would become ordinary, and then it would become knowledge, and then it would become the kind of knowledge that did not feel like knowledge because it had always been there.

She only needed enough time.

She did not let herself think about that too long. She looked up at her daughter standing on the mound's crown with her arms out again, her wool shift catching the last of the afternoon light, her face turned toward the creek.

"Lena," she said.

Lena looked down.

"Tell me the first name."

Lena said it. She said it clearly, without hesitation, the way she said things she had already decided she knew.

"Good," Saya said. "Now the next.", -

The winter Lena was nine, Saya brought her back to the mound in snow.

Not deep snow. A finger's width, maybe two, the kind that fell in the night and lay still in the cold morning air without melting. It outlined everything. The mound's crown stood up from the white ground like a dark shoulder, the leaf mold and cedar needles showing through where the snow had not settled evenly. The oaks were bare and the light came straight through them, flat and grey, the way December light did. Not warm, not directional, just present.

Lena walked beside her, not running today. She was nine now and had learned that the mound required a different kind of attention than most places. She had learned this without being told, the way children learn the tone of a room before they learn the words for it.

Saya stopped at the base of the mound and did not go up. She stood and looked at it.

"Today I am going to tell you something I have not told you before," she said. "Not the names. Not the soil. Something else."

Lena waited. Her breath made small clouds in the cold air.

"Something is buried here that is not a person," Saya said. "Two things. One is older. One your father put there himself."

Lena's face shifted. Not surprise, exactly, but a sharpening. "Father put something in the mound?"

"I asked him to."

She had not spoken of it since the day her husband buried the oilskin packet, the winter before he died. She had thought about it often, standing at the fire, grinding corn, walking the eastern boundary of the field, but she had not spoken it aloud. Now she found that the words came without difficulty, the way things come that have been held a long time and are ready.

"A document," she said. "Written on paper. It named this ground, the mound, the creek bend, the high field, the oak stand, and it said this land belongs to us. It said our people have lived here since before the memory of any living person. It named the ancestors. It named the boundaries in the way the English name boundaries, with distances and directions and marked trees." She paused. "I carried it to the colonial assembly. I stood before the men who make the laws and I read it to them and I gave it to them."

Lena was very still. "What did they say?"

"They said they would consider it." Saya's voice stayed level. "They did not consider it."

The woodpecker that had been working the dead oak to the north went quiet. The silence it left was total for a moment. No wind, no creek sound, nothing.

"So we buried it," Saya said. "Not to hide it. To keep it."

Lena looked at the mound. Her eyes moved across the snow-covered crown, down to the eastern depression where the cedar stood, its thin branches holding small sleeves of white.

"Why didn't you dig it up?" she said. "When the petition failed. Why not take it back and try again?"

It was the right question. Saya had known, somewhere, that Lena would eventually ask it, and she had been turning the answer over for years without knowing she was preparing it.

"Come here," she said.

She crouched at the mound's base and pressed her palm flat against the snow-dusted earth, just where the slope began. Lena crouched beside her.

"Put your hand here."

Lena put her hand beside Saya's. The ground was cold, the soil just barely yielding under the frost.

"The document underground," Saya said, "is proof that the claim was made. That on a specific day, in a specific year, a woman of this community stood before the colonial assembly and said: *this ground is ours, and we have the names and the boundaries to prove it.* That proof does not become more true if we dig it up. It does not become less true if we leave it. It is in the ground the way a seed is in the ground. It does not need air to be real."

Lena's hand was still flat against the earth. She was listening with her whole body, the way she listened when something was going into her that she knew she would need later.

"But," Saya said, "a document in the ground cannot speak. It cannot walk into a room. It cannot answer a question." She looked at her daughter. "That is what you are for."

Lena met her eyes.

"The document in your memory," Saya said, "is proof that the claim was never abandoned. That after the assembly refused us, a woman came home and taught her daughter the boundaries, the names, the mound, the logic of the layers, and that daughter will teach her daughter, and so on, until the day someone asks: *was this claim ever given up?* And the answer will be in the mouth of whoever is standing there."

She stood. Her knees ached in the cold. They had started doing that this winter, a new thing, one of the body's quiet announcements.

"Two tracks," she said. "The buried document says: *we made the claim.* The living memory says: *we never stopped.* One without the other is incomplete. The assembly can ignore a document in the ground. They cannot ignore a living person who knows everything the document says and more."

Lena stood too. She looked at the mound for a long moment, her face serious in the grey winter light.

"What is the other thing?" she said. "You said two things were buried. One is the document. What is the other?"

"A bead," Saya said. "Your grandmother's. Blue glass, from a trading party that came south along the Potomac before I was born. She wore it on a cord." She touched her own collarbone briefly, where the cord had been. "I gave it to your father to bury with the document. So the ground would know both things were ours. The written claim and the older claim. The English words and the older language."

Lena was quiet for a moment. Then: "She was from the north?"

"She came south and stayed. That is all I know of it."

"Do we say her name in the list?"

"Yes. She is the name before mine."

Lena nodded slowly, committing this, slotting it into the sequence she already carried. Saya watched her do it. The small movement of the eyes that meant something was being placed precisely, not just held.

The cold was settling deeper now. Saya's breath came in long, visible streams. She looked at the mound one more time. The snow on its crown was beginning to blue in the fading light. The cedar at the eastern edge stood absolutely still, its thin branches unchanged from the summer they had first crouched beside it together.

"There is something else you need to understand," Saya said. "About the document. About the language it uses."

Lena looked at her.

"The English have a phrase they put in every land grant: *heirs and assigns forever.*" She said it carefully, the way she said legal phrases. Not performing them, but carrying them precisely, the way you carry water in a vessel with no margin for spilling. "It means the land passes to children, and to their children, and cannot be taken away by time alone. They use it to protect their own claims."

"But not ours," Lena said.

"Not ours. They said our petition did not constitute a claim a recognized body could hold." Saya's voice did not harden. She had long since burned away the anger and kept only the information. "But the phrase itself is correct. *Heirs and assigns forever.* That is what we are. That is what you are. You are the heir. The assign. The forever."

Lena said the phrase back to her, quietly, in English, with the careful precision she brought to English words she was still learning to trust.

*Heirs and assigns forever.*

"Good," Saya said.

She put her hand briefly on Lena's shoulder. Not a long gesture, not a ceremony. Just a hand on a shoulder in the cold.

"Now tell me the names," she said. "All of them. From the beginning."

Lena began. Her voice was steady and clear in the winter air, each name falling into the silence after the last, the sequence moving backward through time the way the mound's layers moved downward through the earth. Each one holding the one beneath it, each one held by the one above.

Saya listened. She did not correct. Lena had them right., -

The cold came differently in late autumn of Lena's twelfth year. Not the dry, clarifying cold of January, but a wet cold that settled into the joints and stayed. Her husband had been two years dead by then. The cold was Saya's alone to feel.

The fever came in November, the way fevers did in wet autumns. By nightfall of the first day her skin was dry and hot to the touch, and by morning she was speaking in the loose, wandering way of a woman whose thoughts had come untethered from the moment. Lena sent for Mara and kept the fire high.

She knew what to do. She kept her mother warm. She kept water at her lips. She put wet cloth at her wrists and her throat and changed it when it dried. She did not sleep more than an hour at a stretch.

On the sixth day the fever dropped enough that Saya could sit upright, and she called Lena close.

"Bring the documents," she said.

Lena brought them from the shelf where they were stored in oilskin: the copies Saya had made over the years, the Notley ruling, the land record from 1666, the petition draft she had kept for herself. She laid them on the mat beside Saya.

Saya's hands were not steady enough to hold them. She told Lena to hold the Notley ruling open.

The paper was brown at the edges, the iron gall ink faded to the color of old blood. Lena held it flat and Saya looked at it, though she did not need to look. She had the words in her memory the way she had the ancestor names, in order, without gaps.

"Find the phrase," Saya said. "*Ancient possessors.*"

Lena's finger moved across the page, tracing the secretary hand with the careful slowness she still needed for the older script. She had learned to read from this document, among others. Sounding out the long-s letters that looked like f, the abbreviated *sd* for *said*, the Latin that Saya had translated phrase by phrase over five years of winter evenings. Her finger stopped.

"Here."

"Read it."

"*Being the ancient possessors of the said high ground, their claim upon the land descends by right of prior occupation and cannot be extinguished by mere, *" Lena paused. "*Mere grant or patent.*"

"That is Judge Notley's language," Saya said. "He is dead. His bench reversed most of what he wrote. But the reversal is in the record too, which means Notley's words appear twice. Once when he wrote them, once when the new bench copied them in order to say he was wrong. You understand what that means."

Lena's eyes moved up from the page. "His words are harder to remove than if they had only been written once."

"Yes." Saya closed her eyes for a moment. The fever was not gone. It was only waiting. "The phrase *ancient possessors* is in the court record twice. Anyone who reads carefully will find it. If you ever speak to a court, or if Lena's daughter ever speaks to a court, that phrase is the ground you stand on. It is not enough by itself. But it is a phrase a judge used, which means it is a phrase that can be used again."

Lena set the document down and looked at her mother. "Who else knows this?"

"No one who will tell it the way I am telling you." Saya opened her eyes. "The knowledge lives here." She touched her own chest, then Lena's. "And now here.", -

On the fifth day the fever rose again and Saya could not speak clearly. She heard Lena moving around the room. Water, fire, the soft sound of the door. She drifted in and out of a sleep that was not restful. She dreamed of the assembly hall: the clerk with ink on his fingers, the man in the large chair with his reddened face, the moment she had pressed her palm to the page. She dreamed of her father's voice reading aloud from a document he was copying for the Jesuit, his finger moving under the words the way Lena's finger moved now.

On the sixth day she surfaced again, briefly, and found Lena sitting close with a candle lit against the grey afternoon.

"The Jesuit," Saya said. Her voice was rough.

Lena leaned in. "What?"

"My father copied documents for a Jesuit. His name was Father Andries Kern. He was at the mission on the Patuxent for eleven years. He kept records of baptisms and land transactions in a bound volume with a red cover. If that volume still exists, if it was not burned or taken, it will have my father's name in it as a copyist. *Andries Kern.* Say it."

"Andries Kern."

"If you ever need to prove your grandfather's presence in this county, that is where to look. A man named in a Jesuit's record cannot be said to have never been here."

Lena repeated the name twice more, quietly, the way she repeated the ancestor names. Not as a question but as a fixing, a setting of the word into the place where it would stay.

"The clerk," Saya said. "At the Provincial Court. When the petition was entered."

"Thomas Ware."

Saya looked at her. "I told you that."

"Last winter. You told me Ware entered it during the secretary's absence. You said he owed a tobacco debt."

"Twelve pounds of leaf," Saya said. "To your father's cousin. The debt was forgiven. Ware may still be at the court. If he is, he will not want to remember. But the entry is in the record book regardless of what Ware wants. The petition is there. It is entered. It exists." She stopped. The effort of speaking had brought the fever's heat up behind her eyes. "If you ever go to that court, you go knowing the petition is in the record. You do not ask whether it is there. You ask to see the volume for the winter session of 1697. You already know what you will find."

Lena nodded. Her face was very still.

"Don't look afraid," Saya said.

"I'm not afraid."

"You look afraid."

"I look like I'm trying to remember everything at once."

Saya almost smiled. "That is different," she said. "That is good.", -

On the seventh day Mara came and sat with Saya for an hour. She brought dried sassafras root boiled in water, which smelled of the creek bottom in summer, and she held the cup while Saya drank. They did not talk much. Mara had the quality of the very old. She did not fill silences with words, only with presence, and Saya was grateful for it.

After Mara left, Lena came and sat in the same place. The candle had burned low. Outside, the early dark of late autumn was settling, and the redwings had gone quiet.

"Tell me the names," Saya said.

Lena straightened. She set her hands in her lap.

She began with the oldest name Saya had ever given her. The name at the far end of the chain, the one Saya's father had given Saya, which his mother had given him, which went back before the English, before the Jesuits, before the land patents and the metes and bounds and the phrase *ancient possessors*. A woman's name, worn smooth by generations of repetition until only its shape remained, like a stone that has been in a river a long time. Saya did not know what the name had meant in the language it came from. She only knew it was the oldest name, and that it belonged to a woman who had known the mound when it was younger than it was now, and that the name must come first.

Lena said it. Then the next name. Then the next.

She moved through them in order, each name carrying the small identification Saya had taught her. Not just the name but the anchor, the thing that made the name a person rather than a sound. *Ranu, who told the stories. Yanu, who buried the pendant and spoke her daughter's name over the ground.* The names from the long middle distance, the ones Saya knew only as names without stories, because the stories had worn away and only the names remained. Then the names that had stories again. Ahanu, Waya, Kessik, Pell, the names that came with weather and grief and the memory of the mound's older layers. Then the names that were closer, that had faces attached, that Saya's father had known or half-known. Then Saya's own grandparents. Then her parents.

Then: *Saya.*

Lena paused. Not because she had forgotten. Saya could see she had not forgotten. The pause was right. The way you pause at a threshold before you cross it.

Then: *Saya's daughter Lena.*

The names ended.

The room was very quiet.

"You didn't miss one," Saya said.

"No.", -

The fever lifted by the end of the week. Saya's knees ached when she rose, and her hands were slower than they had been, and she tired by midday in a way she had not before. But she was upright, and the fire was burning, and Lena was beside her.

Three days after she was well enough to walk to the mound, she took Lena there.

The ground was frozen in the shadows and soft in the sun, the oak leaves matted flat under a skim of old frost. The cedar at the eastern edge was bare now, its thin branches grey against the pale sky. The mound itself looked the same as it always did. Patient, unchanged, keeping what it kept.

Lena stood beside her and did not speak.

"There is something I did not tell you," Saya said. "About the petition. The eastern boundary."

"The line to the Wekomi land."

Saya looked at her daughter.

"I read the petition," Lena said. "The copy you kept. I read it last winter, when you were asleep and I was watching the fire." She said it plainly, without apology. "The line doesn't close. There's a gap between our eastern boundary and the Wekomi claim. I didn't know if you had left it out by accident or on purpose."

The fever had taken something from Saya, some reserve she had not known she was drawing on. She felt its absence now. Not weakness, exactly, but a kind of clarity, the way a room looks after you have moved the furniture and can see the floor.

She had meant to explain this when Lena was older. She had told herself there was time. She had told herself Lena was not ready. She looked at her daughter, twelve years old, standing straight in the cold light with the ancestor names in her mouth and the petition's gap already known to her, and understood that she had been wrong about that.

"On purpose," Saya said. "I left the Wekomi name out of the petition because I was afraid the assembly would use the dispute between our claims as grounds to dismiss both. I thought I was protecting the petition." She stopped. "I should have told you sooner."

Lena was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "The Wekomi elder. Did he know?"

"I told him. He said he would think on it. Then your father sickened, and the winter came, and I never finished the conversation."

"Is he still alive?"

"I don't know."

Lena looked at the mound. Her face had the quality Saya had always trusted in her. Not the absence of feeling but the discipline of it, the gathering inward before the response.

"Then I'll find out," Lena said. "And if he's alive, I'll finish the conversation."

"The eastern line needs to be closed," Saya said. "If the document is ever found, the gap will be found with it. You'll need to know what to say about it."

"I know what to say. I'll say my mother left it open because she was protecting the petition, and that the gap was always meant to be closed, and that I am closing it now." Lena looked up from the mound. "Is that the truth?"

"It is close enough to the truth that it can be said plainly," Saya said. "Yes."

She put her hand briefly on Lena's shoulder. Not a long gesture, not a ceremony. Just a hand on a shoulder in the cold.

"Now tell me the names," she said. "All of them. From the beginning."

Lena began. Her voice was steady and clear in the winter air, each name falling into the silence after the last, the sequence moving backward through time the way the mound's layers moved downward through the earth. Each one holding the one beneath it, each one held by the one above.

Saya listened. She did not correct. Lena had them right., -

The fever came back in early spring, when the ice on the creek was pulling back from the banks and the first redwings were calling from the cattails. Saya woke one morning and could not get warm. She built the fire higher and still could not get warm, and by midday she knew.

She did not say anything to Lena that first day. She went about her work. The water, the meal, the small repairs that always waited. She told herself it was the damp, the change of season, the tiredness that had lived in her bones since the autumn illness. By evening her hands were shaking when she lifted the pot, and Lena was watching her with the gathered, still look, and Saya sat down and said, "I am going to be ill for a while."

Lena said, "How long."

"I don't know."

Lena said nothing else. She took the pot from Saya and finished the meal and made her mother eat, and then she banked the fire with the careful efficiency she had learned from watching Saya watch Mara, and Saya thought: *she is ready for more than I have given her. And I have given her most of it.*

That was the first night., -

By the third day the fever had its full weight on her. She lay on the sleeping mat with the wool stroud blanket pulled to her chin and felt the heat moving through her in waves, each one leaving her a little less certain of the room's edges. Lena sat beside her and kept a wet cloth on her forehead and did not leave except to fetch water.

On the fourth morning Saya woke with her mind clear and the fever briefly banked, the way a fire goes quiet before it finds new wood. She knew this feeling. She had seen it in her husband. She understood what it meant.

She called Lena close and they said the names together. Saya's voice rough and slow, Lena's steady. The whole sequence from the oldest to the newest, the way they had said them every morning for eight years. When they reached the end Saya was quiet for a moment.

"Say it again," she said. "Alone."

Lena said it alone. Each name in its place, each anchor attached. Her father's name between Saya and herself, placed where it belonged.

"Good," Saya said. "You have all of it.", -

She lasted nine more days.

There were two good days in the middle, when the fever dropped and she recognized Lena and held her hand and said her name twice. Once in Piscataway and once in English, as if she wanted to be sure both versions of her daughter knew she saw her. On the second good day she asked whether the cedar at the eastern edge of the mound was still standing.

"Still standing," Lena said.

She nodded, satisfied, and slept again.

The fever came back on the seventh day, higher than before, and did not drop.

On the ninth day, near dawn, her breathing changed. Lena was beside her, her hand on her mother's hand. Outside, the first birds were starting. A single crow, then silence, then a thrush somewhere in the oaks, making its three-note call in the grey dark.

Saya died in the space between two breaths, quietly, the way a candle goes out when the tallow is simply gone.

Lena sat with her for a while. The room was very still. Outside, the thrush continued its three notes, patient and indifferent and beautiful.

Then she rose and put more wood on the fire and went to find Mara., -

That afternoon, when the community had come and the prayers had been said and the preparations begun, Lena went to the mound alone.

The ground was frozen in the shadows and soft in the sun. The cedar at the eastern edge was bare, its thin branches grey against the pale sky. The mound itself looked the same as it always did.

She stood at its base for a moment. Then she pressed her palm flat against the earth where the slope began, the way her mother had shown her, the way her mother's mother had shown her mother.

She said the names once, alone, in the cold air. All of them, in order, from the oldest to the newest. When she reached Saya's name she placed it where it belonged, after her father's name, before herself, and kept going.

*Saya. Saya's daughter Lena.*

The names ended. The silence after them was large and clean and entirely her own.

She kept her palm against the earth a moment longer. Under the frozen ground, the document lay in its oilskin with its incomplete eastern line, patient as the mound itself, waiting for a question that had not yet been asked.

Lena would ask it. She knew the gap, and she knew why it was there, and she knew what it would take to close it. Her mother had given her that. Her mother had given her all of it. The names and the documents and the logic of the layers and the phrase *heirs and assigns forever* and the understanding that two tracks were better than one and that a living person who knew everything the document said was harder to ignore than a document alone.

She stood up. Her knees did not ache yet. That would come later, her mother had said, one of the body's quiet announcements. The spring light was flat and pale through the bare oaks, and somewhere in the trees to the north the woodpecker was working again, the sound of it carrying clean and sharp through the quiet.

She turned and walked back through the frozen leaves toward the shelter, where Mara was waiting and there was work still to be done.

Behind her, the cedar stood at the mound's eastern edge, its thin branches absolutely still in the cold air, unchanged.
