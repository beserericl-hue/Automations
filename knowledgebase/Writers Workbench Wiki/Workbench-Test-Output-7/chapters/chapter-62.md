The porch light at Nora's house was still on when Tayak pulled in at six fifty-eight, a yellow bulb throwing a cone of light across the frost-stiffened grass. She sat in the car for a moment with the engine running. Through the kitchen window she could see two figures at the table. One was Nora's white-haired shape. The other was someone younger, dark-haired, leaning over something spread on the surface. A third coffee mug sat on the counter, already set out.

She cut the engine.

The notebook was on the passenger seat where she had put it the night before, the same green Rite in the Rain she used in the field, because Nora had said bring your notebook and Tayak had not been able to decide whether that meant a legal pad or something she could trust to get wet. She picked it up. Her hands were cold.

The front door opened before she reached the steps.

"She's been up since four," the young woman said, holding the door. She had a beaded lanyard around her neck and a blazer over a grey shirt, and she spoke quietly, the way people speak in a house where someone old is trying to hold something together. "I'm the tribal attorney. I drove up from La Plata last night."

"Tayak Moyaone."

"I know." She stepped back to let her in. "She told me about you."

The kitchen smelled of coffee and something else, the particular smell of old houses in November, dried wood and cold air pressed against warm walls. Nora was at the head of the table in her zip-front fleece, the blue one with the fraying cuffs, and she looked at Tayak with the expression she always used when Tayak was late for something, which was no expression at all.

"You're on time," Nora said. "Sit down."

Tayak sat. The table was a four-person kitchen table with a laminate surface worn pale at the corners. On it the attorney had arranged a yellow legal pad, a pen, a printed form on a clipboard, and a small digital recorder, black, the size of a deck of cards, its red indicator light already glowing.

"We haven't started yet," the attorney said, pulling out her own chair. "I wanted to wait for you." She glanced at the recorder. "I'll state the record when you're ready, Nora."

Nora wrapped both hands around her coffee mug. Her knuckles were enlarged, the skin thin over them, and she held the mug the way you hold something warm when the warmth is the point.

"I'm ready," she said.

Tayak opened her notebook to the first blank page and uncapped her pen. She told herself this was procedural. Nora had information the petition needed, and a tribal attorney was here to take it down properly, and that was all this was. The recorder's red light pulsed once and held steady.

The attorney leaned toward it. "The date is November fourteenth, 2024. The time is seven oh two a.m. This is the sworn affidavit of Nora Louise Moyaone, taken at her residence at, " She read out the Billingsley Road address. "Also present is Dr. Tayak Moyaone, Principal Investigator, Site 18CH-447, Charles County, Maryland. I am the tribal attorney of record, bar number, " She read the number from a card she had set beside the recorder. "Nora, please state your full name and confirm that you understand you are giving sworn testimony."

Nora said her name in full. Tayak had not known the middle name was Louise.

"I understand," Nora said.

The attorney made a mark on her legal pad. "Thank you. For the record, Tayak, your relationship to the affiant?"

"She's my grandmother," Tayak said. The word felt strange spoken into a recorder. Clinical. She wrote the date at the top of the notebook page and underlined it.

The attorney nodded, made another mark. "Nora, I'd like to begin with your knowledge of the site designated 18CH-447, which the state of Maryland has identified as a burial mound on the high ground adjacent to Mattawoman Creek. Can you tell me, in your own words, what you know about that site and how you came to know it?"

Nora set her mug down. She looked at the recorder for a moment, not at the attorney, not at Tayak, at the recorder, and something in her face settled, the way a face settles when it has decided.

"My grandmother told me," she said. "At this table. October 1962. I was nineteen years old."

Tayak wrote: October 1962. Ida. Kitchen table.

"Her name was Ida," Nora said. "Ida Frances Moyaone. She was a schoolteacher on this road for twenty-two years. She sat where you're sitting now." She looked at Tayak when she said it. "She told me she needed to tell me something before she lost the words for it. That's how she started. I didn't know what she meant until she was done."

The attorney wrote without looking up. The HVAC clicked on somewhere in the house, a low hum that rose and steadied.

"She told me about the mound," Nora said. "She told me about the names. She traced them on a piece of flour-sack cloth. She'd drawn rings on it, like a cross-section, each ring a generation, and she went through them from the outside in." She paused. "I'm going to do that now. For the record. I'm going to name every generation I know."

Tayak's pen was already moving.

"The outermost ring is Ida herself," Nora said. "Then her mother. Then her mother's mother, who was the one who told Ida's grandmother, who told Ida." She stopped. "You need to understand something before I go further. These aren't names I found in a book. These are names that were spoken. Generation to generation. Some of them are names I've held for sixty-two years without saying them to anyone except at the mound, alone, in the dark, because there was no one left to say them to who would understand what they meant."

Tayak stopped writing.

She had not expected that sentence. She had expected a recitation, names and dates, the kind of structured oral genealogy she had read about in NAGPRA consultation documents, traditional knowledge formatted for legal intake. She had not expected sixty-two years without saying them to anyone.

She looked at her grandmother's face and understood, for the first time, that this was not a thing Nora had been withholding. It was a thing Nora had been carrying alone, in the dark, for longer than Tayak had been alive.

"Nora," the attorney said gently, "take your time."

"I have taken my time," Nora said, without sharpness. "Sixty-two years is enough time." She picked up her mug, drank, set it down. "The name before Ida's grandmother is Tessa. Before Tessa is Matta. Before Matta is Saya." She looked at Tayak. "You know that name."

"From the petition," Tayak said. "The 1697 petition."

"The petition Saya wrote," Nora said. "With Okwi. And before Saya, " She stopped. Her hands tightened around the mug. "Before Saya there are names that go back further than the English. Names that Ida said carefully, the way you say something you're afraid of dropping."

Tayak wrote the names as Nora spoke them. Her handwriting was smaller than usual, tighter, the letters pressed close together as if she were trying to fit more than the page could hold.

Outside, a car passed on Billingsley Road, its headlights sweeping briefly across the kitchen wall and gone.

"I need to ask you something," the attorney said, "for the record. These names, do you have personal knowledge of the chain of transmission? Can you describe how each generation received this knowledge from the one before?"

"I can describe what Ida told me," Nora said. "And what I know from my own life. That's what personal knowledge is."

"That's exactly right," the attorney said. She wrote something on the legal pad and underlined it. "Go ahead."

Nora looked at Tayak again. Not at the recorder. At Tayak.

"There's something else," she said. "Something I haven't told you. Something I should have told you a long time ago, and didn't, because, " She stopped. The pause was not uncertainty. It was the pause of a woman choosing her words the way you choose where to put your foot on uncertain ground. "Because I was afraid of what you'd do with it."

The recorder's red light held steady on the table between them.

Tayak kept her pen on the page. She did not ask what it was. She waited, the way her grandmother had taught her to wait at the creek bottom when the water was cloudy. Be still long enough and the silt settles and you can see the bottom.

"The quilt," Nora said. "You've seen it. In the front room."

"Yes."

"It's a map."

Tayak looked up.

"Ida made it," Nora said. "Started it in 1963, finished it in 1971. The pattern looks like a traditional diamond pattern. It's not. The colored squares are locations. The creek bends. The high ground. The mound's position relative to the road. The boundary markers from the 1697 petition." She turned the mug slowly in her hands. "And one other thing. A location that isn't in any document Ida ever saw. A location she put in the quilt because she was afraid that if she wrote it down, it would be used against us."

Tayak's pen had stopped moving.

"What location?" she said.

Nora looked at her steadily. "The second burial," she said. "There's a second site. Older than the mound you've been excavating. Ida knew about it from the chain. It's on the eastern slope, below the tree line. You've been working thirty feet north of it for two months."

The kitchen was very quiet. The HVAC hummed. Somewhere in the house a clock ticked.

Tayak looked at her notebook, at the names she had written, at the date underlined at the top of the page. She thought about the eastern transect. She thought about the cord-marked sherd recovered from the eastern face on September fourteenth, countersigned by Wren, logged in the field record. She thought about the slight depression in the ground she had noted and attributed to root disturbance, a notation she had made and moved past without following.

She thought about what it meant that she had been thirty feet north of a burial site for two months and had not known it.

"For the record," the attorney said quietly, "Nora, are you stating that you have knowledge of an unrecorded archaeological site adjacent to 18CH-447?"

"I'm stating what Ida told me," Nora said. "And what I've known since 1962." She looked at Tayak. "And I'm telling my granddaughter, who is the archaeologist on that ground, because she needs to know before the state reads the petition and sends someone else to look."

Tayak pressed her lips together. She was aware of the recorder. She was aware of the attorney watching her with the careful attention of someone who has learned to read a room quickly.

She wrote: Eastern slope. Below tree line. Depression, root disturbance? Recheck.

Then she wrote: Cord-marked sherd. Sept. 14. Eastern face.

Then she stopped writing and looked at her grandmother, and for a moment the recorder and the legal pad and the intake form on the clipboard were not there, and it was just the two of them at the kitchen table in the early morning, the porch light still on outside, the frost still on the grass.

"Why didn't you tell me before?" Tayak said.

It was not an accusation. It came out quieter than she intended.

Nora looked at her hands around the mug. "Because I didn't know if you were ready," she said. "And because, " Another pause, longer this time. "Because your mother was the one I should have told first. And I didn't. And then she was gone."

The clock ticked. The HVAC cycled off.

The attorney made no mark on the legal pad. She sat very still, looking at the recorder's red light, giving them the silence.

Tayak looked down at her notebook. The names were there in her own handwriting, Saya and Matta and Tessa and Ida, the chain running up the page in her small tight script. Her mother's name was not among them. Her mother's name was not anywhere on the page.

She uncapped her pen again.

"Keep going," she said to Nora. "Tell her everything."

Nora began with Saya.

Not the story of Saya. The chain of Saya. The precise, sequential recitation that Tayak had heard fragments of over years, in the way you hear a song through a wall, enough to know it exists, not enough to know the words.

"Saya," Nora said. "Daughter of a man who worked as a copyist for the Jesuits on the Patuxent. She knew how to read colonial documents. She filed a petition in 1695 and again in 1697, arguing prior occupation of the high ground. She buried two copies of the petition in the mound before she died. In oilskin. Her husband buried them. He died before she did, of fever, and his name goes between hers and their daughter's on the list."

The attorney typed without looking up. Her fingers moved steadily on the laptop keys.

"Saya's daughter was Lena," Nora said. "Lena was four years old when her mother started the petition work and seventeen when Saya died. She received the name-list and the knowledge of the mound's contents. She knew about the oilskin bundles. She knew the eastern boundary question had not been resolved with the Wekomi, and she was supposed to resolve it."

"Did she?" the attorney asked.

"I don't know," Nora said. "Ida didn't know either. The chain holds the task, not the outcome."

Tayak wrote: Lena. Eastern boundary. Wekomi. Unresolved?

She had heard Saya's name from Nora before, once, in passing, the way you hear a name at a funeral that belongs to someone you never met. She had read it in the 1697 folio, the water-damaged petition in Wren's documentation package, the name partially legible in the clerk's cramped hand. She had assigned it a date and a stratum and moved on.

She had not known it was her family.

She kept writing.

"After Lena," Nora said, "the chain thins for two generations. Ida acknowledged this. Names without stories. She held them as names only, a woman called Mara, and before her a man whose name Ida rendered in English as something like Thomas, though she said it was probably not Thomas, just the closest sound available to her when she learned it."

"How did Ida receive those names?" the attorney asked.

"From her grandmother. Who received them from her mother. Each one passing what she had, which was less than what she'd been given, because the times were hard and the keeping got harder." Nora turned the mug once in her hands. "Ida was honest about the thinning. She said some of it was lost. She said it the way you say a fact, not the way you say a regret."

Tayak looked at the names she had already written. She thought about the stratigraphy. She thought about the gap between the 1697 petition layer and the ceramic pipe bowl, the Stratum Two find, mid-eighteenth century, the one she had logged and dated and written four careful pages about without knowing whose hands had made it.

"Then Matta," Nora said.

Tayak's pen stopped.

She had written that name herself. In a folio note, in her own field assessment, in the chain-of-custody log for Stratum Two. Possible maker: artisan in the tradition of the Piscataway pipe-making craft, mid-18th century. She had written possible because that was the correct word. She had written it at a field table with a headlamp on, in the dark, and moved to the next entry.

"Matta," Nora said again, as if she had noticed the pause. "He made pipe bowls. Sold them to an English factor named Hendricks who didn't ask questions. He kept the traditional forms hidden inside the commercial ones, the right clay, the right firing, the right proportions, so the knowledge survived inside the trade. He buried sacred objects in the mound when a constable came through requiring surrender of any items held for Indian ceremony."

Tayak wrote: Matta. Pipe bowls. Hendricks. Constable. Objects buried. Her handwriting had gotten smaller, the way it did when she was concentrating hard.

She thought about the pipe bowl in the conservation suite. She thought about the firing pattern she had noted, the dark-fired exterior, the deliberate cord-marking, the clay composition she had sent to the lab with a note reading possibly intentional formal choice, possibly regional variation, pending comparative analysis. She had written pending because she had not wanted to overclaim.

She had not known she was looking at Matta's hands.

"Matta's granddaughter received the chain," Nora said. "But he was sick when he told her, the fever came before he was ready, and she knew the account was incomplete. She held what she had. She passed it to her daughter, who passed it to hers."

"Do you have names for those generations?" the attorney asked.

"One name," Nora said. "The granddaughter's name. Ida held it. I hold it." She said the name, two syllables, a pause before the second one that changed it from a word into something else. "After her the chain holds two women I know only by their relationship. Matta's line, daughter to daughter, down to, "

She stopped.

"Tessa," she said.

Tayak put her pen down.

She picked it up again immediately. She wrote the name. But her hand had stopped for a full second, and the attorney had seen it, and Nora had seen it, and neither of them said anything.

Tessa. The name from the shell gorget log. The name Wren had traced through the land records, through the Calvert title chain, through the folio that referenced the high ground and the community at the mound. The name Tayak had written in her field assessment as possible community member, mid-eighteenth century, associated with the gorget deposit. The name she had treated as a historical figure, as evidence, as stratum.

"Tessa kept the gorget," Nora said. "She performed the ceremonies at the mound after her brother stopped. She was the last one in that generation to go to the high ground openly. After her it became something done quietly, in the early morning, without witnesses."

"What happened to her?" the attorney asked.

"She died," Nora said. "Old age. She passed the gorget and the name-list to her daughter, who passed them south." She paused. "The gorget came to the mound eventually. Tessa's line brought it there. Ida knew this. She said Tessa was the one who kept the thread when it was most likely to break."

Tayak looked at what she had written. The names ran down the page in her small, tight hand: Saya, Lena, the thinned generations, Matta, his granddaughter, Tessa. A column of names that looked, on the page, like a list. Like evidence. Like stratigraphy.

Like a family.

She pressed her lips together and kept writing.

Nora went on without stopping. The second half-hour was the colonial period thinning into the eighteenth century and out the other side, names carried through women mostly, passed at deathbeds and kitchen tables, some of them in English by then, some still in the older forms, the chain fraying and being reknotted, generation by generation, each keeper doing what she could with what she had received.

Tayak recognized none of these names. They were not in any folio she had read, any deed index, any county record. They were not in the stratigraphy. They were in Nora's voice, in this kitchen, on this morning, and nowhere else on earth.

She wrote every one.

The recorder's red light held steady on the table. Outside, the frost had begun to lift from the grass, the porch light still burning pale against the strengthening grey of the morning. Tayak had filled six pages of her notebook. Her hand ached across the knuckle of her middle finger, the specific ache of sustained writing, the one she recognized from field seasons and hearing transcripts and the forty-three-page assessment she had written by headlamp in the equipment shed.

"Ida's grandmother," Nora said. "She was the one who told Ida. At a kitchen table, in October, hands flat on the cloth. She said: I need to tell you something before I lose the words for it. She knew the chain back to Saya. She acknowledged that names before Saya had been lost. She said this plainly, the way Ida described it to me. She did not pretend the chain was unbroken. She said: here is what I have. Here is what I know is missing. Take both."

The attorney had stopped typing for a moment. She was looking at Nora.

"Ida received the full chain at nineteen," Nora said. "She kept it for sixty-two years." She paused. The number landed differently than it had earlier, not as a duration but as a weight. "She taught it to me when I was nineteen, at this table, in October. She traced the names on a flour-sack cloth because she said I needed to see the shape of it, not just hear it." Another pause. "She died four years later. In March."

Tayak looked at the flour-sack cloth on the table. It was still there, folded beside the sugar bowl, the pencil marks faded to near-invisibility. She had been looking at it since she sat down and had not understood what it was.

"I've held the chain for sixty-two years," Nora said. "I've said it aloud three times. Once to myself, to check. Once to my daughter." The pause was brief and controlled. "And now to you."

The kitchen was very quiet.

Tayak looked at the page. Six pages of names and dates and connections, Saya at the top, Nora at the bottom, and somewhere in the middle of the chain, not written, not spoken yet, but present in the gap between Nora's daughter and the woman sitting at this table with a field notebook and an aching hand, her mother's name. The name Nora had not said. The name that should have been in the chain and wasn't, because her mother had left in the spring of 1989 and not come back, and the chain had jumped a generation the way a creek jumps a dry channel, finding the next available course.

She had been the next available course.

She had not known.

"For the record," the attorney said, her voice careful and even, "Nora, can you confirm that the individuals you've named, Saya, Lena, the generations between, Matta, Tessa, and the subsequent keepers through Ida, are, to your knowledge, direct ancestors of the Moyaone family line?"

"They are my family," Nora said. "They are Tayak's family. Yes."

Tayak looked at her own handwriting on the page. The names she had excavated, Okwi in the folio, Matta in the pipe bowl's firing pattern, Tessa in the gorget's chain of custody, looked back at her from the paper. She had found them in the ground. She had catalogued them. She had written possible and pending and comparative analysis required because that was the correct scientific language, because that was what her training demanded, because you did not claim what you could not prove.

She had been excavating her own family for two months and had not known it.

She had known it. She had not let herself know it.

The distinction mattered. She was not sure yet what it meant.

She turned to a fresh page in the notebook.

Nora reached past the sugar bowl and lifted the flour-sack cloth. She unfolded it once, twice, and laid it flat on the table between them.

Tayak had assumed it was a rag.

It was not a rag. The pencil marks she had taken for old stains were names, written in a hand smaller and more careful than Nora's current script, tracing a column down the center of the cloth with branch-lines running left and right at intervals. Not a list, she saw now, but a tree, or something like a tree, the branches going sideways rather than up, the whole thing oriented around a central trunk of names that ran from top to bottom like a spine.

"Ida drew this," Nora said. "The night she taught me. She said she needed me to see the shape before I could hold the sound of it."

Tayak looked at the faded marks. She could not read most of them. The pencil had gone too thin, and the cloth had been folded and unfolded too many times, the creases eating the letters at their centers. But she could see the structure. She could see that it was a structure.

"The quilt is the same," Nora said.

The attorney's fingers had stilled on the keyboard.

"I started it in 1989," Nora said. "In the spring. After my daughter left."

She said it the way she had said everything this morning, steadily, without decoration. Tayak kept her eyes on the cloth.

"I had been carrying the chain for twenty-seven years by then. I had said it aloud once, to myself, to check that I still had it. I had not written it anywhere, because Ida told me not to. She said paper burns, paper gets taken, paper gets used against you. She had learned that from the chain itself. From what happened to Okwi's petition."

Tayak wrote: Quilt. 1989. After. She did not write the word mother.

"But I was afraid," Nora said. "My daughter was gone and I did not know if she was coming back, and I had no one to teach. You were nine years old. I couldn't give a nine-year-old what Ida gave me." She paused. "So I made a different kind of paper. One that wouldn't look like evidence to anyone who didn't already know."

"For the record, can you describe the quilt?" the attorney asked quietly.

Nora nodded once. She was looking at the window, at the pale November light coming through the glass.

"It is a nine-panel quilt," she said. "Each panel is a generation. The panels run in sequence from the upper left to the lower right, the way you read a page in English, because I made it so that anyone who found it would think it was decorative. The colors in each panel are not random. Blue for water. Brown for ground. A particular shade of green I mixed myself from two different fabrics, cut from an old dress and a curtain, for the high ground. The mound."

Tayak looked up from her notebook.

"The mound is in every panel," Nora said. "In the center of each one. A small raised shape, stitched in that green, oriented the same way in each panel. The long axis running northeast to southwest, the way the actual ground runs. I measured it. I went to the site with a compass before I started the first panel and I measured the axis and I stitched it true."

The recorder's red light held steady. Tayak wrote: Mound axis. NE-SW. Stitched true. She measured it.

"Around the mound in each panel," Nora said, "there are markers. Boundary markers. Not obvious. They look like decorative stitching, like filler. But they follow the actual boundary of the high ground as I understood it from what Ida told me and from what I could find in the county records at the time." She paused. "Some of those boundaries I got wrong. I know that now, from what you've found. The eastern line especially."

Tayak thought of the eastern transect. She thought of the cord-marked rim and the question of where the occupation boundary actually ran. She thought of the forty-three pages she had written by headlamp.

"The names," Nora said, "are in the stitching itself. Along the borders of each panel. They look like a repeating pattern, a kind of chain stitch, which I chose deliberately, you understand, but if you know to look, the lengths of the stitches are not equal. Long, short, long, short-short, long. It is a code I made myself. I wrote down the key once, on a piece of paper, and then I burned the paper, because I did not want the key to exist separately from the quilt. The quilt is the key."

The attorney had stopped typing entirely. She was looking at Nora with an expression Tayak recognized, the expression of someone recalibrating the scale of what they were dealing with.

"You memorized the code," the attorney said.

"I made the code," Nora said. "I remember everything I make."

Tayak pressed her pen against the page. She wrote: The quilt is the key. No separate document. Code held in maker's memory only. She paused, then wrote below it: She made this alone. She made this because she was afraid the spoken version would die.

She looked at that last sentence. She turned it into a question, the way her training had taught her to turn assertions into questions. Afraid it would die with whom?

Not with Nora. Nora was thirty-one in 1989. Nora was not dying.

Afraid it would die because there was no one left to receive it. Because her daughter had left.

"My daughter saw it," Nora said. "Once. In its early years. I had finished the first three panels by then, maybe four. She came back for a few days in the summer of 1991. She was living in Baltimore."

Tayak did not move.

"She saw it on the frame in the front room. She asked what it was. I told her it was a family record. She looked at it for a while. She touched one of the panels, the one with Matta's generation, the brown and green, the pipe-bowl shape I put in the corner." Nora's voice did not change. "She said it was beautiful. Then she left again. She did not ask what the stitching meant."

Tayak looked at her notebook.

She looked at the page she was writing on and she looked at the pen in her hand and she looked at the names she had written, Matta, Tessa, Ida, Nora, and she felt something move through her chest that was not grief and was not anger and was not the clean, manageable feeling of professional discovery. It was the shape of something she did not have a word for. The shape of a room she had been standing in for thirty-five years without knowing it had walls.

Her mother had seen the quilt. Had touched the panel. Had said it was beautiful.

Had left.

Tayak turned to the margin of the page, the narrow white strip to the left of her field notes, the strip she normally left blank for cross-references and context flags, and she wrote there, in letters smaller than her usual hand, pressed hard into the paper:

She knew. She saw it. She left anyway.

She did not say it aloud. She did not look up. She pressed the pen hard enough that the letters would not fade, that they would be legible in ten years, in twenty, as legible as Ida's pencil marks on the flour-sack cloth were not.

"I kept adding panels," Nora said. "After she was gone. After it was clear she wasn't coming back to take the chain from me. I finished the ninth panel in 2001. Twelve years of work."

The attorney said, "For the record, where is the quilt now?"

"In the front room," Nora said. "On the frame. It has been on the frame since 2001."

"Has anyone else seen it?"

"Many people have seen it," Nora said. "No one has read it. Those are different things."

Tayak looked at the window. The frost was fully gone from the grass now. The porch light had clicked off at some point without her noticing, and the morning had come up grey and even, the kind of November light that did not cast shadows, that made everything look equally close and equally far.

She thought about the quilt on the frame in the front room, twelve feet from where she was sitting. She had walked past it when she came in. She had registered it the way you register furniture, present, not remarkable, part of the room's texture. A nine-panel quilt in brown and green and blue, the stitching intricate and even, the kind of thing that took years.

She had walked past her family's two-thousand-year record and noted it as decorative.

She wrote, in the margin, below the first line: I walked past it.

"The center panel," Nora said, and her voice shifted slightly, not softer, but more deliberate, the way it had shifted when she spoke Ida's name, "is the mound itself. Not a generation. Not a person. The mound alone. I put it in the center because it is the center. It has been there longer than any of the names in the other panels. It will be there after the names are gone."

She reached into the pocket of her cardigan, the zipper pull replaced with a twist of wire, and took out a small folded piece of paper. She unfolded it and placed it on the table between the flour-sack cloth and Tayak's notebook.

It was a photograph. Printed on regular paper, the ink slightly smeared at one edge where it had been handled. A photograph of the quilt, taken straight on, all nine panels visible.

"I took that last month," Nora said. "When Marcus told me about the state's filing. I wanted a record that was separate from the object."

Tayak looked at the photograph. Even at this scale, even in the slightly smeared inkjet print, she could see the structure of it. The nine panels in three rows of three. The repeated green shape at the center of each panel, small, raised, consistent, the long axis running the same direction in every one. The border stitching that looked, at this resolution, like a decorative chain but was not.

And in the center panel, larger than the others, the mound shape stitched in that green Nora had mixed from a dress and a curtain, surrounded by a field of brown and blue, ground and water, with a single line of stitching running around its perimeter that Tayak recognized, with a lurch she felt in her sternum, as a boundary line.

She had drawn that line. In her field assessment. In the forty-three pages. She had drawn it from the stratigraphy and the shovel tests and the GPR results and the colonial patent boundary descriptions, and she had drawn it in pencil on graph paper, and it looked, at this scale, in this photograph, like the line Nora had stitched in 1989 from memory and a compass bearing.

"The eastern line," Tayak said. Her voice came out level. She was surprised.

"I know," Nora said. "I told you I got it wrong. I stitched it too far east. I didn't have the documents you have."

"It's close," Tayak said.

"Close isn't the same as right."

"No," Tayak said. "But it's evidence of continuous knowledge of the site's location and extent. Evidence that the oral tradition preserved accurate spatial information over, " She stopped herself. She was doing it again. She was turning her grandmother's twelve years of solitary work into an exhibit.

She looked at the photograph for another moment. Then she picked up her pen and wrote, on the fresh page, in her normal hand, not in the margin: Quilt panel 5 (center): mound outline, NE-SW axis, boundary stitching. Photograph in possession of N. Moyaone. Original on frame, Billingsley Road. No secondary copy of code key. Key held in maker's memory. Photograph taken October 2024 by affiant.

She wrote it because it needed to be written. Because this was what she could do, right now, in this kitchen, with this pen. She could make it legible. She could make it part of the record.

In the margin, in the small hard letters, she added a third line below the first two: Make the record. That's what's left.

Nora watched her write. She did not say anything. She seemed to understand that Tayak needed the notebook the way other people needed silence.

Outside, a car passed slowly on Billingsley Road. The sound of its tires on the cold asphalt came through the window glass and faded.

The attorney reached forward and checked the recorder. The battery indicator showed two bars. She did not say anything, but she shifted it slightly closer to Nora, and the small adjustment, practical, quiet, without interruption, was its own kind of attention.

"There is one more thing in the quilt," Nora said. "In the ninth panel. The last one I finished, in 2001."

Tayak looked up.

"I put a name there," Nora said. "In the border stitching. In the code. I put the name of the woman at the bottom of the chain, the first one, the one who started it. The one whose name I've held for sixty-two years."

The kitchen was very quiet.

"I put it there," Nora said, "because I was afraid I would die before I could say it to the right person. And I wanted it somewhere. I wanted it to exist somewhere other than inside me."

Tayak looked at the photograph. Panel nine, lower right. The border stitching running along its edge in what looked like a simple repeating pattern.

"Now I can say it," Nora said. "But not yet. Not on the recording." She looked at Tayak directly, for the first time since she had begun. "After. When she stops the recorder. I'll say it to you."

The recorder ran.

Nora's voice had changed in the second hour. The deliberate pace was still there, but something underneath it had loosened, not fatigue, something closer to arrival. She had been walking toward this part of the story for sixty-two years. Now she was in it.

She was talking about the pendant.

Not the pendant in the evidence bag at the conservation suite, though she had named it. She was talking about the chain of hands it had passed through before anyone in this room was born. The attorney typed without looking up, her fingers finding the keys by feel, and Tayak sat with her pen uncapped and her notebook open and did not write anything, because there was nothing she could have written that would have been sufficient.

"The woman Ida called the last keeper," Nora said, "was not the one who buried it. She was the one who decided it was time. Her mother had worn it. Her mother's mother had worn it. It went back further than that. Ida said four generations before the one who buried it, maybe five. She wasn't certain. The chain thins at that distance. Names thin."

Tayak looked at her hands. She had read every paper on oral transmission rates in the eastern woodlands. She knew the degradation curves. She also knew, sitting in this kitchen, that every degradation curve she had ever read was a description of loss written by someone who had not been in the room when the transmission happened.

"The trader came from the north," Nora said. "From the lake country, Ida said. He followed the river paths down. He stayed three days at the settlement. He traded the pendant for two deer hides and a pot of rendered fat. Then he left and didn't come back." She paused. "Nobody ever knew why he didn't come back. The women in the chain kept asking that question. It went unanswered for a long time."

The attorney's fingers stopped moving. She looked at Nora, then looked back at the screen, and began again.

"The woman who received it from him kept it for eleven years. Then she gave it to her daughter. Then her daughter gave it to her daughter. That's the shape of it. It moved through women, mostly, though not always. It moved through whoever was holding the ground."

Tayak thought about the pendant in its evidence bag. The green patina on the copper. The drilled suspension hole worn smooth at its upper edge from decades of a cord running through it. She had documented the wear pattern in the field log. She had written consistent with long-term suspension use and noted the angle of wear and moved on to the next observation. She had not thought about whose neck had produced that wear pattern. She had not let herself.

She thought about it now.

"The woman who buried it," Nora said, "buried it for her daughter. Her daughter had died. The pendant had been the daughter's once, just once, just held, Ida said, the way you give a child something beautiful to hold for a moment. The daughter died of a fever. Seven days." Nora's voice did not change. "The mother buried the pendant in the mound for her. She put it face-up. She wanted it to face the sky."

Tayak's pen was in her hand. She did not move it.

She thought about the orientation of the pendant in the stratum. Face-up. She had noted it in the field log. She had written placement consistent with intentional deposition, face-up orientation, possible votive context and flagged it for analysis and moved on.

Possible votive context.

"Ida knew her name," Nora said. "The woman who buried it. Ida said it carefully, the way you say a word that means more than it sounds like. She said there was a pause before the second syllable, that the pause was part of the name, that without the pause it was just a common word. She taught me to say it with the pause."

The fluorescent light above the sink hummed. Somewhere in the wall, the heat came on, a low rush of air through the baseboard, smelling faintly of dust and the mineral dryness of November.

"I've held that name for sixty-two years," Nora said. "I've never said it to anyone. Not on the phone. Not in the council room. Not in the affidavit." She looked at the recorder. "Not on the recording."

The attorney looked up from the laptop.

"I want to say it off the record," Nora said. "After."

The attorney nodded. She reached forward and typed something, a note, a timestamp, a bracket, and then sat back and let Nora continue.

Nora talked for another twenty minutes. She traced the chain forward from the burial, how the mound had grown, how the family had stayed near it, how the name had been passed from keeper to keeper the way the pendant itself had been passed, one generation to the next, sometimes skipping, sometimes held by someone who had not expected to hold it. She named the ones she knew. She named the ones Ida had named to her. She named the ones Ida had said were already thinning when Ida's grandmother had spoken them, names that had survived as sounds without full meaning, held because letting them go entirely felt like a second death.

Tayak wrote some of it. Not all of it. Her handwriting was smaller than usual.

Then the attorney said, "I need to pause for a moment."

She picked up the recorder. The red light went out.

The kitchen went very quiet.

The attorney turned the recorder over, found the small panel on its base, and pressed it open with her thumbnail. She slid out the memory card, a thing the size of a fingernail, and set it on the table beside her laptop. She took a fresh card from the inside pocket of her blazer, where she had been keeping it warm, and seated it in the slot with a small precise click.

In the silence before she pressed record again, Tayak understood.

Not as a thought. As a fact arriving fully formed, the way stratigraphy arrives when you have been reading the ground long enough. Not a discovery but a recognition, the moment when what you have been looking at becomes what it is.

She had been excavating her own family's ground.

The pendant was not evidence of continuous Piscataway occupation of Site 18CH-447. It was evidence of her family's continuous occupation of Site 18CH-447. The woman who had buried it face-up in the earth, so it would face the sky, was her ancestor. The chain of names Nora had been reciting for two hours, Kaya, Waya, Suki, the woman at the third depth, the woman at the second depth, Okwi, Saya, Lena, Matta, Tessa, Ida, was her family's chain. The mound she had been excavating with trowels and brushes and a Total Station and a forty-three-page field assessment was the mound her family had built and tended and buried their dead in and stitched into a quilt and carried in spoken words across two thousand years so that it would still exist, still be legible, still be found.

She had found it. She had not known she was looking for it. She had thought she was doing science.

She sat with this for the length of time it took the attorney to check the battery indicator, confirm two bars, and set the recorder back in the center of the table.

She did not cry. She was not going to cry. She pressed her lips together once, hard, and then let them go, and the feeling went somewhere else, down into her chest, into the place where the things she could not process in the field went, the place she would open later, alone, when there was time.

There was not time now.

The attorney's hand moved toward the record button.

"Wait," Tayak said.

The attorney waited.

Tayak looked at Nora. Nora was watching her with the same steady attention she had held all morning, not waiting for a particular response, not managing her, just present.

"How long have you known it was her?" Tayak said. "The woman at the bottom. How long have you known she was ours?"

Nora did not hesitate.

"Since Ida told me," she said. "Sixty-two years."

The recorder light came back on, red and steady.

The attorney said the date, the time, and the case reference number. She asked Nora to confirm her name. Nora confirmed it. Her voice was steady and unhurried, the same as it had been at seven in the morning.

Then the attorney said, "We're going to the signature page now."

She turned the laptop to face Nora and scrolled to the bottom of the document. Tayak watched Nora read it. Nora read slowly, the way she did everything, not because her eyes were failing but because she had decided long ago that speed was for people who were not sure what they were doing. Her lips did not move. She read the jurat line twice. Then she picked up the pen the attorney had set beside the laptop, a plain black ballpoint, the kind that came in a box of twenty, and signed her name in the space provided.

Her handwriting was large and deliberate, each letter fully formed, the way people wrote when they had learned penmanship from someone who believed it mattered.

The attorney witnessed it. She signed her own name below Nora's, dated it, and printed her bar number in the line beneath. Then she saved the document, ejected the memory card from the laptop's reader, and held both cards, the recording card and the document card, between her thumb and forefinger for a moment, as if weighing them.

From the inside of her briefcase she took a small paper envelope, the kind used for evidence preservation, white, with a chain-of-custody form printed on the front in a grid of boxes. She wrote the date and the case reference and her own initials in the appropriate boxes. She slid both cards inside. She sealed the flap and pressed it flat along the adhesive strip, then wrote her initials across the seal.

She set the envelope on the table between them.

"I'll have the transcript to you by Friday," she said. "The filed copy goes to Marcus's office. You'll each receive a certified duplicate."

Nora nodded. She did not look at the envelope. She looked at Tayak.

The attorney began closing her laptop. She did this quietly, with the practiced efficiency of someone who understood when a room needed to change and had learned to change with it. She capped her pen. She stacked her papers. She said, "I'll give you a few minutes," and took her briefcase to the front room, and they heard the soft sound of her sitting down on the couch and the faint tap of her phone screen coming to life.

The kitchen was quiet. The baseboard heat had stopped. The fluorescent light above the sink continued its low, even hum.

Nora reached across the table and moved the evidence envelope two inches to the left, so it was no longer between them.

"The affidavit is not the end," she said.

"I know."

"You don't know yet. You think you do." She folded her hands on the table. "The affidavit is what makes the next step possible. It's the instrument. Not the answer." She paused. "The answer is still in the ground."

Tayak said nothing.

"You need to go find her," Nora said.

The fluorescent light hummed. A car passed on Billingsley Road, its tires loud on the cold asphalt, and then the sound faded and the road was quiet again.

Tayak understood what Nora meant. Not the pendant. The pendant was already catalogued, already in the conservation suite, already photographed and logged and numbered. She meant the woman who had buried it. The deepest layer. The stratum they had not yet fully opened.

"We're close," Tayak said. "Another week of careful work. Maybe less."

"Then go."

Tayak looked at her notebook. She had been writing in the margins for the last twenty minutes, fragments, names, dates she wanted to check, the phrase chain of title with a question mark, and one other thing she had written without fully deciding to write it: her mother's name, in the margin beside the quilt section, written once, then circled.

She had not written her mother's name in a notebook in twenty years.

She closed it.

"There's a problem," she said.

Nora waited.

"The moment I name her in a published report, the woman at the bottom, I have to declare the familial connection. That's the protocol. And the moment I declare it, the state has grounds to remove me as PI. Conflict of interest. They'll say I can't be objective about my own ancestor's burial." She kept her voice level. "They'll take the site."

Nora was quiet for a moment. The heat came back on in the baseboard, the low rush of air, the mineral smell of November dust.

"They may try," she said.

"They will try. Slade has been looking for a reason since September. This hands it to him."

"Then you need to find her before they find out you're looking." Nora's voice was not unkind. It was direct, the way a good trowel is direct. It does not negotiate with the ground. "You've been careful your whole career. Be careful now. But don't stop."

Tayak looked at the table. At the rectangle of late-morning light that had moved across the linoleum since they sat down, shifting with the sun, now reaching the far edge of Nora's chair. At the quilt in the front room, which she could see from where she sat, the dark blue square in the third row that Nora had said was the creek bend, and the copper-colored thread she had not noticed until Nora pointed to it, running from that square to the lower left corner where the mound sat in the pattern, not labeled, not explained, just there for the person who had been taught to see it.

"There's one more thing," Nora said.

Tayak looked up.

"Her name." Nora held her eyes. "I told you I've never said it to anyone. That I've held it for sixty-two years."

"Yes."

"I'm going to say it now. Off the record. The way Ida said it to me." She glanced toward the front room, where the attorney's phone screen was still faintly audible, a low voice from some video or call. "Not for the affidavit. Not for the court. For you."

Tayak set her pen down.

Nora said the name.

She said it with the pause before the second syllable, the pause that was part of the name, that changed it from a common word to something specific and irreplaceable, the pause that Ida had taught her and that Ida's grandmother had taught Ida and that had been taught and retaught across two thousand years so that it would still be sayable, still be the right shape in the mouth, still mean the particular woman it had always meant and no other.

Tayak did not write it down.

She held it.

After a moment she said, "Thank you."

Nora nodded once. She picked up her water glass and drank, and set it down, and that was that. The name was given, the sixty-two years were over, and the kitchen was the same kitchen it had been before, the linoleum and the baseboard heat and the hum of the fluorescent light, except that it was not the same at all.

The attorney came back in. She picked up the evidence envelope from the table and put it in her briefcase. She gave Tayak a certified copy of the affidavit in a manila folder, twenty-three pages, numbered paragraphs, the jurat at the end with Nora's large deliberate signature and the witness line below it.

"I'll call Marcus this afternoon," she said. "He'll want to know it's done."

"He already knows," Nora said. "He's been waiting since six."

The attorney smiled at this, briefly, and let it go. She said goodbye to Nora, and Nora walked her to the door, and Tayak heard them exchange a few words in the front hall, something about the drive, something about the cold, and then the front door opened and closed and Nora's footsteps came back to the kitchen doorway and stopped.

"Leave when you're ready," Nora said. "Not before."

Then she went down the hall, and a door closed softly, and Tayak was alone in the kitchen.

She sat for another minute. She looked at the quilt in the front room. She looked at the copper thread.

Then she picked up her notebook and her copy of the affidavit and her field jacket from the back of the chair, and she went out through the front door into the cold.

The truck was where she had left it, pulled half onto the gravel and half onto the frozen grass at the edge of Nora's driveway. The windows had fogged slightly from the inside during the hours she had been in the house. She got in and pulled the door shut and sat with the engine off.

The cold came in immediately, the particular cold of a Maryland November that had been sitting in metal and glass since early morning, damp and still and smelling faintly of the creek bottom, of leaf decay and iron-rich mud.

She set the manila folder on the passenger seat. She set her notebook on top of it.

Then she picked the notebook back up and opened it to the margin note.

Her mother's name. Written in her own handwriting, in the margin beside the quilt section, at the moment she had not been able to stop herself. Written once, then circled. Not underlined, not starred, not annotated with a date or a question mark, just circled, the way you circle something when you don't yet know what to do with it but you know you cannot let it go.

She had stopped asking about her mother when she was nine years old. Not because someone told her to stop. Because asking had produced nothing, not answers, not comfort, not even the useful friction of a refusal, and she had understood, the way children understand things they are not told, that the question itself caused damage to the people around her, and she had put it down the way you put down a tool that isn't working.

She had put it down for thirty years.

She looked at the circled name.

The woman at the bottom of the mound had a name now. Tayak held it in the same place she had put the feeling from the kitchen, down in her chest, in the place she would open later. She did not say it aloud. Not yet. Not here in the cold truck in Nora's driveway with the fog on the windows and the dead grass at the edge of the gravel and the road beyond it empty in both directions.

But she did not close the notebook.

She sat with both names, the one in the margin, circled, and the one Nora had given her, held without being written, and she did not start the engine, and she did not look away.
