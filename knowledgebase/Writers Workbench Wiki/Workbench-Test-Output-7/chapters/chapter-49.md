The anger and the gratitude were both true. She had known this since the moment Wren said *I'm sorry.* She had known it before that, when Wren was still explaining the boundary language and Tayak was standing in the cold with the phone warm against her ear and her mind already doing the calculation she didn't want to do.

The right thing had happened. The wrong person had made it happen, in the wrong way, without authorization. Both of those facts were real. She was not going to collapse them into one.

She thought about Nora.

Nora had kept the oral history for sixty-two years. She had sat with it through every failed petition, every state review, every decade when the recognition effort went quiet because the people doing the work ran out of money or energy or hope. She had kept the chain of names, Saya, Okwi, Lena, Matta, Tessa, all the way back to the woman at the bottom of the mound whose name Nora would not say on the phone, and she had kept it not because the keeping was easy but because someone had to and she was the one who had been given it.

Nora had not sent the names to anyone without asking. Nora had not made unilateral decisions about what the community needed. Nora had sat with the knowledge for sixty-two years and waited until the moment was right, and when the moment came she had called Tayak and said *come home* and meant it.

That was one way to carry something. It was not the only way. But it was the way that kept faith with the people who had handed you the weight.

Tayak stood up. Her knees ached from crouching. The cold did that now, in a way it hadn't five years ago. She pulled her glove off and pressed her bare hand flat against the frost-pale grass at the trench edge, feeling the cold come up through the soil. The ground was firm. Below the frost line it would be softer, the clay matrix yielding to the trowel in the way it had for the last three weeks, each lift revealing something older than the one before.

Below the pendant layer, something curved and intact.

She did not know what it was. She knew what she hoped it was, and she knew that hope was not methodology, and she knew that Cecile Marant would tell her to put the trowel down and document and let the ground give what it gave in the order it gave it.

She put her glove back on.

Thursday's council meeting was four days away. She would have to stand in front of Marcus and Delia Swann and Eleanor Runningwater and the rest of them and put two things on the table: the breach, and the result of it. She would not soften the breach to make the result easier to receive, and she would not minimize the result to make the breach easier to condemn. They were two things. The council would decide what to do with both of them.

That was the process. The process was not perfect. It was slower than she needed and more complicated than she wanted and it had failed the community before in ways that still showed. But it was the community's process, not hers to shortcut, not even when the shortcut led somewhere real.

She heard a truck on the field road. Dawes doing his afternoon round, the door sound familiar enough that she didn't turn to look. The flagging tape at the unit corners lifted and settled in the wind. The sky was the same flat white it had been all morning, the kind of sky that gave no information about what was coming.

Forty minutes. She had forty minutes before Wren arrived with the stratigraphy log and the field kit, and she would use them to pull the context sheets for the pendant layer and set up the section drawing frame so Wren could start immediately. There was a feature below the pendant. She needed the section drawing before she went another centimeter deeper. She needed the soil color recorded in Munsell notation and the matrix description and the exact relationship between the pendant's compression ghost and whatever was below it.

She needed to know what was there. She needed to know it in the right order, the way the ground gave it.

The phone was still in her coat pocket. She thought about calling Marcus again, not to tell him anything new, but to hear the way he processed things, the careful deliberate way he held competing facts without forcing them to resolve. She did not call him. That conversation needed to happen in person, needed the council's table between them, needed the right setting.

She thought about Nora instead. *Come back to the site,* she had told Wren, and that was right, that was the next step, but after the site there was Nora, and Nora had something she had kept for sixty-two years, and Tayak had not yet gone to hear it.

The pendant was in the conservation suite. The thing below the pendant was still in the ground. Nora's knowledge was still in Nora, waiting for Tayak to come and receive it the way it deserved to be received, sitting down, notebook open, the full attention of someone who understood what it cost to carry something that long.

She would go to Nora. Not today. Today was the trench, the section drawing, the feature below the pendant layer. But soon. Before the council meeting. Before she stood in front of Delia Swann and put the two things on the table, she needed to know what Nora knew, because Nora's knowledge was the ground beneath everything else, the deepest layer, the thing the rest of it was built on.

She walked to the equipment shed and pulled the context sheets for the last three lifts. The paper was cold and slightly damp at the edges from the morning's frost. She spread them on the folding table inside the shed and looked at the soil descriptions, silty clay loam, 10YR 3/2, transitioning to 10YR 2/1 at the lower boundary, and the small sketch she had made of the pendant's position before lifting it, and the note in her own handwriting at the bottom of the sheet: *possible feature below. stopped. document first.*

Document first. Then excavate. Then understand.

She had learned that in the Patuxent lowlands with clay on her hands and Cecile's voice in her ear, and she had learned it again every time she had gone faster than the ground could give and paid for it. The ground did not arrange itself for your convenience. It gave you what it gave you when it gave it.

Wren would be here in forty minutes. The feature would still be there. The petition had what it needed. The anger was still real. The slow, reluctant acknowledgment that the right thing had happened was also real. She was not going to resolve them into one.

She picked up a pencil and began to set up the section drawing frame on a clean sheet of graph paper, measuring the unit dimensions from memory, marking the known strata in their correct proportions, leaving the bottom open. The space below the pendant layer, the space she had not yet entered, the space that was waiting.

She was still at the folding table when she heard the car. Not Dawes's truck, something smaller, the sound of tires on the gravel turnout at the field road's end. She looked at her watch. Thirty-seven minutes. Wren was early.

She did not go out to meet her.

She finished marking the strata on the section drawing, the glass bead layer at the top, the ceramic pipe bowl below it, the gorget's compressed soil signature, the pendant's ghost, and left the bottom third of the paper blank. The unknown portion. She labeled it in pencil, small and precise: *below pendant layer, undocumented.*

Footsteps on the frozen grass, then Wren's shape in the shed doorway, backpack over one shoulder, the stratigraphy log tucked under her arm in its plastic sleeve. She had changed into field clothes, canvas work pants, the green fleece, boots with dried clay still on the laces from the last session. She had not stopped to clean them. Tayak decided not to read it either way.

"The log," Wren said, and set it on the table.

Tayak pulled it toward her without looking up from the section drawing. She opened the log to the last entry, pendant layer, 10YR 2/1, lower boundary irregular, possible feature, and read it the way she read everything Wren produced: looking for what was missing, what was compressed, what the notation left out. There was nothing missing. There never was.

"The section frame's set up," Tayak said. "I need you on the east face. Full Munsell notation, matrix description at every five centimeters. Don't interpret. Just record."

"All right."

"And I need the relationship between the compression ghost and the lower boundary. Exact. If there's a feature below it, I need to know its shape before we go in."

"I'll use the line level and the plumb bob."

"Yes."

Wren set her backpack down and began pulling out equipment, the folding ruler, the plumb bob on its cord, a fresh set of context sheets clipped to a board. Her movements were the same as they always were. Careful. Unhurried. The same hands that had photographed the Calvert patent and uploaded it to Marcus's legal team without asking.

Tayak looked at the section drawing.

"The council will be told," she said. "About all of it. The documentation and how it got there."

Wren stopped with the plumb bob in her hand. "I know."

"I'm not going to manage how that lands for you."

"I'm not asking you to."

The wind moved through the gap in the shed wall, lifting the corner of the context sheets. Tayak put her hand flat on them.

"Then we're clear," she said.

Wren nodded and went out toward the trench.

Tayak stood in the shed for a moment longer. The section drawing was in front of her, the known strata in their correct proportions, the bottom left open. She thought about what Nora had said, *come home, I'm ready,* and about the sixty-two years Nora had carried whatever she was carrying, and about the pendant in the conservation suite, and about the thing below the pendant that had no name yet, no notation, no Munsell number, nothing but the compression in the soil and her own handwriting at the bottom of a context sheet: *possible feature below.*

She picked up the drawing and went out to the trench.

The light was going. Not gone. The sky was still that flat white, but the quality of it had changed, the way it changed in late afternoon in November, everything going slightly grey at the edges without announcing itself. The flagging tape at the unit corners lifted and snapped. Wren was already at the east face, crouching, the plumb bob hanging steady from her extended hand, her eyes moving between the soil profile and the context sheet on her board.

"10YR 2/1 at the upper boundary," Wren said, not looking up. "Transitioning. There's a color shift at about twelve centimeters below the pendant layer."

Tayak crouched at the opposite edge and looked. The shift was there, a darkening, not dramatic but real, the soil going from the dark greyish brown she knew to something closer to black. She had seen that color before. Not here. In the Patuxent lowlands, in a context that had turned out to be a prepared surface. A floor, or something like a floor. Something laid down deliberately.

"Record it," she said. "Don't call it anything yet. Just the color and the depth."

"Already writing."

Tayak looked at the profile for a long time. The pendant had been placed. She had known that since the moment it came out of the ground, the deliberate positioning, the orientation, the lack of disturbance around it. Someone had opened the ground and put the pendant there and closed the ground again. And below the pendant, this darkening. This change.

She straightened up and looked at the field. The light was going faster now. Dawes's truck was a shape at the far end of the field road, parked, his afternoon round done. The trees at the mound's edge had lost their color, gone to silhouette. November dark came early in Charles County, and tonight it would arrive before she was ready for it.

"We stop at the light," she said. "We document what we have and we stop."

"There's maybe forty minutes," Wren said.

"Then we use them."

They worked in silence. Wren called measurements; Tayak recorded them on the section drawing, filling in the lower portion of the blank space with the color shift, the depth, the irregular boundary of the darkened layer. The plumb bob swung slightly in the wind and steadied. The soil smell was sharper at this depth. Not the leaf-litter smell of the upper strata but something older, something that had been sealed for a long time and was now open to the air. She had no word for it that belonged in a report, so she wrote *organic-rich matrix, possible prepared surface, confirm with flotation sample* and left it at that.

When the light was nearly gone Wren stood up and stretched her back, and Tayak covered the unit with the tarp and weighted the corners with the sandbags they kept in the equipment shed. The flagging tape went still when the wind dropped. Somewhere in the tree line a bird made one sound and stopped.

Wren pulled off her gloves and tucked them in her jacket pocket. She looked at the covered trench, then at Tayak.

"Tomorrow?" she said.

"Six-thirty," Tayak said. "Before the light changes."

Wren nodded. She picked up her backpack and the stratigraphy log and walked toward the field road without looking back. Tayak watched her go, the shape of her against the grey field, the log under her arm, the boots with the clay still on them.

She looked back at the covered trench. Below the tarp, below the sandbags, below the pendant layer's compression ghost and the darkening soil, something was waiting that had been waiting for a very long time. She did not know what it was. She knew its color, 10YR 2/1 shading toward black, and its depth, and the fact that it had been prepared by someone's hands, and nothing else.

Tomorrow she would know more. And before she stood in front of the council, she would go to Nora.

She picked up the section drawing and walked back to the equipment shed. The drawing was almost complete now, all the known strata in their proportions, the color shift recorded at the bottom, and below that a single line she had drawn freehand, not yet labeled, marking the boundary of what she had not yet reached. She looked at it for a moment in the shed's failing light.

Then she rolled it carefully, slid it into the cardboard tube, and put it with the others.
