The dark before dawn had its own weight. Waya had learned this as a girl, lying still while her mother's breathing slowed into sleep, listening to the settlement go quiet. The last dog. The last coal popping in the fire pit. Then nothing but the river moving somewhere below the ridge. The dark was not empty. It pressed.

She lay on her side and did not move.

The pendant was in her left hand. She had taken it from the cord around her neck sometime in the night. She did not remember doing it, but she had done it before, on nights when something was ending. Her palm had warmed it. Now it sat in the cup of her fingers, small and dense, the edges slightly irregular where the hammering had not come out even. She could feel that unevenness in the dark. She had felt it so many times she could have drawn the pendant's outline in the dirt without looking.

Copper. Her mother had told her the word the trader used, a word from his own language that Waya had never heard anyone else say. She had kept the sound of it the way you keep a stone from a riverbank. Not because it is useful. Because you cannot make yourself put it down.

The pendant had come to Waya from Ahanu.

She had been twelve when Ahanu pressed it into her hands. Not her mother's hands, not her grandmother's, but hers, directly, which had surprised her and which she had never entirely understood. Ahanu had not explained the choice, only said: *your father would have known what to do with this, and you are more like him than you know.* Her father had been dead three years by then. She had not known whether to be comforted or unsettled by the comparison, and had decided, in the way of twelve-year-olds, to be both.

What she had learned later, from Ahanu, in pieces, over years, was the pendant's longer history. It had come to Ahanu's family from a trader who had traveled the river paths from the north and west, a man who had stayed three days, given the pendant in exchange for two deer hides and a pot of rendered fat, and left. He had not returned. The trader had said the copper had passed through many hands before his, coming down from somewhere far up the Ohio-river country, where people worked native copper cold, hammering it thin and heating it between blows to keep it from cracking, then hammering it again until it held its shape. The pendant had passed through Ahanu's hands for many years before Ahanu gave it to Waya. Near the end, when the drought had broken and the two of them sat together on the high ground above the settlement, Ahanu had said that the pendant had always felt borrowed, as if it were waiting for the person it actually belonged to.

*Perhaps that is you,* Ahanu had said. *I have been holding it for you.*

Waya had not known what to say to that. She still did not know, twenty years later.

She pressed the pendant flat against her sternum and stared at the smoke hole above the fire pit, where the first grey of the sky was just beginning to separate itself from the dark. The fire had burned down to coals. She could smell the hickory ash and, under it, the green smell of the river, which meant the wind had shifted in the night.

Ahanu had not said where the pendant came from before the trader. The trader himself had not said, or Ahanu had not remembered, or the remembering had thinned over years the way remembering does. It had come from somewhere. Someone had cold-hammered it into this shape. She could feel the slight ridges at the edges where the work showed, the surface worn smooth at the center by years of hands. That was all she had.

And yet.

She turned the pendant in her fingers. The edge that had been hammered thinnest caught the first pale light from the smoke hole and gave it back, a dull reddish warmth, nothing like fire, more like the color of a coal just before it goes grey. She had seen copper objects in the hands of other traders over the years, beads and small cut pieces, and none of them had this quality of holding warmth. She did not know if that was the copper itself or the years of hands working into the surface. Her own hands. Ahanu's hands. The trader's before that, and however many hands before his. Either way it was true.

*It means we are not alone.*

Those were Ahanu's words, the ones that had come with the pendant. She had believed them then. She was less certain now. She wanted to believe them. She believed them some days. On the days she believed them, the pendant felt like proof, that somewhere, far up the river paths, people were making things like this and sending them out into the world, and the world was wider than one settlement on one ridge above one river. On the days she did not believe them, the pendant felt like a beautiful story someone had needed to tell, and the trader was a man who had walked a long way for practical reasons and given a pretty object to a family he would never see again, and Ahanu had made of that what was needed.

Today she was giving it to her daughter.

Her daughter was seven years old and already too serious for seven, which was Waya's fault and also not her fault. The child watched everything. She had her father's habit of standing very still when she was thinking, which made her look like a small, intent version of someone much older. She would ask questions about the pendant. She would want to know where it came from, and how far, and why the trader had not returned, and Waya would have to answer.

*This came from far away. It means we are not alone.*

She said it quietly in the dark, testing the words. They sounded right. They had always sounded right. The words were not the problem. The problem was the silence that came after them, the silence in which her daughter would look at her with those still, waiting eyes, and Waya would have to hold the words steady against her own doubt.

She sat up.

The coals gave off enough light to see by, barely. Across the fire pit, her daughter was asleep on her mat, one arm thrown over her face in the way she had slept since she was an infant. Her breathing was even and slow. She had no idea what today was.

Waya looked at her daughter for a long time.

Then she looked down at the pendant in her hand. The copper had taken the warmth of her palm completely. If she closed her fingers around it she could almost believe it was alive, that the warmth was its own and not borrowed from her.

She would give her daughter the pendant at first light, before the settlement woke. She would use Ahanu's words. She would hold them steady.

Whether she believed them fully was a question she would carry alone. Perhaps that was its own kind of answer: that you pass the thing forward not because you are certain but because the passing itself is the point. One hand opens. Another receives. The warmth moves from palm to palm across the dark.

The smoke hole had gone from grey to pale. Somewhere below the ridge, a bird called once and stopped.

Waya closed her fingers around the pendant and waited for her daughter to wake., -

The bird called again, closer this time, and the child's arm moved. Not waking, just shifting. Waya watched the breathing change from deep to shallow and back, the body deciding whether to surface.

She had been twelve herself when Ahanu placed the pendant in her hands. She remembered the morning with the kind of precision that only comes from having returned to it many times, worn the memory smooth by handling.

It had been early autumn. The hickory trees on the eastern slope were just beginning to turn, the leaves going from green to a dull, waxy yellow at the edges, and the air had that quality it gets for perhaps ten days a year: cool enough to be comfortable, warm enough that the coolness feels like a gift rather than a warning. The hickory nuts had not yet fallen; in another week the ground beneath those trees would be thick with them, and everyone would be out gathering. But that morning the slope was still and the nuts still clung to their husks, and Ahanu had found Waya at the river before the settlement stirred.

She remembered that first: the hand on her shoulder, firm but not urgent, and then Ahanu's face in the low morning light, closer than faces usually came.

*Walk with me.*

No explanation. Ahanu did not explain things in advance. So Waya had gone, still wool-headed with sleep, and they had walked to the eastern edge of the settlement, where the ground dropped away toward the river path. The ridge was quiet. Below them the river was a sound more than a sight, the low, continuous movement of water over stone, which Waya had heard every morning of her life and which she no longer heard unless she listened for it. That morning she had listened. Ahanu stood beside her and did not speak for a long time.

Then Ahanu had reached into the front of the wrap and taken out the pendant.

Waya had seen it before, of course. Worn against Ahanu's chest, occasionally brought out on certain mornings, certain ceremonies. She knew its weight and its color and the slight irregularity of its edge. What she had not known was that it was about to become hers.

Ahanu held it up by the cord. In the early light, the sky just beginning to separate grey from dark, the copper caught nothing. It looked dull, almost brown, the color of old bark. Ahanu turned it once, slowly, as if checking something.

*This came from far away,* Ahanu said. *It means we are not alone.*

Said it the way you say something you have said before, or have thought before, many times. Not reciting. Not stiff or formal. With a particular steadiness, the way you carry a full water jar: carefully, because the contents matter. Then Ahanu stepped behind Waya and lifted the cord over her head and settled it around her neck, and Waya felt the pendant drop against her sternum, cold at first, the copper having held the night air, and Ahanu's hands came to rest on her shoulders, one on each side, their full weight, the way you place your hands when you want someone to feel they are being held in place.

The weight of those hands. Waya could feel it now, sitting in the dark beside the banked coals, twenty years and more removed from that morning. The specific weight of Ahanu's palms. The slight roughness of the skin at the heel of each hand from years of work. Not a gentle touch. A deliberate one. *Stay here. Take this.*

She had stood very still. The pendant had warmed quickly against her skin, faster than she expected, as if the copper were eager to take her heat. Below them the river continued its low, indifferent sound. Ahanu had not moved those hands.

*Where did it come from?* Waya had asked. She had known even then that it was the wrong question, but she was twelve and it was the question she had.

Ahanu had been quiet for a moment. Not the quiet of someone who does not know the answer. The quiet of someone deciding how much of the answer to give.

*A man brought it,* Ahanu said. *He came from far up the river paths. He traded it for deer hides and dried fat, and then he left, and we never saw him again. But it had already traveled far before it reached us. The trader said it had passed through many hands before his.*

*Why didn't the man come back?*

*I don't know.* Said without apology. *I asked the same question, when I first received it.*

Waya had thought about that. Ahanu asking the same question, standing in some earlier version of this same morning. It had made her feel, for the first time, the length of the thing she was holding. Not just the object but the question attached to it, the question that traveled with it.

*Does it matter?* she had finally asked.

Ahanu had moved those hands then, sliding them from Waya's shoulders to her upper arms, turning her gently so they were face to face. Ahanu's eyes in that light were dark and direct and entirely serious.

*It matters that he came at all,* Ahanu said. *It matters that someone, somewhere, made this and sent it out into the world. That is enough.*

Waya had not been sure, even then, that it was enough. But she had understood that Ahanu was not asking her to be sure. She was being asked to carry the question., -

Across the fire pit, her daughter made a small sound and turned onto her side. Her arm came down from her face. Her eyes opened, not gradually the way some people wake, but all at once, as if she had been waiting just below the surface of sleep for permission to surface. She found Waya immediately, the way she always did, her gaze landing on her before she had even fully oriented to the room.

Waya opened her hand. The pendant lay in her palm, the copper gone fully warm now, the reddish surface catching the first thin light from the smoke hole.

Her daughter sat up. She looked at the pendant. She looked at Waya's face. She was already, at seven, very good at reading faces. Too good, Waya sometimes thought, for a child who should still be allowed to not understand things.

"Come here," Waya said.

She came. She crossed the space between her mat and Waya's without hesitation, folding herself down to sit cross-legged in front of her mother, her eyes still on the pendant. Up close, Waya could see the sleep-crease on her cheek, the slight tangle of her hair, the particular stillness she went into when she was paying full attention. Her father's stillness. It caught at Waya every time.

She lifted the cord. She held the pendant up between them. In the growing light it looked less dull than it had in the dark. The copper had taken on that quality it sometimes had in the early morning, a warmth that was not quite color, not quite glow, something in between.

Her daughter reached out and touched it. One finger, very careful, tracing the edge where the hammering had not come out quite even.

"Where did it come from?" she asked.

Waya felt the question land in her chest. The same question. Of course the same question.

She took a breath. She placed the cord over her daughter's head and felt the pendant drop, and then she put her hands on her daughter's shoulders, the full weight of them, deliberate, and she said the words Ahanu had said, the words that were not hers and were entirely hers:

"This came from far away. It means we are not alone."

Her daughter was quiet for a moment. The pendant rested against her small sternum, already beginning to warm. Then she looked up, those still, waiting eyes finding Waya's face, and asked the question Waya had known was coming, the question that traveled with the pendant as surely as the cord:

"How far?"

The light through the smoke hole had gone from grey to pale gold. Not *where*, the question Waya had braced for, not her own childhood question, but *how far*, which was harder.

Waya kept her hands on her daughter's shoulders. The pendant lay flat against her chest, the cord settling into the hollow of her collarbone.

"I don't know," Waya said.

Her daughter considered this. She had a way of receiving information, even unwelcome information, without flinching. She sat with it the way you sit with a stone you have just picked up, feeling its weight before deciding what to do with it.

"Farther than the river?"

"Yes."

"Farther than the place where the river bends and you can't see the settlement anymore?"

"Much farther than that."

She looked down at the pendant. Her finger found the edge again, the slightly uneven rim where the hammering had gone a little long on one side, a detail that had survived every hand.

"Did the man who brought it walk the whole way?"

"Some of it. Some of it may have gone by canoe." Waya paused. "It passed through many hands before it reached our family. That is how things travel that far. Not carried by one person the whole distance, but passed along, each person carrying it a little way."

Her daughter was quiet, working through this. Outside, the first birds had started. Not the full morning chorus yet, just one, then another, the sound thin and particular in the cool air.

"So someone made it," she said slowly, "and gave it to someone, and that person gave it to someone else, and it kept going until it got here."

"Yes."

"And now it's here."

"Now it's here."

She held the pendant in both hands, cupping it against her sternum without lifting it, the cord still around her neck. Her brow had that slight furrow it got when she was solving something.

"Will the man come back?" she asked. "The one who brought it to us."

"No."

She looked up. "How do you know?"

"Because he never did. Not in Ahanu's time. Not before." Waya moved one hand from her daughter's shoulder to her hair, smoothing it once, the way she had done since she was an infant and the gesture had meant *you are safe*. "Some people pass through once and are gone. That is the nature of passing through."

Her daughter received this without argument. Then, after a moment: "It's warm."

"Yes."

"It was warm when you put it on me." She looked down at it again. "I thought copper was cold."

Waya opened her mouth to say *it was in my hands all night*, which was true, and was the simple answer, and was not the answer that came out.

What came out was something else. It arrived the way certain things arrive, not thought but spoken, as if the words had been waiting in some part of her that was not quite her own:

"It takes warmth from whoever holds it. That is how it remembers."

Her daughter looked up at her.

Waya held very still. She did not know where the words had come from. They felt true in the way that things feel true when they have been true for a long time before you found them. Not invented but uncovered, the way you uncover a hearthstone under a season's worth of ash and recognize that it was always there, that it was always warm underneath.

Her daughter looked back down at the pendant. She was quiet for a long time, long enough that the single bird outside became several, the thin pre-dawn sound thickening into something fuller.

"So it remembers you," she said finally. "And Ahanu. And all the ones before."

"Yes."

"And now it will remember me."

"Yes."

She sat with this. Her hands were still cupped around the pendant, not gripping, just holding, the way you hold something you are still deciding the shape of.

Then she said, very quietly, not quite a question: "What if I'm not here long enough for it to remember much."

Waya's chest tightened. She moved her hands back to both of her daughter's shoulders, the full weight of them, and waited until she looked up.

"Then it will remember what it has," she said. "That is all any of us can give it."

Her daughter held her gaze for a moment. Then she nodded, once, with the gravity of someone much older, the gravity that had lived in her face since she was an infant and that Waya had never entirely known what to do with, whether to be proud of it or frightened by it.

She let the pendant go. It settled back against her chest.

"Can I wear it today?" she asked.

"It is yours to wear."

She stood. She was already moving toward the door, the pendant swinging slightly with the motion, catching the morning light once before she stepped through and was gone into the cool air outside. Waya heard her feet on the packed earth, then the sound of the river, low and continuous, the way it always was.

She sat alone in the house for a moment. The mat where her daughter had slept still held the shape of her. The fire had burned down to coals that glowed orange at their centers, cooling at the edges to grey.

Waya put her hand to her own sternum, where the pendant had rested for twelve years. The skin there was warm. It would be warm for a while yet. The body is slow to give up what it has held.

She thought of Ahanu's hands on her shoulders. She thought of the question Ahanu had not answered and had not pretended to answer: *why didn't he come back*. She thought of her daughter's version of the same question, which was not quite the same question, which was perhaps the question the pendant asked of every person who held it, in whatever form that person needed to receive it.

She did not know what her daughter would do with it. She did not know how long she would carry it, or what she would say when the time came to pass it on, or whether the words would come the way they had come to Waya this morning, unbidden, fully formed, arriving from some place that was not quite inside her and not quite outside.

She hoped they would. She thought perhaps they would. The pendant had been traveling long enough that it seemed to know how to do this, how to carry not just warmth but the words that went with warmth, the words that explained what warmth was for.

Outside, her daughter called something to someone, a neighbor's child from the sound of it, the ordinary morning business of being seven years old and alive and unaware of most of what you are carrying.

Waya listened to her daughter's voice until it moved away down toward the river. Then she got up and went to tend the fire., -

Thirty years is not a long time when you are inside it. It is only long when you look back.

Waya understood she was dying on a morning in early autumn, sitting outside with her back against the south wall of the house, which held the warmth well into the afternoon. She was not frightened. She had expected this for long enough that it had stopped being a surprise and become simply the next thing. The way the season turns. Not sudden but accumulated, each day a little shorter than the one before until one morning you look up and the light is different and you understand that the change has already happened and you were inside it all along.

Her daughter came and sat beside her. The pendant was at her collarbone. It had been there for thirty years.

They did not talk about dying. They talked about the children and about a woman in the river households who had been sick and recovered, and about whether the hickory crop on the eastern slope would be worth the walk. The nuts would be coming down soon; the squirrels had already started. Ordinary talk. The kind that fills the space when the important thing is too large to say directly, and saying it directly would only make it smaller.

After a while her daughter put her hand over Waya's hand where it rested on her knee. Her palm was warm. The pendant caught the sun.

Waya thought: *I do not know if it is true*. The old question. The one that had never quite left. *I do not know if we are not alone. I do not know if Ahanu knew something or only hoped it. She thought: *I gave them to her anyway.*

Waya died in the early spring, when the ground was soft enough to work. She had asked for nothing particular. She had said what needed saying to the people it needed saying to, and she had let the rest go.

Her daughter pressed the pendant to her lips the morning of the burial. The copper was cold in the early air, and then it was warm, and then she held it there a moment longer than she had meant to, her eyes closed, not praying exactly, just holding the warmth against warmth and letting that be enough.

Then she let it fall back against her chest and went to do what needed doing., -

Thirty years after that morning when she had sat with the pendant in her cupped hands and asked what it remembered, thirty years after Waya had said *it takes warmth from whoever holds it, that is how it remembers*, Waya's daughter woke before dawn and lay still in the dark and listened to her own breathing.

Her hands had begun to tremble. Not badly. But she noticed it in the mornings, the slight unsteadiness when she first reached for the water jar, the way her fingers took a moment to find their grip. Her mother's hands had done this. She had watched them do it.

She sat up slowly. The house was quiet. Her husband was asleep. Her children were grown and in their own houses. The fire had burned to coals.

She reached up and took the pendant off.

She held it in her palm in the dark.

It warmed.

She thought about her mother's hands on her shoulders. She thought about the words, *this came from far away, it means we are not alone*, which she had carried for thirty years the way you carry a thing you have been told to carry. Faithfully. Without examining too closely whether you believe it, because the carrying is the point and the believing is a separate question.

She asked herself now, in the dark, with the pendant warm in her trembling hands: *do I believe it?*

She did not answer immediately. She had learned, from her mother, not to answer immediately.

What she believed was this: the pendant had been warm in her fist when her first child was born, and the child had lived. What she believed was this: she had pressed it to her lips the morning her mother was buried and it had been cold and then it had been warm and that had meant something, even if she could not have said what. What she believed was this: she had worn it for thirty years and in that time she had not felt alone in the way she might have felt alone without it, and she did not know if that was the pendant or her own mind or whether those were different things.

She thought: *my mother did not know either. I could see it in her face that morning. She gave me the words and she did not know if they were true and she gave them anyway.*

She thought: *perhaps that is what the words are for. Not to be true. To be given.*

She sat until the light began at the smoke hole. Then she put the pendant back on, because she was not ready yet. Not ready to give it, not ready to say what she had begun to understand she would need to say when the time came.

She got up and went to tend the fire.

The doubt went with her. It always had. It lived in the pendant the way the warmth lived in it, taken from whoever held it, passed on without announcement, arriving in the next pair of hands already present, already familiar, as if it had always been there.

Which perhaps it had., -

The trembling in her hands got worse in the summer.

She said nothing about it. Her daughter noticed anyway. The girl had her grandmother's eyes, that quality of seeing without appearing to look. But she did not name it, because her mother had not named it, and in their house the unspoken had its own kind of courtesy.

By autumn Waya's daughter could not carry the water jar from the river without stopping twice. She told herself it was the heat. The heat broke. She told herself it was the cold. The cold settled in and her hands shook in the mornings and sometimes in the afternoons and she stopped telling herself things.

She called her daughter to her in the evening, when the fire had come down to a steady burn and the smoke rose straight up through the hole in the roof and the sky above it was the color of river water before rain.

"Sit," she said.

Her daughter sat. She was thirty-one years old and had her mother's way of going still when stillness was called for. She did not ask what was wrong. She could see what was wrong.

She was not able to explain the whole of it.

"I don't know where it came from," she said. "Far away. That is all I have. Ahanu did not know either. The man who brought it did not say, or Ahanu did not remember, or the remembering thinned over the years the way remembering does." She looked at the copper. "But it came from somewhere. Someone made it. Someone cold-hammered it into this shape and annealed it in a low fire and hammered it again until it held, and polished it and gave it away, and it traveled, and it arrived here, and that is not nothing."

"No," her daughter said. "It is not nothing."

"The mound will keep it longer than we can," she said. "That is why."

She said it simply, without drama, the way you say a thing you have known for a long time and are only now finding the words for. Then she set the pendant on the sleeping mat between them, because her hand was tired and she wanted to rest it.

She did not pick it up again.

She died three days later, before the first frost, in the early morning when the light came grey through the smoke hole and the river was loud after rain. Her daughter was beside her. She had not left the house., -

Afterward, when the others came and did what needed doing, her daughter sat alone for a time. The fire had gone low. The pendant lay on the mat where her mother had set it, the cord coiled loosely beside it. The copper had gone cold.

She picked it up.

It was lighter than she had expected, though she had held it before. Her mother had let her hold it as a child, once, briefly, with the instruction not to drop it. She remembered the weight of it then. Now it seemed lighter. She did not know if that was the pendant or her own hands, which were stronger than a child's and could not feel small weights the way a child could.

She held it and looked up through the smoke hole. The sky was pale grey, the color of ash, the color of the river before rain. The mound was to the east. She could not see it from here, but she had known its location since she was old enough to walk to it, and she knew it now the way you know the position of a thing you have never needed to look directly at.

Her mother's voice was already in the pendant the way her mother had said it would be. She had not expected to feel this so quickly. She had thought it would take time, that grief would come first and the rest later. But the voice was there, low and steady, saying: *the mound is the hand that does not let go.*

She sat with the pendant in her palm and the question sat with her.

Her mother had told her what to do. She had heard it clearly. She had said *I know* and she had meant it.

And yet.

The copper warmed in her hand. It always warmed. That was what it did. It took warmth from whoever held it, and gave nothing back but itself, which was perhaps the same as giving warmth back. She could not decide. She pressed her thumb across its face. The surface was smooth where it had been worn smooth by hand after hand across the generations, and rough at the edges where the hammering still showed, small ridges like the texture of bark.

Someone had made this. Far away, in a place she could not name, someone had taken a piece of native copper and cold-hammered it and heated it in a low fire and hammered it again until it was this shape, this thinness, this particular reddish sheen that went dark at the edges. Then they had given it away, or traded it, or sent it along a chain of hands she could not follow, and it had arrived here, and Ahanu had received it from a man who came through once.

She thought: *if I keep it, it stays in the family. That is not nothing either.*

She thought: *my mother knew I would think this. She said what she said because she knew.*

Outside, a bird called once and was quiet. The fire breathed. The smoke rose through the hole in the roof and the sky above it was beginning to lighten, grey going pale, pale going toward the thin gold of early morning.

The mound was to the east.

She rose. She did not put the cord around her neck. She carried the pendant in her closed fist, the copper warming against her palm with each step, and she walked out of the house into the cool air and turned east, toward the high ground, toward the mound that held the dead and what the dead had known.

The river was loud this morning. She walked toward the sound of it, and then past it, climbing the slope where the hickory trees stood bare in the early light, their branches black against the pale sky.

At the mound's base she stopped.

She stood there for a long time, the pendant warm in her fist, her mother's voice in it saying *not lost, kept*, and she held both things at once: the grief and the rightness, the loss and the giving, the way the two were not opposites but the same motion seen from different sides.

Then she knelt at the mound's southern face, where the earth was soft from the recent rain, and she worked her fingers into the soil. The earth was reddish-brown loam, iron-rich, smelling of wet clay and old ash where her fingers broke through to an older layer. She pressed the pendant into the ground and covered it.

Not lost. Kept.

The pendant lay in the dark below the surface, cold now without a hand to warm it, and the mound held it the way it held everything it had been given: without judgment, without forgetting, with the patience of ground that has been keeping things for longer than any name in the chain could reach.

Above it, the morning came on. The river ran. The hickory trees stood bare against the pale sky. The settlement below the ridge was beginning to stir, the first smoke rising from the first fire, the first voices carrying in the cool air.

The mound was quiet. It had always been quiet. It would be quiet long after the last name in the chain was spoken and the last voice that had spoken it was gone.

It held what it was given. That was what it did. That was enough.
