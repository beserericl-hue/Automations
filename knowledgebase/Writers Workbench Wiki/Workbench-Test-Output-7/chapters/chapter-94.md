The field road had dried hard since September, Dawes's tire ruts baked into the clay like the record of a decision already made. Tayak parked at the pull-off — gravel now, properly graded, with a low timber barrier at the far end — and sat for a moment with the engine off, listening to the October silence settle around the car.

She had not been here in six weeks. She had told herself this was intentional, a form of discipline: let the site be what it was becoming rather than what it had been. She was not sure she believed that. She had stayed away because she did not know yet how to arrive without a trowel.

She got out. The air smelled of cut grass and damp clay and something sharper underneath — the particular cold of ground that has not been touched in weeks, that has been left to itself. The sky was the kind of blue that only happens in October, too clean to last.

The fence stopped her before she had taken twenty steps.

Cedar posts, set at even intervals, maybe four feet high, with two horizontal rails of the same wood running between them. Not chain-link, not steel — someone had thought about this. The posts were new enough that the cut ends still showed pale against the weathered grain, still giving off the faint resinous smell of fresh cedar. Between two posts at the entrance, a gate stood open, and beside it a sign on a dark-stained board, routed letters filled with white paint:

PISCATAWAY ANCESTRAL SITE
18CH-447
MANAGED BY THE PISCATAWAY TRIBAL NATION
PLEASE STAY ON THE MARKED PATH
NO COLLECTION OF MATERIALS
NO UNAUTHORIZED DISTURBANCE

Below the English, the same text in smaller letters she could not read from here but knew were there — she had reviewed the sign design in March, in a conference room in Annapolis, arguing mildly with Marcus about the font size.

She stood at the gate and looked through it.

The mound itself was thirty meters in, rising from the grass with the same modest authority it had always had — not a dramatic hill, not the thing people imagined when they heard the word *mound*, but a low, deliberate swelling of the earth, maybe a meter and a half at its crown, the grass over it a slightly different shade from the surrounding field. Darker. Denser. The way ground grows differently when it has been layered by human hands.

The excavation units were gone. The tape flags were gone. The datum stakes, the string lines, the labeled bags folded under the site tarp — all of it gone, the disturbed soil backfilled, the surface regraded and reseeded. She had overseen that herself in August, on a day when the air was so thick with humidity that her field notes had gone soft at the edges. She had pressed the last of the new turf flat with both palms and then stood up and signed the closure form and handed it to Wren without looking at the mound again.

She was looking at it now.

The grass over the filled units was still slightly paler than the rest — it would take another growing season to fully knit — but from the gate you would not notice unless you knew what to look for. She knew what to look for. She had spent eleven months on her knees here, reading the soil in centimeter increments, learning the exact color and texture of every stratum down to the base layer. She could have drawn the site's profile from memory in the dark. She could still feel the weight of the copper pendant in her gloved hand, the way it had caught the morning light when she lifted it from the earth.

She did not go through the gate.

She stood with one hand on the cedar post — the wood still slightly tacky with resin, warm already from the low sun — and she looked at the site as if she were seeing it for the first time, which in a way she was. All the previous times she had stood here, she had been measuring something. Counting something. Writing something down. There had always been a next task, a next unit, a next layer to reach. The site had always been a problem she was in the middle of solving.

It was not a problem now. It was just a place.

The school bus was parked fifty meters further along the field road, its yellow paint bright and incongruous against the brown grass. She could hear children before she saw them — not the chaotic noise of a playground but something more contained, a low hum of voices being deliberately kept quiet, the way children sound when an adult they respect has asked them to listen. They were coming around the northern perimeter path, a loose line of maybe fifteen kids, eight or nine years old, walking in pairs with their hands at their sides or clasped behind their backs in unconscious imitation of the adults with them.

Two teachers. And at the front of the group, moving with the slow, deliberate pace of someone who has learned not to hurry sacred ground, Elder Delia Swann.

Tayak had not expected her. She had known there would be an elder for the ceremony — Marcus had handled the arrangements — but she had assumed it would be someone younger, someone from the education committee. Delia Swann was seventy-one years old and had voted no on the excavation authorization. She had voted no on the grounds that the obligation to the ancestors was prior to any evidentiary argument, and she had been right, and Tayak had known she was right even at the time, and they had not spoken directly about it since.

Delia Swann was leading fifteen children around the perimeter of the site that Tayak had excavated, and she was doing it with the unhurried authority of someone who had been waiting a long time to do exactly this.

Tayak watched the line of children move along the outside of the cedar fence. They were not going in. That was the point — the perimeter path existed so that people could be near the ground without disturbing it, could walk the shape of the place and understand its scale without touching it. One of the teachers said something quiet and two children who had begun to drift toward the fence straightened back into line.

Her hand was still on the cedar post. She became aware of this and took it away.

She had arrived with empty hands and she did not know what to do with them. She had carried a trowel on this site for eleven months. She had carried a field notebook, a hand lens, a brush roll, a measuring tape, a camera, a clipboard with the unit log. She had carried the weight of the permit application and the institutional hearing and the commission filing. She had carried Nora's voice on the phone and the copper pendant in its evidence bag and the folder with the commission ruling. She had always been carrying something.

She put her hands in the pockets of her jacket.

The children were a quarter of the way around the perimeter now, and Delia Swann had stopped at the eastern face of the mound — the section where Delia Marsh had found the cord-marked sherd, where the stratigraphy had been cleanest, where the profile had shown the clearest evidence of deliberate layering. Delia Swann could not know any of that. She had stopped there because the eastern face caught the morning sun.

She was saying something to the children. Tayak was too far away to hear the words, only the cadence — slow, unhurried, with pauses that the children seemed to understand they were not supposed to fill. One child raised a hand and Delia Swann looked at it for a moment before nodding, and the child said something, and Delia Swann said something back, and two or three other children turned to look at the mound as if they were seeing it differently now.

Tayak stood at the gate with her hands in her pockets and watched, and felt something she did not immediately have a name for. Not grief, not exactly. Not pride, which was too small a word and pointed in the wrong direction. Something more like the feeling of setting down a weight she had been carrying so long she had stopped noticing it was there.

The cedar post beside her was warm in the October sun, and the grass along the perimeter path was worn to bare earth in a narrow strip where feet had already been — not hers, not the field team's, but other people's feet, people who had come here in the weeks since the site opened and walked the boundary of a place they had been kept from. The bare earth strip was maybe four inches wide and ran the full length of the fence she could see, and it was the most important piece of evidence she had encountered in a year.

People had been coming here. On their own. Without her.

She had not known she needed to see that until she saw it.

The children had reached the eastern face now, and Delia Swann had turned to face the mound itself. She raised both hands, palms open, not toward the children but toward the low rise of earth behind the fence. The children went still without being told. Tayak had watched field crews go still like that when she called for silence during a delicate extraction, but that stillness was professional, performed. This was different. These children had simply stopped because something in the elder's posture made stopping feel correct.

Tayak moved away from the gate. She did not go toward the eastern face — that was not her place this morning — but along the western fence line, keeping the mound to her right, putting herself at the outer edge of the gathered people. There were perhaps forty adults standing in loose clusters between the gate and the cedar posts, some she recognized from the council meetings, some she did not. Marcus stood near the center with his hands clasped in front of him. He had not seen her arrive. She was glad of it. She did not want to be greeted. She wanted to stand here without anyone orienting toward her.

She had spent eleven months on this site being the person everyone oriented toward. Where do you want this unit? What's the context number for the new feature? Should I call the lab or do you want to? She had answered every question and she had been good at answering them and she had believed, honestly believed, that being good at it was enough. It had not occurred to her until this moment, watching Delia Swann hold her hands open toward the eastern face of the mound, that being the authority and being present were not the same thing. That you could know every layer of a place and still be standing outside it.

Delia Swann began to speak.

Her voice was low enough that Tayak caught only fragments across the distance — *relatives*, she heard, and *morning*, and something she could not make out that made three of the adults near her bow their heads. The elder's voice had the quality of water finding a path it had used before: no effort, no announcement, simply the sound of something going where it belonged. She was not performing. She was not explaining. She was speaking to the ground, and the children were witnesses to that, and the adults were witnesses to the children witnessing, and Tayak was at the outer ring of all of it, which was exactly where she should be.

One of the children — a girl, seven or eight, with her hair in two braids that had begun to escape their ties — turned from the elder and looked directly at the mound. Not at the fence, not at the adults, at the mound itself. Her face was not solemn in the way children perform solemnity when they sense adults want it. She looked the way Tayak looked at a profile wall when the stratigraphy was saying something she had not expected: concentrated, slightly frowning, trying to read it.

Tayak watched her and felt the thing she had not had a name for shift into something clearer.

This child would carry this morning. Not the fence or the sign or the laminated placard with the site number and the tribal seal — she would carry the shape of the mound against the October sky, the sound of the elder's voice, the worn earth under her feet on the perimeter path. She would carry it the way Tayak had carried the watercress lesson from her grandmother without knowing she was carrying it, without being told it was important, without a field notebook or a context number or a chain-of-custody form. She would carry it because she had been here and paid attention.

The ceremony lasted perhaps twenty minutes. Tayak did not time it. At some point Delia Swann lowered her hands and the quality of the silence changed, became ordinary again, and people began to move and talk quietly. The children stayed in their loose group near the eastern face, two of them crouching to look at something in the grass along the fence line — a beetle, probably, or a pebble, because they were also eight years old and a beetle was interesting regardless of the sacred nature of the surrounding ground. One of the teachers said something and they stood up again.

Marcus saw her then. He raised his chin in acknowledgment and she returned it, and he did not cross toward her and she did not cross toward him, and that was right too.

She turned and looked at the mound.

From this angle, the western face, it was easy to see what a casual visitor would see: a low rise, maybe a meter and a half at its highest point, grass-covered, unremarkable to anyone who did not know what unremarkable meant in this context. The cedar fence followed its perimeter at a distance of three meters, close enough to define the space, far enough to keep feet off the margins. The morning light came across it from the east and threw a shadow on the western grass, a shallow crescent that showed the mound's shape more clearly than the mound itself. She had spent eleven months looking at the interior of this place. She had not spent enough time looking at it from the outside.

She thought about what Carver had told her in her second field year, when she had been too eager to open a feature she had found and he had made her walk the surrounding area for an hour first. *You want to know what you're looking at before you change it. Once you've changed it you can't look at it the same way again.*

She had changed this place. She had done it carefully and correctly and with the best possible intentions, and it had needed to be done, and she would do it again. And she had changed it. The stratigraphy she had read would never be unread. The pendant was in a conservation suite in College Park. The document was in an acid-free folder in a climate-controlled room. The gorget had been photographed from thirty-seven angles. She had taken things out of this ground that the ground had been keeping for two thousand years, and she had done it in service of the people whose ancestors had put them there, and that was true, and also the other thing was true, and she was learning to hold both without deciding which one cancelled the other.

Delia Swann was walking toward her.

Tayak did not move. She had a brief impulse to straighten, to prepare some professional version of herself, and she recognized the impulse and let it go. She stood with her hands in her pockets and waited.

The elder stopped beside her. She was a small woman, shorter than Tayak by several inches, with the kind of stillness that had nothing to do with size. She looked at the mound for a moment without speaking. The shadow crescent on the western grass was shortening as the sun climbed.

"You didn't bring your tools," Delia Swann said.

"No."

The elder considered this. "Good."

They stood there. One of the children had detached from the group and was walking the perimeter path alone, slowly, touching each cedar post as she passed it — not grabbing, just the tips of her fingers, a brief contact, post to post, as if she were counting them or confirming they were real. The worn earth strip was narrow enough that her feet stayed on it without effort.

"She does that at home too," Delia Swann said. "Counts things. Her mother says she'll be a mathematician."

Tayak watched the girl move from post to post. "Or an archaeologist."

"Maybe." The elder's voice was neutral. Not unkind. "Or she'll just know how to count what matters."

Tayak did not answer. The girl had reached the southeastern corner and paused there, hand on the post, looking at the mound's eastern face where the ceremony had been. The morning light hit the grass at a low angle and the rise of the mound was very clear from that corner, clearer than from anywhere else on the perimeter. Tayak had noted it in the site survey fourteen months ago as the optimal viewing angle for the feature's profile. The child had found it by walking and paying attention.

"I voted no," Delia Swann said. "You know that."

"I know."

"I'd vote the same way again."

Tayak turned to look at her. The elder's face was weathered and direct, the deep lines at the corners of her eyes catching the light. She was not apologizing and she was not attacking. She was stating something she needed Tayak to understand.

"So would I," Tayak said. "Vote no, I mean. If I were you."

Delia Swann looked at her for a moment. Something shifted in her expression — not surprise exactly, but a recalibration, the look of someone updating an assessment they had held for a while.

"Then why did you do it?"

Tayak looked back at the mound. The shadow crescent was gone now, the sun high enough to flatten it. "Because someone was going to dig it. Garrett Wills had bulldozers scheduled. If it wasn't me it was going to be a highway crew and a state archaeologist who'd never heard of Saya."

The elder was quiet for a moment. "That's a hard reason."

"Yes."

"You could have said that at the council meeting."

"I didn't think it was my place to say it there."

Another silence. The girl with the braids had rejoined the group, and the teachers were beginning to gather the children for what looked like a walk back toward the road. The low hum of contained voices rose briefly as the group reorganized, then settled again.

"It was your place," Delia Swann said. "It's still your place. That's what I came over here to tell you." She paused. "You stand over there by yourself like you're waiting for permission. You don't need permission to stand on your own ground."

The words landed somewhere below Tayak's sternum. She did not look away from the mound.

"I didn't want to be the authority," she said. "Not today."

"There's a difference," the elder said, "between being the authority and being present." She said it without emphasis, the way you state something that has been true for a long time. Then she turned and walked back toward the group, her pace the same unhurried deliberate pace she had used leading the children around the perimeter, the pace of someone who had learned not to hurry sacred ground.

Tayak stood at the western fence and watched her go, and the mound threw no shadow now in the high morning light, and the cedar posts ran their circuit around the rise of earth that held two thousand years of her family's keeping, and she thought: *I am going to bring someone here*. The thought arrived fully formed, without preamble. Not a field team. Not a colleague. Someone who needed to walk this perimeter and touch these posts and hear what the elder had said this morning. Someone who had been waiting longer than Tayak had for permission to be present on this ground.

She took her hands out of her pockets.

Nora was where Kimi had put her: folding chair, wool blanket, the good angle on the mound's southern face. The blanket was the one from the back of Nora's armchair on Billingsley Road, the one that smelled of cedar and the particular dry warmth of an old house. Tayak had seen it her whole life. Seeing it here, in the open field, against the post-and-rail fence with the mound rising behind it, was briefly disorienting, as if two rooms of her life had been placed next to each other without warning.

Kimi had gone to get water from the car. Nora sat alone, hands folded in her lap, watching the children reassemble near the road. She did not look up when Tayak came to stand beside her.

"You can sit," Nora said. "There's ground."

Tayak sat in the grass at the chair's left side, her knees up, the way she had sat at Nora's feet as a child. The grass was cool and slightly damp from the morning. She could smell the clay in the soil beneath it, that particular mineral sharpness she associated now with the site, with the trench, with her hands in the earth. She had not expected to smell it from the surface. She had spent so long going down into the ground that she had forgotten it breathed upward too.

The elder — the man who had led the ceremony, whose name Tayak had been told and kept carefully — was moving toward the mound's face now, the children's group trailing at a respectful distance behind their teachers. He was a broad man, deliberate in his movement, wearing a ribbon shirt in dark blue and cream that the wind pressed flat against his chest as he walked. He stopped at the southern face, where the grass was shorter and the rise was most pronounced, and he turned to face the mound rather than the children.

Tayak watched Nora's hands. They had not moved.

The elder began to speak. His voice was low enough that Tayak caught only the rhythm of it from where she sat — not the words, just the cadence, the rise and fall that she recognized from Nora's kitchen on Billingsley Road, from the particular quality of attention that gathered in a room when something important was being said without being explained. The children were quiet. Even the youngest ones, the ones who had been fidgeting and pulling at each other's sleeves all morning, went still. Tayak did not know what he was saying. She did not need to.

Then she looked at Nora's lips.

Nora's eyes were closed. Her hands were still folded in her lap, perfectly still, and her lips were moving. Not following — Tayak saw this immediately, with the precision she had trained into herself over eleven years of reading evidence — not following but *ahead*. Nora's lips formed each word a half-beat before the sound reached them, the way you mouth the words of a song you have known so long you no longer hear it from the outside.

Tayak did not move. She watched her grandmother's lips and she counted what she was seeing.

Nora had been eighty-two years old when she told Tayak, in the trench, the name she had carried for sixty-two years. Sixty-two years. Tayak had been standing in the deepest layer of the mound with clay on her hands and she had heard the name and she had understood, in the abstract way you understand a fact before it becomes real, that this was something Nora had held alone for a very long time. She had thought she understood what that meant.

She had not understood.

She watched her grandmother's lips move through names she could not hear, names she had spent a year learning to hold herself, and she understood now that Nora had been doing this — exactly this, the silent recitation, the keeping — for longer than Tayak had been alive. Longer than Tayak's mother had been alive. Nora had received this chain when she was twenty, from a woman who had typed it on a manual typewriter in a kitchen in 1962 and then told Nora the part that could not be typed, and Nora had carried it through everything: through Tayak's mother leaving in the spring of 1989, through the years of silence, through the quilt and the folded blanket and the phone call on the second ring at four in the morning, through the hearing and the ruling and the drive out to Charles County in Kimi's car. Through all of it, every day, the names had been in Nora's mouth.

The elder's voice rose slightly, and Nora's lips stilled for a moment, and then moved again, and Tayak saw her grandmother's jaw tighten in the particular way it did when she was holding something she did not want to lose.

Tayak looked at the mound. The grass on its southern face caught the midday light at a low angle and the rise was very clear, the subtle asymmetry of the eastern slope that Tayak had measured and logged and described in forty-three pages of careful qualified language. She had written it in the third person, the way Marant had taught her. *The investigator notes.* She had written it like she wasn't there.

She was here now.

She heard the elder's voice drop to something almost inaudible, and she looked back at Nora, and Nora's lips were still. Her eyes were still closed. Her hands had not moved. But there was something in the set of her face — not peace exactly, not relief — something closer to what Tayak had seen in the field when a context finally resolved: the particular stillness of a thing that has been uncertain for a long time becoming certain.

"Nora," Tayak said. Quietly.

Nora opened her eyes. She looked at the mound for a moment before she looked at Tayak.

"You knew all of them," Tayak said. "Every name he said."

"Yes."

"How long have you known them?"

Nora was quiet for a moment. The wool blanket had slipped at her left shoulder and she did not pull it back. "Since I was twenty. Ida told me in her kitchen. She made coffee first. The coffee was terrible." A brief pause. "I didn't tell her it was terrible."

Tayak looked at her grandmother's hands. The knuckles were swollen at the first joints, the skin thin and slightly mottled. She had seen those hands her whole life and she had never thought about what they had been holding.

"Did you say them?" Tayak asked. "The names. By yourself."

"Every day," Nora said. "Or nearly. Some days I was too tired. Those nights I'd wake at three in the morning and say them in the dark so I wouldn't lose the sequence." She said it without drama, the way you report something that is simply true. "You don't get to put them down."

The elder had finished. He stood at the mound's face with his head slightly bowed, and the children were very quiet, and the wind moved through the cedar posts and the grass on the mound's crown and the wool blanket at Nora's shoulder, and Tayak felt the weight of what her grandmother had just said settle into her the way soil settles after rain: slowly, completely, finding every space.

"I would have helped you," Tayak said. "If I'd known."

"I know you would have." Nora's voice was even. "But it wasn't time."

"When did you know it was time?"

Nora looked at her. "When you called me from the trench."

Tayak thought about that morning. The copper pendant in the tray. The depth reading. Her hands shaking slightly when she picked up the phone, though she had not admitted that to herself until much later.

"I didn't know what I was calling about," Tayak said.

"I know. That's how I knew." Nora looked back at the mound. "You weren't calling because you'd figured something out. You were calling because you were standing in the right place and you didn't know yet what that meant. That's the only time the name can go in."

The elder had turned from the mound and was walking back toward the children, and the teachers were gathering the group again, and the sound of children's voices rose briefly in the mild air — a laugh, a question, the particular bright noise of young people who have been quiet for longer than they expected and are now released. One of the teachers said something Tayak could not hear and the group began to move toward the road.

Tayak watched them go. The girl with the braids was near the back of the group, still walking slowly, still looking at the ground. She was not counting the posts anymore. She was just walking, the way you walk when you are thinking about something that does not have words yet.

Nora watched her too.

"She'll come back," Nora said.

Tayak did not ask how she knew. She had spent eleven years asking how people knew things. She was learning, slowly, that some knowing did not work that way.

She sat in the grass at her grandmother's feet, and the mound threw no shadow in the high noon light, and the names were in the air around them like something just spoken, like something that had always been there and had only now become audible.

The group moved on. The teachers' voices carried back in fragments — *stay together, watch the edge, we'll stop at the road* — and the children sorted themselves into the loose, drifting shape of a school group that has finished the official part of the day and not yet been told what comes next.

Tayak stayed where she was.

She had no trowel. She had no field notebook. She had no reason to be standing at the mound's edge except that she was here and the ground was here and there was nothing she needed to extract from it today.

She had not understood, until this year, how much of her professional life had been organized around extraction. The careful kind, the documented kind, the kind that filled in the record and served the living — she did not regret any of it. But she had always had a reason to be on the ground. A unit to open, a profile to read, a measurement to take. She had stood in this field for eleven months with a trowel in her hand and a notebook in her back pocket and a question she was trying to answer, and the question had been answered, or answered enough, and now she was standing here with her hands at her sides and the grass pressing against her boots and no particular reason to move.

She did not miss the trowel. That surprised her.

The mound's southern face caught the midday light at a shallow angle that made the rise visible — the subtle swell of the ground, the asymmetry on the eastern slope she had measured to within two centimeters. The grass was growing back well. The erosion-control matting they had laid along the northern edge in the spring was already invisible under new growth, just a slight regularity in the texture if you knew where to look. The cedar posts stood at their measured intervals, the laminated signs pale against the dark wood, and the chain-link fence along the access road threw a thin shadow across the mowed perimeter path.

It looked, from here, like what it was: a rise in the ground. A subtle, deliberate, two-thousand-year-old rise.

She heard the footsteps before she turned.

The girl was perhaps eight years old. Red jacket, the bright synthetic kind that holds color through a Maryland November. Her hair was in two braids, tight at the scalp, and her face was serious in the way that some children's faces are serious — not unhappy, just attending. She had broken from the rear of the group and was walking toward Tayak with the purposeful step of someone who has decided to ask a question before she loses her nerve.

She stopped at the edge of the mowed path, two meters from Tayak, on the visitor side of the fence. She looked at the mound. Then she looked at Tayak.

"What's in it?" she said.

Tayak looked at the rise of ground. The grass on the crown moved slightly in the wind.

"Names," she said.

The girl considered this. Her eyes went back to the mound, then returned to Tayak. "Whose names?"

"Yours," Tayak said.

The girl was quiet for a moment. She looked at the mound again — not with skepticism, not with the polite blankness of a child receiving an adult answer she doesn't believe — but with the particular focus of someone checking a thing she had already half-suspected against the evidence in front of her. She nodded once. A single, small, conclusive movement, the way you nod when something confirms rather than surprises.

Then she turned and walked back toward her group.

Tayak watched her go. The red jacket was bright against the pale November grass. The girl did not look back.

Nora said nothing. Tayak had not heard her grandmother move, but she was aware of her presence the way you are aware of a fire — not looking at it, but knowing exactly where it is.

The wind came off the creek and moved through the cedar posts and across the mound's crown. The grass bent and straightened. Somewhere behind the fence line a crow called once and was silent.

Tayak thought about the first time she had stood at this mound. The permit in her hand, the field notebook still uncracked, the smell of turned clay and creek-bottom damp and the particular sweetness of cut grass in September heat. She had written the site number on the cover of the notebook before she did anything else, the way Carver had taught her. She had written it in the third person. *The investigator notes.* She had been so careful, for so long, to write it like she wasn't there.

She was here now.

The mound held its names in the dark below the grass, below the erosion matting, below the stratigraphy she had documented in forty-three pages of careful qualified language. The pendant. The gorget. The pipe bowl. The colonial document in its oilskin, the glass bead in its context layer. And below all of it, in the deepest matrix, in the base layer that matched the original river terrace ground surface: the chamber, the figure with seven cuts on its raised arm, and the name that Nora had said into the air an hour ago with her eyes closed and her hands flat on her knees.

A name that had traveled two thousand years to reach this field, this morning, this woman standing at the mound's edge with no trowel in her hand.

The girl in the red jacket had reached her group. A teacher touched her shoulder briefly, not in reprimand, just acknowledgment, and the girl fell into step beside the others. She did not look back at the mound. She had what she came for.

Tayak's boots were damp at the toe from the grass. The mound threw no shadow in the high noon light. She could hear, very faintly, the sound of the children's voices as the group rounded the path toward the road — the bright, ordinary noise of young people moving through the world, carrying things they did not yet know they were carrying.

She stayed where she was.

The bus left at half past two. Tayak heard the diesel engine turn over, heard the door hiss shut, heard the gravel crunch as it backed onto the access road. She did not watch it go.

The elder's fire had been properly closed an hour before — the coals raked apart, each one named and covered, the cedar ash gathered into a cloth and folded. Tayak had stood at a respectful distance while it was done, far enough back that her presence was witness and not intrusion. When it was finished the elder had nodded at her once, the same kind of nod the girl in the red jacket had given the mound, and Tayak had nodded back.

Kimi's car was the last to leave. Nora was in the passenger seat, her grey cardigan visible through the glass, her hands folded in her lap. She did not wave. She had said everything she needed to say this morning, and she was not a woman who repeated herself. Kimi raised two fingers from the steering wheel as the car passed. Tayak raised her hand.

Then the field was quiet.

The light had moved since noon. It came now from the southwest, low and flat, the particular November light that makes everything look like it is being examined rather than simply seen. The cedar posts threw long shadows across the mowed path. The laminated signs caught the angle and went briefly white, then settled back to pale. The chain-link fence along the access road ticked once in the wind, a single metallic note, and was still.

Tayak stood where she had been standing for the last ten minutes, at the edge of the mowed perimeter, and looked at the mound.

Then she walked.

Not toward the gate, not toward her car. She walked along the fence line to the northern end of the site, where the access road curved away and the ground dropped slightly toward the creek. The erosion-control matting along the northern slope was fully grown over now — she could see the slight regularity in the grass texture, the way the blades stood at fractionally different angles where the mat's grid lay beneath them, but you would have to know what you were looking for. She had laid that matting herself, on her knees in April rain, her fingers working the stakes into ground that was still winter-cold a few inches down.

She found the place without difficulty. She had always known where it was.

The trench was gone. Of course it was gone — she had supervised the backfill herself, documented it in the site closure report, signed off on the final compaction readings. The ground here was level, or nearly level, the slight depression of the first months already corrected by settling and new growth. The grass was shorter here than on the mound's crown, still establishing itself, a slightly different green — the particular yellow-green of grass that has not yet had a full season. In six months it would be indistinguishable from the surrounding field. In a year, in two years, you would not find this place without a map.

She crouched at its edge.

The soil smelled of clay and wet and the faint mineral sharpness that she had learned, over eleven months, to associate with this specific ground — the river terrace matrix, the particular iron content of the subsoil, the way the Mattawoman drainage had laid down its deposits over centuries in thin, patient laminations. She had put her hands into that soil a hundred times with a trowel. She had measured it, sampled it, photographed it, described it in language so careful it could have been written by someone who had never been here.

She pressed both palms flat to the earth.

The ground was cold. Not the cold of ice but the cold of depth, the temperature of things that have been still for a long time. She could feel the slight give of the topsoil under her palms, the firmness below. She could feel, or imagined she could feel — she was not sure the distinction mattered — the compacted fill beneath the new grass, and below that the undisturbed matrix, and below that the stratigraphy she had spent a year reading: stratum one, stratum two, the pipe bowl, the gorget, the pendant in its context of dark soil and crushed shell, and below all of it the chamber, the figure, the seven cuts on the raised arm.

The name.

She did not say it aloud. Nora had said it this morning, standing at the mound with her eyes closed and her hands flat on her knees, and the sound of it had moved through Tayak like the first cold water of a creek in summer — a shock and then a recognition, as if her body had known the shape of it before her mind caught up. She did not need to say it again. It was already in the ground. It had been in the ground for two thousand years, patient as the clay, waiting for someone to come back and press their hands flat and know they were not the first.

The light moved.

She watched it go across the mound's crown — the long, slow transit of a November afternoon, the shadows of the cedar posts stretching east, the laminated signs going dark as the angle dropped. A crow landed on the fence post at the far end of the site and sat there for a moment, its head cocked, regarding the field with the frank assessment of a creature that has no opinion about history. Then it dropped off the post and was gone.

Her knees ached. She was aware of this the way you are aware of a distant sound — present, noted, not urgent. She stayed where she was.

She thought about what Carver had told her, years ago on a different site, kneeling over a different feature in different soil. *You do not get to want things faster than the ground can give them.* She had understood it then as a rule about excavation. She understood it now as something larger — a rule about the relationship between a person and the thing they are trying to know. The ground had given her this. Not quickly. Not without cost. But it had given her this: the pendant, the names, the chamber, the figure with its seven cuts, and the name that her mother had spoken over her without knowing where it came from, the way you speak a word in a language you have forgotten you know.

The grass bent under her palms. She could feel the individual blades against the heel of her hand, the slight resistance of the soil beneath.

She had spent eleven years writing herself out of her own work. *The investigator notes. The field team observed. The stratigraphy indicates.* She had done it because it was correct, because the record had to stand without her, because the moment you put yourself in the report you gave someone a reason to discount the data. She did not regret it. The record stood. The commission had ruled. The mound was protected.

But she was here now. Not the investigator. Not the field director. Not the PI on the permit.

Here. Palms flat. The cold of the ground moving up through her hands.

She thought of Nora's quilt, the central panel with the woman figure in dark thread, arms raised, hands open. She thought of Wren's field drawings, the careful pencil lines of the chamber profile, the pendant in its matrix, the figure lying on its side with the arm extended. She thought of Ida's six typed pages, the careful withholding of the name that was not for paper. She thought of every layer she had documented, every person who had stood at this rise of ground and pressed something into the earth — a pendant, a gorget, a pipe bowl, a document wrapped in oilskin, a glass bead in a knotted cloth — and spoken a name into the soil so the ground would know it and hold it and give it back when the time was right.

She thought of the girl in the red jacket. *Whose names?* *Yours.*

The light was going. The western sky had gone the color of old copper, and the mound's crown caught it and held it for a moment — the subtle swell of the ground luminous in the flat, failing light, the asymmetry on the eastern slope visible to anyone who knew where to look. The grass on the crown moved in a wind she could not feel from where she crouched. It moved and settled and moved again, the way breath moves in a sleeping person.

Tayak's hands were numb at the fingertips. She did not lift them.

She stayed until the light changed, until the copper went out of the sky and the mound became simply a darker shape against the darkening field, until the cedar posts were silhouettes and the chain-link fence was a line of faint glints along the access road and the creek sounds rose in the quiet the way creek sounds always rise when the human noise stops — patient, continuous, indifferent to the century.

Then she lifted her palms from the earth.

She looked at her hands. The soil had left a faint print on both palms, the particular grey-brown of this ground, the river terrace clay that had been here longer than any name she knew. She did not brush it off.

She stood. Her knees straightened slowly. The field was dark at the edges now, the tree line to the east already black against a sky going from copper to violet. The mound was still visible — would always be visible, she thought, to anyone who knew how to read a rise in the ground, a subtle asymmetry, the way a place holds its history in the shape of itself.

She picked up her jacket from the fence post where she had left it and walked back along the perimeter path toward the gate. The mowed grass was wet with dew now, the cold coming up fast the way it does in November when the sun goes. Her boots darkened at the toe with each step.

At the gate she stopped and looked back.

The mound was a shape in the dark. A rise. A deliberate, two-thousand-year-old rise, holding its names in the cold below the grass, below the new growth, below the stratigraphy she had documented and the layers she had read and the chamber she had found and the figure she had measured and the name she had been given this morning by an eighty-two-year-old woman with her hands flat on her knees and her eyes closed.

Tayak stood at the gate for a moment.

Then she closed it behind her, heard the latch click, and walked to her car.
