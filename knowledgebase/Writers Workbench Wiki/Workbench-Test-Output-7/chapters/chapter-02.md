The cedar chest sat at the foot of the back bedroom's single bed, and Nora had not opened it in eleven months.

She knew the exact count because the last time had been the previous December, the night before the tribal council's annual meeting, when she had needed to check something she already knew. She had lifted the lid, looked at the folded panel on top of the stack, and closed the chest again without touching it. That had been enough.

Now she stood in the doorway. The bedroom was cold, she kept the gas heater off in rooms she wasn't using, a habit from decades when the bill had mattered more than comfort, and it still mattered, the way old habits mattered even when the reason had blurred. The pale winter light came through the thin curtain and lay across the floor tiles in a grey-blue stripe that reached the foot of the chest and stopped.

She crossed to it and knelt, which took a moment and cost something in her right knee, and lifted the lid.

Cedar smell rose first, sharp and clean, and under it the faint sweetness of the lavender sachets she replaced every two years. Under that, older: the dry, papery smell of fabric that had been folded for a long time in the dark, a smell like old letters, like a room that had been closed.

The panel lay on top, wrapped in a square of unbleached muslin she had sewn around it herself. She lifted it out. It weighed almost nothing, it always surprised her, the lightness of it, as though the weight she felt carrying it was somewhere else entirely.

She closed the chest and sat on the edge of the bed and set the panel in her lap.

For a moment she did not unwrap it. The muslin was smooth under her palms, worn to a near-translucency at the fold lines, and she could feel through it the slight stiffness of the panel's edges, the way the batting had compressed over years into something denser than it had started. Her hands knew the shape of it the way hands knew a thing they had held since before they were old.

She unwrapped it.

The panel was roughly the size of a pillowcase, pieced from cotton scraps in shades that had once been rust and indigo and a particular green that had no name she knew in English. Time had pulled everything toward ochre and grey. The stitching was in thread that had darkened to the color of old wood, fine and close, the kind of work that required good light and better eyes than she had now. Her grandmother Ida had done this stitching. Ida, who had taught school for thirty years and kept her hands busy in the evenings because stillness made her anxious, who had told Nora the whole of it on a single night in 1962, told it all at once because she had run out of time to wait for the right moment, and Nora had been twenty years old and had not understood half of what she heard, and had spent the next sixty-two years understanding more.

She spread the panel on the bed.

The stitching formed a map.

You had to know what you were looking at. If you didn't, it could have been a landscape in the abstract mode, ridgelines and water suggested by thread, a kind of decorative geography. But Nora knew. The long curved line at the top was the ridge, running east to west, the slight dip in the middle where the old oak had stood before the storm of 1972 took it. Below the ridge, the irregular shape filled with close parallel stitching was the swamp, the low ground that stayed wet from October to April and smelled of iron and rotting grass. And at the center, where the ridge curved and the high ground rose above the drainage, was the mound itself, rendered not from outside but from within, a cross-section, the interior chambers laid out in careful geometric pattern, each stratum marked by a different stitch: herringbone, chevron, a tight cross-stitch that Nora had always thought looked like the marks Ida made in her grade books when she was tallying something important.

Seven layers. Seven distinct zones of stitching, from the surface down to the deepest mark, which was a single horizontal line of stem stitch in thread so dark it was nearly black.

Ida had not told her what was in each layer. She had told her the layers were there, and she had told her that the map itself was the memory, that the stitching was a way of holding a shape that words alone could not hold, because words changed in the mouth from one generation to the next but thread stayed where you put it. She had said it the way she said things that mattered: quietly, without decoration, looking not at Nora but at the panel itself. *This is not just anything.* And then she had wrapped it back in its muslin and put it away and never taken it out again, and two years later she was gone, and the chest had come to Nora, and the panel with it.

Tayak had grown up in this house. She had played in this room. She had, as a child of perhaps six, pointed at the cedar chest and asked what was in it, and Nora had said *old things* in a tone that closed the question, and Tayak had accepted this the way children accepted the locked drawers and closed doors of the adults around them, as simply part of the landscape of the house, unremarkable, not worth pressing. She had never asked again. Nora was not sure whether this was because Tayak had forgotten or because she had understood, in the way perceptive children understood, that the answer was not yet hers to have.

*Which mound, exactly.*

She had asked it before Tayak named the ridge. She had known, or almost known, the moment Tayak walked in with the folder and her careful professional face and the particular brightness in her eyes that she got when something was going right. Nora had seen that brightness before, had seen it when Tayak defended her dissertation, when the first site permit came through, when she called from the field to say she'd found a stratum nobody had expected. It was the brightness of someone who believed they were moving toward something true.

Nora traced one finger along the ridge-line of the stitching. The thread was raised slightly from the fabric, and the pad of her finger followed it the way a finger followed a line on a map, reading the terrain by touch.

She had not lied to Tayak. She had not told her a single untrue thing. She had said *be careful what you look for*, which was true, and she had said it quietly, which was the only way she knew to say a thing that was true and insufficient at the same time.

What she had not said: that she had known about the mound since she was twenty years old. That Ida had drawn its interior in thread before Nora was born, from a description passed down through four women, each one adding what the one before had told her, the map growing more detailed with each generation not because anyone had dug into it but because the stories had been so precise, so repeated, so carefully kept, that the shape of the thing had stayed true across two centuries of telling.

What she had not said: that the deepest layer, the one marked in near-black stem stitch, held something that the stories named but that Nora had never spoken aloud to anyone, because speaking it would make it a claim, and a claim required proof, and the proof was in the ground, and the ground was now, finally, about to be opened.

Her finger stopped at the center of the cross-section, at the deepest mark.

She was eighty-two. She had been losing words for eight months, not dramatically, not in any way a doctor would call significant, just occasionally: a name that slipped sideways when she reached for it, a phrase that arrived a beat late, the small fraying at the edges of a mind that had always been precise. She noticed it the way she noticed the stiffness in her knee, as information, not catastrophe, but information that had a direction to it. Things moved in one direction only. She had time, but she did not have the luxury of waiting for the right moment anymore. Ida had taught her that.

She sat for a long time.

Outside, the first bus of the morning braked at the corner with its familiar hydraulic sigh, and somewhere down the block a dog voiced a single opinion about it and went quiet. The gas heater in the kitchen ticked as it cycled. The light through the curtain had shifted from grey-blue to the flat white of full morning, and the stitching on the panel was sharper now, the shadows in the seams more distinct, each herringbone row casting a thin line of shade on the row below it.

She did not put the panel away.

She folded the muslin back around it, not to hide it, just to protect it, and left it on the bed. Then she stood, slowly, and went back down the hall to the kitchen.

The permit copy was still on the table where Tayak had left it. The paper had warmed to the room's temperature now, and the official stamp, blue ink, slightly raised where the pen had pressed, caught the light from the window. *Maryland Historical Trust, Office of Archaeology. Authorization for Archaeological Investigation.* The coordinates were printed in a small font near the bottom of the page, and Nora did not need to read them. She knew the coordinates the way she knew the ridge-line: by the shape of the thing, by the feel of it under her hand.

She put the kettle on.

She stood at the counter waiting for the water to come up, and thought about what it meant that her granddaughter was at the ridge right now with her orange-vested student and her survey tape, flagging the high ground first, working down toward the swamp, moving with the careful professional patience of someone who had trained herself not to reach past what the evidence could tell her.

Nora had trained her well. She had taught Tayak to read, and to argue, and to hold a thing in her hands and ask what it was without deciding first. She had taught her, without ever naming it as a lesson, that the ground kept things and that the things it kept were true.

She had not taught her what was in the deepest layer. She had not taught her whose name was there.

The kettle began to knock against its element, working toward the boil.

There was a right moment. There had always been a right moment, and Nora had been waiting for it for sixty-two years, and it was possible, she allowed herself this, standing in her kitchen in the cold morning with the permit on the table and the panel on the bed and her granddaughter already at the ridge, it was possible that the right moment had finally arrived, and that she was the one who was not ready.

She poured the water. She drank her coffee standing at the counter, looking at the permit copy on the table, the paper sitting there patient and official in the morning light.

Then she picked up the phone and called Marcus.
