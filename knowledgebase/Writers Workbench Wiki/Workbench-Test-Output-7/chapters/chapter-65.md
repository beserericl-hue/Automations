The fire had been burning since midday, fed by the community's best oak, and by dusk it stood chest-high and threw its light across the faces of everyone Ranu had known for thirty years. He stood at the speaking place, the flat ground north of the mound where the earth had been packed smooth by ten thousand feet across ten thousand gatherings, and he felt the weight of all of them watching him.

He was the storyteller. He told stories the way other people breathed: without deciding to, without stopping to consider whether the air was worth taking in. He had told the story of Mekwa's father at this same fire, and Panu's grandmother, and the old man whose name had three syllables and a catch in the middle that Ranu had practiced for a week so he would not stumble on it in public. He had told the story of the drought years and the story of the flood that took the eastern field and the story of the boy who drowned in the river trying to prove something to his older brother. He had told those stories well. People had wept at the right moments and laughed at the right moments and gone home carrying something they had not had before.

He opened his mouth.

The fire popped, a sharp crack like a green branch splitting, and a spray of sparks rose and died in the dark above the crowd.

He closed his mouth.

The words that had come were not wrong words. They were the right words. They were the words he used for the dead: the formal opening, the name set in the center of the sentence like a stone placed in water, the ripple of the life moving outward from it. He knew those words so well they came without thought, which was the problem. They were shaped for other people's dead, ground smooth by use, and Taya was not other people's dead.

Taya was his daughter.

He stood at the speaking place and looked at the fire and tried to find a different beginning.

Around him the community waited with the patience they gave to storytellers, a particular quality of silence, not empty but held, the way you hold water in cupped hands. Yanu sat at the outer edge of the firelight, her face turned slightly away from him in the way she had when she was trying not to help. She knew he did not want her help. She also knew he needed it, and her solution was to look away so that he could fail without an audience for the failing.

He loved her for that. He was also, at this moment, too far inside his own chest to feel it properly.

He tried again.

*Taya was.*

The words stopped in his throat. Not because he did not know what came next. He knew exactly what came next. He could have told the whole shape of her life in the time it took the fire to settle: the seven years of it, the way she had learned to run before she had fully learned to walk, the fever that came on in the first hard frost and burned for seven days and then stopped burning. He knew the story. He had been telling it to himself since the morning they buried her, assembling it the way he assembled all stories, finding the line that ran through the middle of a life and following it.

But the line he found when he looked at Taya's life was not a story. It was a fact. She had been alive and now she was not. There was no line running through the middle of that. There was only the before and the after, and nothing in the after that explained the before.

He sat down.

Not slowly, not with ceremony. He simply sat down on the packed earth of the speaking place, cross-legged, the way he sat when he was listening rather than talking. Someone near the fire's edge made a small sound, not unkind, just surprised. The held silence broke into something softer, a murmur that moved through the crowd and then settled again.

Yanu did not look at him.

The fire burned. The oak had been burning since midday and it had gone to coals at the center now, the coals the deep red of something that has been giving heat for a long time and intends to keep giving it. Above the mound, the sky had gone from blue to the color of river water at dusk, that particular grey-green that has no name in any language Ranu knew, though he had tried once to describe it in a story and had used four words where one would have served if the word had existed.

He sat at the speaking place and said nothing, and the community sat with him, because that was what you did when the storyteller sat down. You waited. You did not fill the silence. The silence was part of the story even when there was no story.

An elder named Chawa shifted her weight on her sitting-stone, the ash-wood stick she walked with propped against her knee. She said nothing. Her face in the firelight was the face of someone who has waited out harder silences than this one.

The fire settled. The coals shifted with a sound like something breathing out.

Ranu looked at the mound.

It rose at the south edge of the firelight, its profile dark against the last grey of the sky, the long low shape of it, the earth packed tight by many hands over many years, the grass on its crown that caught the wind differently from the grass around it. He had stood at this mound for thirty years. He had told stories at this fire about the people inside it. He had never once, in thirty years, thought about what it would mean to tell a story about someone he had put there himself.

He understood now why some things did not get told at fires.

He stood up again.

The community stilled.

He opened his mouth, and the formal words came again, the shaped words, the words worn smooth by ten thousand dead. He stood there with them in his mouth like stones he could not swallow and could not spit out. Then he closed his mouth a third time, and this time he did not try again.

He walked back to his place at the fire's edge and sat down beside Yanu, who did not look at him and did not speak, and whose shoulder was warm against his.

The next night he tried again.

He had spent the day telling himself it would be different. He had found a way in, not the formal opening, not the shaped words worn smooth by the dead. Something smaller. He would begin with the morning she was born, the specific grey of that particular dawn, the way Yanu had made a sound he had never heard her make before or since. That was not a story about grief. That was a story about a morning. He could tell a story about a morning.

The fire was lower than the night before, built from hickory rather than oak, and it gave a sharper, thinner light. The community had gathered again, not all of them, fewer than the night before, but enough. Chawa was there, her ash-wood stick across her knees. Three of the younger men sat at the outer ring. Two women with a child between them. The night was cold enough that breath showed, small white puffs that rose and disappeared into the dark above the fire.

Ranu stood at the speaking place.

He said: *The morning Taya came into the world, the sky was.*

He said: *She was the kind of child who.*

He said: *I want to tell you about the way she.*

He heard himself.

That was the problem. He heard himself in a way he had never heard himself before, the voice slightly lifted, the rhythm slightly slowed, the particular music a story makes when it knows it is being heard. He had been speaking for three sentences and already the story was doing what stories do: shaping, selecting, making the thing smaller and truer and more bearable than it actually was. He could feel it happening the way you feel a current pulling at your legs in shallow water, not violent, almost gentle, the story drawing him toward the place where grief becomes something that can be held in the mouth and passed to another person.

He did not want to pass it to another person.

He stopped mid-word. The word was *her*, or the beginning of it, a sound that died before it finished, the way a coal dies when you pull it from the fire and the air hits it wrong.

The silence was different from the night before. Last night's silence had been surprised. This silence knew where it was.

Across the fire, an elder woman looked at him. He did not know her name well. She was from the settlement at the river's bend, one of the people who came for the seasonal gatherings and stayed at the mound's edge. She was older than Chawa, her face so deeply lined it seemed to have been carved rather than lived into, her eyes catching the firelight in two small points. She was not looking at him with pity. He had expected pity, had been bracing against it since the night before, the soft faces, the careful eyes of people who wanted to give him something and did not know what to give.

She was not giving him anything. She was simply looking at him with the face of someone who knew exactly what he was standing in and did not pretend otherwise. Recognition, without remedy.

He looked back at her for a moment that lasted longer than it should have.

Then he stepped back from the speaking place. Not sitting down this time, just stepping back, one step, as if the ground there had become too hot to stand on. He pressed the heel of his hand against his sternum, a gesture he did not intend, and looked down at the packed earth.

The fire crackled. The hickory was burning fast, the way hickory does, bright and brief. A child at the outer ring whispered something to its mother and was hushed.

He could not finish. That was the true thing, the thing the story kept trying to cover over. He could not finish because finishing required him to stand at enough distance from Taya to see her whole, and he could not stand at that distance. He was not a man standing outside a life, looking at its shape. He was a man still inside it, in the middle of the after, and the after had no shape yet. It was only weight.

He walked back to his place.

Yanu was watching him now. She had turned her face toward him, which she had not done the night before. He sat down beside her and she did not say anything for a long time, and the fire burned down to its quick hickory coals, and the community around them began to move and murmur and let the gathering dissolve into the ordinary sounds of the night.

"You keep trying to make it a story," Yanu said. She said it quietly, not as a criticism. As a fact.

"I know how to make things into stories."

"I know you do."

He looked at the mound. The crown of it was invisible now in the full dark, only the base of it lit by the dying fire, the earth pale and close-packed where many feet had worn the grass away.

"I don't know what else to do with it," he said.

Yanu was quiet for a moment. The elder woman across the fire had gone. He had not seen her leave, but her place at the fire's edge was empty, only the impression in the grass where she had sat.

"I know what to do," Yanu said. Not to him, exactly. To herself, or to the mound, or to the dark. She said it the way a person says something they have been thinking for a long time and have finally finished thinking.

He did not ask her what she meant. He was tired, and the fire was low, and the half-word he had started and could not finish was still sitting in his chest like a coal pulled from the fire too soon, still holding heat, not yet dark.

The third night he did not stand.

He found his place at the outer ring before the fire was fully built, settling onto the packed earth while others were still arriving, still arranging themselves in the familiar half-circle that the community made around the mound's base without being told to. He sat with his knees drawn up and his hands loose in his lap, and he watched the fire take hold: the dry oak catching first, then the heavier wood underneath, the flames climbing and steadying until the mound's lower slope came into the light.

The speaking place was empty.

He had stood in it for two nights. He would not stand in it tonight. He knew this the way he knew, some mornings, that the weather had changed before he stepped outside, a knowledge that lived in the body before the mind caught up with it. The speaking place was a hollow in the air above the packed earth, shaped by everyone who had ever stood there, and tonight it was not his.

Around him, people settled. The settlement at the river's bend had sent fewer this third night. The families from the eastern slope were present, the ones who had been at all three nights, their children quieter now, worn down by three nights of sitting in the cold. The air had dropped since midday. He could smell the river in it, the particular cold-mud smell that came off the water when the temperature fell fast, a smell that always meant the season was turning for good, that the warm days were finished.

Chawa sat two places to his left, her stick across her knees. She had not looked at him yet. She was watching the fire with the patient, slightly inward expression she wore when she was waiting for something she was not sure would come.

The speaking place stayed empty.

He watched it. He was aware, in a way he had not been on the first two nights, of what the empty space was doing. It was not passive. An empty speaking place at a gathering was a presence, not an absence. It pulled attention the way a gap in a fence pulls the eye, the way silence after a sound is louder than the silence before it. People were aware of it. He could see the awareness in the way they arranged themselves, the slight angle of heads, the careful not-looking that was its own kind of looking.

He had put that emptiness there. Two nights of stopping mid-word had made it.

He felt something that was not quite shame and not quite grief, a third thing, closer to the feeling of having broken a tool you depended on and not yet knowing how to work without it.

Then Senu stood up.

Ranu knew him, a young man, perhaps twenty-two or twenty-three, broad through the shoulders, with the kind of face that was always slightly more serious than his age warranted. He hunted well. He had a wife and, recently, a child not yet walking. He was not a storyteller by nature or by practice. He was the kind of man who listened to stories rather than told them, who sat at the outer ring and laughed at the right moments and went home and did not think much about the words afterward.

He stood at the outer edge of the speaking place, not quite in it, with the careful posture of a man who was not sure he was allowed to be where he was standing.

He said: "I want to tell about the hunt at the north ridge."

His voice was steady but slightly too loud, the way voices get when someone is compensating for uncertainty with volume. A few people shifted. One of the children at the outer ring looked up.

"Three days ago," Senu said. "Before the cold came in."

And then he began.

It was a hunting story. A good one, as it turned out. Ranu had not expected it to be good, had expected something rough and serviceable, something that would fill the silence the way you stuff bark into a gap to keep the wind out. But Senu told it well. He had the instinct, even without practice, of knowing where to slow down and where to move fast. He told about tracking a doe from the creek bottom up through the hickory stands where the ground was soft enough to hold prints, losing the track on the rocky outcrop, finding it again where she had paused to drink from a shallow pool that had not yet frozen over. He told it with his hands, the way hunters do, showing the angle of the prints, the direction of the wind, the moment he had gone still behind a deadfall and waited.

The fire burned. The cold settled.

People listened.

Ranu listened.

He had not meant to. He had meant to sit at the outer ring and be a man enduring an evening, waiting for it to end so he could go home. But Senu's voice had the quality that good stories have, not performance, not decoration, but the quality of someone who has seen a thing and wants you to see it too. Ranu found himself following it the way you follow a path in the dark by keeping your feet on the packed earth.

The doe had gone up the ridge. Senu had followed. The light was going. He had one throw left before the dark made it impossible, and the angle was wrong, and he had to decide whether to take the throw or let her go and come back in the morning.

He took the throw.

The fire crackled. A piece of wood shifted and sent a brief column of sparks up into the dark.

He had missed, Senu said, and his voice had the rueful flatness of a man who had made peace with a fact. The doe had gone over the ridge and he had stood there in the failing light with the wrong angle and the thrown weapon on the ground and no meat and a long walk home. He had stood there for a moment, he said, and looked at the place where she had been. Then he had picked up his weapon and gone home.

That was the end of the story.

There was a brief, warm sound from the people around the fire, not laughter exactly, more the sound of recognition, the small collective exhale that a story draws out of an audience when it lands true.

Ranu sat with his hands in his lap and understood something.

He understood it the way you understand a thing that has been true for a long time but that you have not looked at directly, not as a new thought but as a thought that had finally turned to face him.

Senu's story had a shape. It had a beginning and a middle and an end. It had a wrong angle and a missed throw and a long walk home. It was complete. You could hold it in your hands, turn it over, pass it to someone else, and they would receive it whole.

Taya did not have a shape yet.

She was not a thing that had finished happening. She was still happening, in him, in Yanu, in the hollow place at the fire where her name should have been and was not. She was still the weight in his chest that had not yet become anything else. She was still the sound he had started on the second night and could not finish, the half-word that had died in his mouth like a coal pulled from the fire too soon.

You could not make a story out of a thing that was still happening to you.

He had been trying to do it anyway, because making stories was what he knew how to do, the way a man who knows how to carve will reach for a blade when he does not know what else to do with his hands. He had been reaching for the only tool he had, and the tool was the wrong shape for the work, and he had not understood that until now, listening to Senu tell about a doe going over a ridge in the failing light and a long walk home with no meat.

That story was finished. Senu had let it be finished.

Taya was not finished.

The fire had burned down to its coals by the time Senu sat back down, and people around the ring were beginning the small movements that meant the gathering was ending, children lifted, cloaks pulled close, voices dropping to the murmur of people saying their ordinary goodnights. The cold was sharp now. His breath made a small cloud in front of his face each time he exhaled.

He stayed where he was.

Chawa rose and walked past him, and her hand touched his shoulder once, briefly, without stopping, the lightest possible touch, the kind that says *I see you* without requiring anything back. He did not look up. He heard her footsteps on the packed earth, moving away toward the path that led back to the settlement.

The mound was dark. The fire's last coals gave just enough light to see the base of it, the pale worn earth, the grass gone thin where the feet of three nights had pressed it down. He looked at it for a long time.

He was not a man standing outside Taya's life, looking at its shape. He was a man still inside the after. The after had no shape yet. It was only weight. Weight was not a story. It had to be carried before it could be told, and he had not yet carried it long enough, and there was no way to know how long it would take.

He got up slowly, his knees stiff from the cold ground, and walked home.

The fire had gone out.

He had not tended it when he came in, and it had gone out while he slept, and now the hearth was cold ash and one dark coal that gave nothing. The house was the color of nothing, not black exactly, but the absence of color that comes before first light, when the eye reaches for shapes and finds only the approximate.

He lay still for a moment. Yanu was asleep on the far side of the sleeping platform, her breathing slow and even, one arm curved around the place where Taya had slept. She did not know she did it. He had watched her do it every night since the seventh day. He did not tell her. Some things you watch and do not speak.

He got up carefully, without sound.

The packed earth was cold under his feet. He found his way to the storage rack by memory, three steps from the sleeping platform, turn, two more, and his hands found the antler pieces he kept there, the ones he had been meaning to work into handles for the next season's tools. His fingers moved over them in the dark. Most were too thick, too long, the wrong shape for what he did not yet know he was making. One was different. A short piece, curved slightly at one end, dense and smooth from years of handling. He had taken it from a young doe two winters ago. He had been keeping it without knowing why.

He took it and sat down on the mat beside the dead hearth.

He did not light the fire. He sat in the dark with the antler piece in both hands and waited for the light to come.

It came slowly, the way it always did in the cold months, a grey thinning at the smoke hole above, then a pale line along the bottom edge of the door mat, then enough to see his hands. He could see the antler piece now. It was the length of his palm, curved, pale where the outer layer had been worn away, darker at the dense core. He turned it over. He turned it again.

He did not know what he was going to make.

That was not true. He knew, in the way that the body knows a thing before the mind has named it. He had known when his fingers found it in the dark. He had known on the walk home from the mound, though he had not let himself think it directly, the way you do not look straight at a fire because the looking will ruin your sight for everything around it.

He reached for his blade, the small flint one, the one he used for fine work, kept wrapped in a scrap of deer hide on the rack above the hearth. He unwrapped it and held it.

He began.

He did not plan. Planning was for things that needed to come out right. This did not need to come out right. It only needed to come out true, and those were different requirements entirely.

He worked the thick end first, removing material in short careful strokes, the blade biting into the antler and lifting thin pale shavings that fell onto his thighs and the mat around him. The smell rose immediately, faint and specific, something between bone and wood, the chalky sharpness of worked antler that he knew from years of making tools, familiar and this morning more present than usual, or he was more present than usual. He had worked antler many times. This morning the smell seemed to ask something of him.

The curved end he left. He did not know why. He left it and worked around it, reducing the thick straight section, narrowing it, taking off the rough outer surface until the denser inner material showed through, smoother, slightly darker, with a faint grain like compressed time.

The light was strengthening. He could see his work clearly now.

He was not making a figure of a child. He was not making a representation of anything. He was making the simplest possible shape that meant *small* and *curved* and *weight held in the hand*. He was removing everything that was not that. The shavings accumulated around him on the mat, pale curls of antler, and the shape in his hands grew smaller and more itself with each stroke.

Yanu's breathing changed. He heard it, the slight shift that meant she was moving toward waking, and then it steadied again and she went back down into sleep. He did not look up. He kept his eyes on his hands.

The blade slipped once and took more material than he intended from the narrow section, and he sat for a moment with the piece in his hands and looked at what remained. It was thinner there now, more delicate. He turned it. The thinness was not wrong. It made the curve at the end more pronounced, gave the whole piece a quality he had not planned, a slight forward lean, as if the shape were resting its weight on something, or into something, the way a small child leans into a body it trusts.

He did not correct it.

He took the sandstone abrader from the rack, a flat piece worn smooth on one face from years of use, and began to work the surface. This was slower work. The abrader moved in small circles, smoothing the blade marks, bringing the surface to a consistency that was not polished but not rough either, something between the two, the texture of a thing that has been handled.

He worked the curved end last. He did not add detail. He did not incise a face or mark a head or make it more than it was. He smoothed the curve and left it. The shape did not look like Taya. It did not look like anyone. It looked like the idea of smallness, of a weight that fits in a closed hand, of the particular density that a young child has, that solidity that surprises you every time you lift them, the way they are heavier than they look, more real than you expect.

He set the abrader down.

The light was full now. Through the smoke hole the sky had gone from grey to the pale white of a cold clear morning. He could hear the settlement beginning, a dog, someone's fire being coaxed, the sound of a clay pot set down on packed earth somewhere to the north. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a morning that did not know what had happened and did not need to.

He held the figure in his closed hand.

He did not speak over it. He had spoken over everything for three days, at the gathering, at the fire, in the night when the words came and he let them come because stopping them felt worse than speaking. He had spoken until the speaking had shown him its own limits, shown him the shape of what it could not do. He held the figure and was quiet.

It was the most honest thing he had ever made. It had no story attached to it. It was not the beginning of a story or the end of one. It was not a record of what had happened or a vessel for what he needed to say. It was a shape that meant *she was here* and *she was small* and *I carried her*, and that was all it meant, and that was enough.

The shavings lay around him on the mat, pale against the dark fiber. He looked at them for a moment, all the material he had removed to find this shape, and then he looked at the figure in his hand.

He heard Yanu wake. The small sounds of it: the shift of her body, a breath drawn differently, a pause. Then stillness, the way she went still each morning in the moment before she remembered, and then the slight change in the quality of her stillness when she did remember. He knew that pause. He lived in it himself, every morning, the brief moment of ordinary before the weight returned.

He turned to face her.

She was sitting up on the sleeping platform, her hair loose, her eyes finding him across the dim space of the house. She looked at him, and then at his hands, and then at him again.

He held out the figure.

She did not move for a moment. Then she came off the platform and crossed the few steps between them and sat down beside him on the mat, close enough that her shoulder was against his. She took the figure from his open hand. She held it the way he had held it, closed fist, the shape contained. She did not look at it. She looked at the dead hearth.

He waited.

Outside, the dog barked once and went quiet. The fire sounds continued from the north side of the settlement. The light through the smoke hole was steady and cold and clear.

Yanu opened her hand and looked at the figure.

She looked at it for a long time. He watched her face and did not speak. Her face did not do what faces did when they were about to speak. It did not gather itself, did not shift toward the small preparations of expression that meant words were coming. It stayed still. It stayed with the figure.

Then she closed her hand around it again.

"I know what to do," she said.

Her voice was quiet and certain, the way a voice is when it is not asking for agreement or explanation, when it is simply reporting a thing that has become clear. He did not ask what she meant. He understood, not the specific thing she intended, which he could not have named, but the quality of the knowing, the way it had arrived in her. He had felt that quality himself, the night before, listening to Senu's story, when the understanding had turned to face him. Some things you know before you know what you know. You trust the knowing and wait for the rest.

He put his arm around her.

She leaned into him, the figure still closed in her fist between them, and they sat together in the cold house in the full morning light with the shavings of antler on the mat around them and the hearth cold and the settlement waking outside. He did not ask, and she did not explain, and the trust between them was the most important thing he had ever done, though he would not have called it that, because he was not a man who named the things he was doing while he was doing them.

He was a man who told stories.

This was not a story.

This was the thing that came before stories, the weight, the shape, the silence that a thing has before anyone has spoken it. He held that silence and let it be what it was.

The morning deepened around them. The cold in the house did not lift but it changed, became the cold of a place where two people were breathing rather than one. Ranu felt the warmth of her shoulder against his and did not move.

After a while Yanu straightened. Not pulling away from him, just settling into her own weight, the way she did when she had decided something. She opened her hand one more time and looked at the figure. Turned it once, slowly, the way he had turned it in the night. Her thumb moved across the curve he had left at the top, the place where the shoulders met, the slight roundness that meant *carried* without meaning anything else. Then she closed her fingers around it.

She stood.

He watched her cross to the sleeping platform and pull her outer robe from the peg above it, the heavy one, deer hide with the fur turned in, worn at the right hem where she always caught it on the door frame. She put it on. She tucked the figure somewhere inside the folds, against her body. He could not see where. She did not show him.

She looked at him.

He looked back at her.

"I'll be back before midday," she said.

He nodded.

She went out. The door mat swung back behind her and the cold air came in for a moment and then the house was still again.

He sat on the mat among the antler shavings. The pale chips had dried completely now, curled slightly at their edges, some of them light enough that her movement through the house had shifted them. He did not sweep them up. He looked at the dead hearth. The ash was white and fine, the way it went when a fire had burned completely down and nothing had been added for hours. The hearthstones were dark with years of use, their surfaces cracked in the patterns that heat makes in stone when it is applied and removed enough times, a map of all the fires that had burned here, all the mornings, all the nights.

He had built this hearth. He remembered the morning, late autumn, the year they had come to this place, the ground still soft enough to seat the stones. Taya had not yet been born. He had not yet known she would be born. He had set the stones and thought: *this is where we will be warm.* That was all he had thought. He had not known what would happen here, what would be cooked here, what conversations would take place in the light of the fire he was making a place for. You never know, when you are making a place for something, what that something will contain.

He got up. His knees ached from the hours on the mat, the cold having settled into them while he worked, unnoticed until now. He went to the door and lifted the mat and looked out.

The settlement was fully awake. Across the open ground he could see Senu's fire, a small bright point in the gray morning air, and the shape of Senu moving near it. Two children ran between the houses to the south, their voices thin and high in the cold. The sky above the tree line was the pale, washed color that came after frost, and the frost itself was still on the thatch of the neighboring house, white along the north-facing edge, already melting where the sun had reached it.

He could see Yanu. She was moving along the path that led up toward the high ground, her robe dark against the pale grass, the figure invisible inside it. She walked the way she walked when she had made up her mind, not fast, but with a settled quality, each step placed as if she had already been where she was going and knew what the ground felt like underfoot.

He watched her until the tree line took her.

Then he let the door mat fall and went to rebuild the fire.

He worked by feel, the familiar sequence of it, the small dry bark first, then the split wood laid across at angles to let the air move. He had done this ten thousand times. His hands knew it without him. He took the fire-bow from its place beside the hearth, set the spindle into the hearthboard, and worked it until the friction caught, a thin thread of smoke rising from the notch, and then a coal, small and red and steady. He cupped his hands around the tinder and breathed it carefully until it had hold of the bark and did not need him anymore.

He sat back on his heels and watched it grow.

The smoke rose and found the hole above and went out into the morning. The warmth began, not the warmth of a full fire yet, just the promise of it, the leading edge. He held his hands out toward it.

He thought: *she knows what to do.*

He did not know what she was going to do. He understood that she was going to the mound, the direction of her walking had told him that much, and the figure inside her robe told him more. But he did not know the specific thing, the arrangement of it, the words she would or would not say. He had not asked. He had felt no pull toward asking.

This surprised him, a little, even now. He was a man who asked. He was a man who wanted to understand the shape of a thing before it happened, who turned situations over in his mind the way he turned antler in his hands, looking for the grain. He had spent three days trying to find the right approach to Taya's story, the angle, the entry point, the form that would let him carry it without dropping it. He had tried and tried and the trying had shown him only what could not be done.

And then he had made the figure instead. And then he had given it to Yanu. And then she had said *I know what to do* and he had believed her without needing to know what she meant.

Sitting by the young fire, he understood that this was its own kind of knowledge. Not the knowledge of a man who has thought his way through to an answer. The knowledge of a man who has reached the edge of what thinking can do and found that the edge is not the end. Beyond it there is someone else who knows the next part, and trusting them to know it is not the same as not knowing. It is knowing that you are not the only one.

He had told stories his whole life. He knew the structure of them, the way a story needed someone to act, someone to receive the action, something to change between the beginning and the end. He had always been the one who held the structure, who knew where the story was going even when the people inside it did not.

This was not that. In this, he was not the one who held the structure. He had made the figure and given it to Yanu and she had taken it and gone, and the story, if it was a story, if it would become one, was in her hands now, not his.

The fire had taken hold. The hearthstones were beginning to warm. He could feel the heat on his face and on the backs of his hands, and the cold in the house was retreating toward the walls. He added a heavier piece of wood and watched the fire receive it.

Outside, the settlement went about its morning. He could hear it without seeing it, the ordinary texture of people doing the things people do, the sounds that did not know what had happened and did not need to. He sat inside the sounds and let them be around him.

He thought: *she was here. She was small. I carried her.*

The figure was gone from his hands. Yanu had it. He had nothing to hold. He put his open hands on his knees, palms up, and felt the fire's warmth move across them.

*This is enough,* he thought. *This is the most I can do, and it is enough.*

He did not know, could not have known, sitting in the warming house with the antler shavings still on the mat around him, that what he had done would last. That the figure would go into the ground and the ground would keep it. That the keeping would outlast him and Yanu and everyone they knew and everyone those people knew, down through more generations than he had numbers for, until a woman with careful hands and a different language would lift it from the earth and hold it in gloved fingers and write its description in a log, and the description would be inadequate, as all descriptions are, but the figure itself would still mean what it had always meant: *she was here, she was small, I carried her.*

He did not know any of this. He sat by his fire and waited for Yanu to come home.
