The fence at Site 18CH-447 was eight feet of chain-link topped with two strands of orange construction tape that had gone pink in the weather, and Tayak stood outside it with her back to the mound because she could not look at it while she was on the phone. The January air tasted of frozen mud and something faintly mineral. The creek, two hundred yards downslope, exhaled cold through the bare sycamores. She had her field jacket zipped to the throat and her notebook in her left hand, pen clipped to the cover, though she had not written anything. The notebook was eleven. She had filled ten before it.

Marcus had been talking for four minutes. She counted without meaning to.

"The vote was six to two," he said. "Institutional support, legal backing, the full weight of the council's mandate. We get this to the commission before the thirty days expire and we have a case."

"Who voted no?"

"Eleanor and Delia Swann."

Tayak had expected that. Eleanor had been consistent. *Obligation to the ancestors is prior to evidentiary arguments.* She had said it in the first meeting and she would say it in the last one.

"The attorney," Tayak said. "Ashby."

"He reviewed the MOU last night. The voidance clause is solid. Farrow's people pulled the alternate road alignment in February without notice. That's the material breach. Ashby says we can move."

She looked down at her boots. The ground here was hard with frost, the dead grass pressing flat where she had stood for the past ten minutes. She had driven out at four-thirty and sat in the truck until first light, watching the mound's silhouette come up out of the dark the way it always did. Reluctantly, by degrees, as if it had to decide each morning whether to be visible.

"Wills," she said.

"Bulldozers are scheduled for day thirty-one." Marcus paused. She heard him breathe. "Which means we need something from that ground in twenty-two days, Tayak. Not a theory. Physical evidence. Pre-contact stratigraphy that the commission can't set aside."

Twenty-two days. She had been at this site for eleven months. She had filed the permits, the field assessments, the Section 106 consultation, the NAGPRA obligations. She had fought the suspension notice and the hearing and the NSF proposal that had stripped her name from her own coordinates. She had read Nora's affidavit in the trench at dusk with her hands shaking. She had done everything the institutions asked of her and then she had done it again when the first version wasn't enough.

Twenty-two days.

"The deepest layer," she said.

"That's your call."

"I know it is." She turned and looked at the mound for the first time that morning. It rose maybe twelve feet above the surrounding terrace, gentle-shouldered, covered in brown January grass, and it looked like nothing. It looked like a rise in the ground. That was the point. That was always the point. "I'll call the team in this morning."

"Good." She heard him moving, a chair, a door. "There's one more thing. The Doomsday Clock announcement came out last week. Ninety seconds to midnight. Same as last year."

She waited.

"I'm not saying it's relevant," Marcus said. "I just, Eleanor mentioned it in the meeting. She said we keep measuring how close we are to the end of things and we never actually change the things that are bringing us there. She was talking about the mound, not the bomb. But she said it like it was the same sentence."

Tayak said nothing.

"You have what you need," Marcus said.

She held the phone against her ear for a moment after he stopped talking, listening to the static that wasn't quite silence. Then she said, "Thank you, Marcus," and ended the call.

She stood at the fence. The mound was forty yards away and the frost on the grass caught the low January sun at an angle that made the whole surface look briefly silver. Then the angle changed and it was just brown again. Somewhere down by the creek a great blue heron gave its single flat call, that prehistoric complaint, like something tearing. Then silence.

*What she needs is still in the ground.*

She had not said it to Marcus because he would have heard it as doubt, and it wasn't doubt. It was the only true thing she knew this morning. Nora had given her the chain of names. The affidavit had given her the paper trail. The stratigraphy had given her five layers of continuous occupation going back to contact and beyond. But the bottom layer, the founding layer, the one Nora would not discuss on the phone, the one she had said *I will tell you when you are standing in front of it*, that layer was still sealed.

She had not opened it because she had not been ready to be wrong. Carver had taught her that. *You do not get to want things faster than the ground can give them.* Cecile Marant had taught her the rest: *do not decide what you are looking at until you have looked long enough to be wrong twice.*

She had been wrong once already. She had thought the copper pendant was the bottom. It wasn't.

She opened her notebook and wrote the date and the time: *January 2024. 06:47.* Then she wrote: *22 days.* Then she drew a line under it. Then she wrote the field number: *18CH-447.* The way Carver had taught her. File number first, always, before anything else, so you know where you are.

She clipped the pen back to the cover and put the notebook in her jacket pocket. The heron called again, closer this time. She looked toward the creek but couldn't see it through the sycamores.

She took out her phone and called Wren.

The team arrived in stages. Wren first, at seven forty-five, her breath making small clouds above the site table as she set down her field bag and pulled on her nitrile gloves without being asked. Then Delia, in the proper field jacket, water bottles clipped to both sides of her pack. Then Osei, who had borrowed a fleece again, a different one this time, dark green, two sizes too large, and arrived with coffee in a paper cup that steamed in the cold air. Elspeth came last, her Total Station case banging against her hip as she came down the field road, and beside her a woman Tayak had not seen in four months: Priya Suresh, geologist, coat buttoned to the throat, hands already in her pockets.

Tayak had not called Priya. Priya had simply appeared.

"I heard you were going to the bottom," Priya said.

"From who."

"Marcus. He texted me at six."

Tayak looked at her. Priya's face gave nothing back. She had a mug at home that read GEOLOGY ROCKS and she wore her coat indoors in November and she had forwarded Tayak the NSF listing that started the whole hearing, and she was here now because she had decided to be here and no other explanation was going to be offered.

"You're not on the permit," Tayak said.

"I'm a geologist standing in a field. There's no law against that."

Tayak let it go.

The site table was a sheet of plywood on two sawhorses, set up at the western edge of the excavation grid, close enough to the trench that you could see the stratigraphy profile on the east wall from where you stood. Tayak had covered it with a tarp against the frost, and she pulled the tarp back now and laid out the stratigraphy chart, the one she had drawn herself, by hand, in field notebook eleven, and then photocopied at the county library because the university printer had been down for a week. Six sheets, taped end to end. The full sequence from surface to the sealed base layer, each stratum labeled with its depth, its material signature, its associated finds.

She weighted the corners with field bags and a thermos of coffee that Delia had brought and that no one had opened yet.

The five of them stood on the other side of the table and looked at it.

The January light was flat and cold, coming in low from the southeast, and it put everything in the same gray-white wash. The mound rose behind Tayak's left shoulder. She could feel it the way you feel a wall in the dark. Not touching it, just aware of it, its mass displacing something in the air.

"You've all seen parts of this," she said. "Today I want you to see all of it."

She ran her finger along the chart from top to bottom.

"Stratum one: contact period, late seventeenth century. Glass bead, consistent with Dutch or English trade goods. A colonial document, oilskin-wrapped, two copies. We have the document. We have the bead. We have the Calvert patent reference placing this site on the colonial survey record as a Piscataway burial ground in 1687."

Wren's jaw tightened. Tayak did not look at her.

"Stratum two: ceramic pipe bowl, mid-eighteenth century. Consistent with Piscataway manufacture. Stratum three: shell gorget, worked marine shell, pre-contact style, probably late woodland. Stratum four: a second gorget, older, different hand, deeper wear. Stratum five: the copper pendant."

She stopped. The thermos of coffee shifted in the wind and Delia caught it.

"The pendant is Great Lakes copper. Cold-hammered. The form is consistent with late woodland to early Chesapeake tradition, but the material is not local. Someone carried it here, or traded it here, or received it here from someone who had carried it a long way. The AMS date we're waiting on will tell us more. But the pendant is not the bottom."

Osei looked up. He was holding his coffee cup in both hands and his face above the steam was careful and attentive.

"Below the pendant," Tayak said, "there is a sealed layer. It has not been opened. The soil matrix at that depth shows no disturbance since deposition. What is in it, I don't know for certain. But I have a document that tells me what to expect."

She reached into her jacket and took out the folded photocopy. Not the original. The original was in Nora's house on Billingsley Road, where it had been for sixty-two years, and it was going to stay there until the hearing. This was the copy she had made with her phone, crouched in the trench at dusk, reading it a second time with her hands steadier than they'd been the first time.

She unfolded it and laid it flat on the chart, weighting one corner with her thumb because the wind was picking up from the north.

"This is an affidavit. Sworn in 1962. The woman who swore it held oral history of this site going back, by her account and by the chain of names she documents, to approximately one hundred BC."

Nobody moved.

"The affidavit describes a founding burial. A woman. Placed at the base of what became this mound. The associated grave object is described as a copper pendant placed face-up in the earth." She looked at the chart and then at them. "The same pendant we recovered from stratum five was not the founding deposit. It was a later placement, a keeper, someone who had held it for a generation or more, placing it back into the ground. The founding deposit, if the affidavit is accurate, is below it."

The wind moved across the plywood and the photocopied pages shifted under her thumb.

"The state has designated this site a colonial landmark. Their basis is the 1687 Calvert patent. Their argument is that the site's period of significance begins with colonial contact and that the relevant history is therefore colonial history. If that designation stands, the site goes to Chesapeake Heritage Development in twenty-two days and the bulldozers follow on day thirty-one."

She looked at each of them in turn. Wren, who had been on this site for two months and whose face was composed and still. Delia, who had found the cord-marked sherd on the eastern face in September and who was now holding the thermos with both hands as if she needed something to hold. Osei, who had come underdressed on the first day and had not made that mistake again. Elspeth, who ran the Total Station and kept the measurements and had not yet said a word this morning. Priya, who had no official role here and had come anyway.

"The colonial designation cannot survive pre-contact stratigraphy. If the founding layer is what the affidavit says it is, a burial, a pendant, a continuous chain of occupation going back two thousand years, then the state's argument fails. Not weakens. Fails."

She folded the photocopy and put it back in her jacket.

"What I need from you is the deepest layer. Documented to standard. Chain of custody complete. Every photograph, every measurement, every soil sample. I need it done in a way that the commission cannot set aside, that no opposing expert can fault, and that holds up in a federal hearing if it comes to that."

The flat call of a great blue heron came up from the creek, that single prehistoric complaint, and then silence.

"We are going to the deepest layer."

Nobody spoke.

The wind moved the dead grass on the mound's southern face. Somewhere in the sycamores a woodpecker started and stopped. The frost on the plywood table had not yet melted and the stratigraphy chart lay across it like a map of something that had taken two thousand years to make and twenty-two days to lose.

Wren was the one who spoke.

She was looking at the chart, not at Tayak, and her voice was level and without drama.

"What's the name?" she said. "In the affidavit. The woman at the bottom. Is there a name?"

Tayak had known the question was coming. She had known it since six forty-seven that morning when she had written *22 days* in her notebook and drawn the line under it. She had known it since the night in the trench when she had read the affidavit the first time and her hands had started shaking and she had not been able to say clearly whether they were shaking from cold or from something else.

"There is a name," she said. "I haven't spoken it yet."

Wren looked up.

"When?" Wren said.

"When we're standing in front of it."

Wren held her gaze for a moment. Then she nodded, once, and looked back at the chart.

"We'll be ready," she said.

Tayak believed her.

She picked up her field bag from the ground and slung it over her shoulder. The bag was heavy, notebook, trowel, evidence bags, the camera, the small brush roll that Marant had given her in her second field year and that she had used on every site since. She had carried it for eleven months on this particular ground and the strap had worn a groove into the shoulder of her jacket that she had stopped noticing somewhere around month four.

"Elspeth," she said. "I need the Total Station on the north marker by eight-thirty. We're opening the base layer from the center, working outward. No guessing on depth. Every five centimeters we stop and document."

"Yes."

"Delia and Osei, east and west transects, same as before. If the soil matrix changes at any point, you stop and you call me. You don't decide what you're looking at. You call me."

They nodded.

"Wren." She paused. "You're with me."

Wren picked up her field bag without a word.

Priya had not moved from her place at the end of the table. She was looking at the stratigraphy chart with the expression she wore when she was reading rock, that particular stillness, the slight narrowing of the eyes that meant she was measuring something against something else she already knew.

"Priya," Tayak said.

"Mm."

"The soil at the base layer. If I need a read on deposition sequence."

"I'm already here," Priya said, without looking up from the chart.

Tayak turned toward the mound.

The frost on the brown grass caught the low sun at the same angle it had caught it at six forty-seven, and for a moment the whole southern face went silver. Then the angle shifted and it was just a rise in the ground again. Twelve feet of earth and two thousand years of keeping and twenty-two days left on the clock.

She started walking.

The notebook was number eleven in the series she had kept since her second field year. She had filled ten before this one, sites in Virginia, the Patuxent lowlands, two seasons on the Eastern Shore, the preliminary survey here at 18CH-447 before the permit came through. Carver had told her to write the file number first, always, before anything else, so that if the notebook was found separated from its context someone would know what it belonged to. She had done that on every one. She did it now.

*18CH-447, Field Notebook 11, Moyaone, T.*

Then, on the next line, she wrote the date. Then, on the line below that, she wrote:

*22 DAYS*

She drew a line under it with the edge of her thumbnail because she had not thought to bring a ruler and the line needed to be there. She read it back to herself once, the way Carver had taught her to read measurements back. Not to confirm what you wanted to see but to make sure you had written what you meant. Then she turned the notebook so the team could see it.

They were gathered at the plywood table, the stratigraphy chart still spread across it, the frost on the plywood now beginning to pull back from the edges where the low sun reached. Wren was to her left, standing close enough that Tayak could see the fine print of the field log she was holding, column headers, evidence numbers, the careful block letters Wren used for context descriptions. Delia had set the thermos down at last and had her own notebook open, pen uncapped. Osei was looking at the chart. Elspeth was looking at the mound.

"Twenty-two days," Tayak said. "That's the working window. Day twenty-three, the commission reviews. If the evidence package is incomplete, or if it fails to meet federal documentation standards, the review proceeds without us."

She set the notebook flat on the table and uncapped her own pen.

"Elspeth. North marker, Total Station, eight-thirty. I want a fresh datum established for the base layer. We are not extrapolating from the stratum five measurements. New datum, new record."

Elspeth pulled a small spiral pad from her vest pocket and wrote something in it. "Do you want me to run a second check line off the creek bearing, or anchor off the original site datum?"

"Original datum. If there's any discrepancy, I want to know about it before we're a foot down, not after."

Elspeth nodded and wrote again.

"Delia, Osei. East and west transects, same assignments as before. You are going to depth targets, not to features. I need the soil matrix documented at every five-centimeter interval. Color, texture, inclusion density. If the matrix changes character between intervals, if you feel a shift before you see one, you stop. You put your tools down and you call me. You do not decide what you are looking at. That is my job."

Delia had already written *5 cm intervals* and underlined it twice.

"Osei," Tayak said.

"Yes."

"The west transect is going to be slower. The deposition sequence on that side is compressed. You saw it in stratum five, the layers are thinner and they're not running perfectly horizontal. If you hit what looks like a disturbed zone, don't assume it's intrusive. Call me."

"Understood."

She looked down at the notebook. The two words sat there on the page, blunt and factual as a survey measurement. She had written harder things in field notebooks, feature descriptions that required a steadiness of hand she had not always felt, context notes for finds she had not been ready for. But those had been objects belonging to people she knew only as stratigraphy. This was different, and she had not yet decided what to do with the difference, so she set it aside the way she set aside everything she could not resolve in the field. She wrote around it, she documented what she could see, she kept her hands moving.

"Chain of custody," she said. "Every find goes through the evidence log before it goes anywhere else. Every photograph gets a scale bar and a north arrow. Every soil sample gets a context number that matches the log. If it doesn't have a context number it doesn't exist. We are building a record that a federal commission can read without us in the room to explain it. That means nothing is assumed. Nothing is implied. Everything is written down."

Priya, still at the end of the table, had produced a small notebook of her own, a geologist's field book, narrow and waterproof, with graph paper inside. She was making marks in it that Tayak recognized as a stratigraphic column sketch, working from the chart. She had not been asked to do this. She was doing it because she was Priya and because she had been doing it since she walked onto the site forty minutes ago with her coat buttoned to the chin and her quality of attention that Tayak had relied on for six years.

Tayak looked at the team. Delia was ready. Osei was ready. Elspeth had already turned toward the north marker, the Total Station case over her shoulder, moving with the efficient, unhurried pace of someone who had a task and intended to complete it. Priya was still drawing her column.

Wren was looking at the stratigraphy chart.

She had been looking at it for the last four minutes with the expression Tayak had come to recognize over two months of working alongside her. Not the expression of someone reading, but the expression of someone checking what they had already read against what they were now seeing. Wren read things twice. She read them the first time for content and the second time for what the content implied. It was a quality that made her slow in casual conversation and precise in the field, and Tayak had stopped finding it frustrating somewhere around month two and had started finding it useful.

"Question," Wren said, without looking up.

"Go."

"The base layer." Wren set her finger on the chart at the lowest marked stratum, the unmarked depth below stratum six, the layer Tayak had labeled only with a question mark and a depth estimate in pencil. "If the burial is primary, if the pendant was the founding deposit and nothing was placed above it for a significant interval, then the surrounding matrix should be undisturbed sub-soil. No cultural inclusion, no charcoal scatter, no organics except what came in with the burial itself."

"That's what I expect."

"Then the identification problem isn't the burial. It's the interface." Wren looked up. "The transition zone between the mound fill and the original ground surface. That's where the pre-contact occupation evidence will be, if there's evidence of habitation predating the mound's construction. Surface scatter, fire-cracked rock, any micro-debitage that ended up in the fill when they started building. If that interface layer is intact, it's the oldest context on the site. But it's also the easiest to lose." She paused. "Are we planning to hand-excavate the full interface, or are we going to core sample and section?"

The question was not simple. It was the question, the one Tayak had been turning over since she had read the affidavit and understood what the base layer might contain. Full hand excavation of the interface would take time they did not have in surplus. Core sampling was faster but would give them a transect, not a surface, and if the commission's expert argued that a transect was insufficient to characterize the full extent of the occupation evidence, they would be right. Section and sample was the standard approach for a site under time pressure. Full hand excavation was what the evidence deserved.

Twenty-two days.

"We section the interface," Tayak said. "North-south and east-west. Full hand excavation of the quadrants that the sections cross. Soil samples from every quadrant at the interface horizon, whether or not they contain inclusions. If the matrix is consistent across all four quadrants and the sections show a clean, unbroken interface, we have a defensible characterization of the full surface."

Wren nodded slowly, still looking at the chart. "And if the sections show variation, if the interface is irregular?"

"Then we expand the hand excavation to cover the variation. We don't stop at the section lines if the evidence tells us not to."

"That could add three days."

"I know."

Wren was quiet for a moment. The woodpecker started again in the sycamores, three sharp strikes, a pause, two more, then stopped. The frost on the plywood had retreated to a narrow strip along the north edge of the table where the sun had not yet reached, and the stratigraphy chart lay across the bare wood with its penciled question mark at the bottom, the depth estimate in Tayak's handwriting, the word *undisturbed?* written in the margin in a different pen at a different time, from a morning when she had been less certain of everything.

"We'll need the peristaltic pump," Wren said. "If there's any groundwater intrusion at that depth, it's a creek terrace, the water table could be within two feet of the interface, we'll need to pump continuously while we excavate. Otherwise the section walls won't hold long enough to document."

This was not a question. It was a statement of preparation. Wren had already thought through the groundwater problem, had already identified the pump as the solution, and was now telling Tayak so that Tayak would know it had been thought through.

Tayak looked at her.

Wren was twenty-eight years old and had been on this site for two months and had made one serious mistake, the document transfer, the Calvert patent, the chain of events that had led in part to the designation letter and the twenty-two-day clock. She had apologized for it in the trench, in the rain, with the mud on her knees and her voice level and her face showing the specific quality of someone who was not asking to be forgiven but was making sure the other person knew they understood what they had done. Tayak had not yet decided what to do with that either. She had set it aside in the same place she had set the other unresolvable things, and she had kept working, and Wren had kept working beside her, and the work had been good.

"The pump is in the equipment shed," Tayak said. "It needs a new intake hose. There's a replacement coil in the blue bin on the left shelf."

"I'll fit it this morning," Wren said.

"After we open the base layer section. I want you with me for the first cut."

"Yes."

Wren looked at her then, not the way she looked at the chart, reading and checking, but directly, the way she had looked at her in the trench the night of the apology, when there had been nothing left to say that was useful and they had both known it and had stood in the rain anyway because neither of them had been ready to leave.

"We'll be ready," she said.

Tayak held that for a moment. The words were simple. They were the kind of words that could be said without meaning much, the kind of reassurance that moves through field teams the way weather moves through, present, impersonal, not addressed to anyone in particular. But Wren had said them looking at her, and Wren did not say things she did not mean, and Tayak had worked beside her long enough to know the difference between professional confidence and something else.

She believed her.

That was the problem. She believed her completely, and belief of that kind, the kind that settles into the body like a measurement confirmed, meant the thing was real now in a way it had not been real before. The twenty-two days were real. The base layer was real. The name in the affidavit, the name she had not yet spoken aloud, was real and waiting and she was going to be standing in front of it in a matter of days with her trowel and her notebook and her field bag worn to the shoulder, and she did not yet know what she would do when that happened.

She picked up the notebook and capped her pen.

"Eight-thirty," she said. "Elspeth has the datum. We start the center section at nine."

She walked toward the mound.

The frost on the southern face had gone entirely now, the brown grass dull and flat in the morning light. Somewhere below the frost line, below the stratum six fill and the antler figure and the shell gorget and the pipe bowl and the glass bead and the colonial document and all the layered keeping of two thousand years, the base layer waited. Undisturbed. Patient. Indifferent to clocks.

She was not indifferent to the clock. She had twenty-two days and a team she trusted and a question she had been carrying since her grandmother had said *I need to tell you something* and had not yet told her, and the only way forward was down.

She put her hand on the mound's southern face, the way she had on the first day, the way she did every morning. The grass was cold and damp under her palm. The earth below it did not move.

She started down the slope toward the trench.

The team had dispersed by nine-fifteen. She could hear them, Elspeth calling a reading from the north datum, Osei's shovel working the west baulk, Wren's voice low and precise somewhere in the equipment shed, the metallic click of the pump fittings being checked and rechecked. The sounds were clean and purposeful and they belonged to other people.

Tayak stood at the south edge of the trench.

The base layer was four feet below her boots. The section walls held it on three sides, brown-gray silt, dense and undisturbed, the kind of matrix that had not been touched since the last time a human hand had pressed something into it. The color was different from the strata above. Darker. Heavier with moisture. The smell coming up from it was the smell of deep cold ground, the smell of things that had been in the dark a very long time. Not rot, not decay, but the specific mineral stillness of earth that had not breathed.

She had been looking at it for three minutes and had not moved.

She reached into the inside pocket of her field jacket and took out the pages.

Nora had given them to her folded into thirds, the way you fold a letter when you intend it to be carried and not displayed. The paper had been folded so many times over so many years that the creases had gone soft and gray at the edges, the way cloth goes soft at the collar. Tayak had read it four times since Nora had pressed it into her hands at the kitchen table on Billingsley Road, and she had not yet unfolded it without her hands doing something involuntary, tightening, or going still, or both.

She unfolded it now.

The affidavit was six pages, typed on a manual typewriter on paper that had once been white and was now the color of old cream. The typing was uneven, some letters struck harder than others, the *e* slightly elevated, the period a small dent in the page. Ida had typed it. Nora had told her that. Ida, who had held the document for thirty-one years before Nora, who had sat down at a kitchen table in the summer of 1962 and typed what she knew so that the knowing would not die with her body.

Tayak turned to the last page.

She had read it enough times that she could have said it from memory. But memory was not the same as the page, and the page was not the same as the trench, and she needed all three of them in the same place at the same time.

*The woman at the beginning of the chain is described in the oral record as follows: she buried a pendant of worked copper in the earth at the high ground when her daughter died. The daughter was a child. The pendant had belonged to the daughter, who had held it and returned it, and the woman could not keep it in the house after the child was gone. She put it in the earth with her hands and she spoke the child's name over it. This was the first burial. The mound grew from this. I do not write the woman's name here because a name of this age is not for paper. I have held it for thirty-one years. The person I give this document to will hear the name from me when they are ready to go down to the bottom layer. Not before.*

Ida had signed it. Nora had witnessed it and added three lines in her own hand below the typed text, in blue ballpoint ink that had faded to the color of a winter sky.

*I received this from Ida in the summer of 1962. I have held it since. The name is the same name Ida gave me. I have not changed it or added to it. When the time comes I will say it to the person who needs to hear it. I will not say it on the phone.*

Tayak's hand tightened on the pages.

She had known about the pendant since stratum five. She had excavated it herself, the copper disc, green-gray with age, the surface worked in a pattern she had not been able to identify until Wren had pointed out that the marks were not decorative but representational, a heron in profile, the long neck bent, the beak angled toward water. She had logged it as artifact 18CH-447-051 and sent it to conservation and written the description in her field notebook in the precise, affectless language Carver had taught her: *copper alloy disc, diameter 4.3 cm, surface decoration present, condition moderate, context secure.* She had done all of that. She had done it correctly.

She had not written in the notebook that when she had lifted it from the matrix and held it in her palm, her hand had gone cold in a way that had nothing to do with the temperature of the soil.

A daughter who had died. A pendant the daughter had held and returned. A woman who could not keep it in the house.

The trench was four feet deep here at the south section. The base layer was below the stratum six fill, below the antler figure, below the shell gorget and the pipe bowl and the glass bead and the colonial document, below sixty years of Nora's keeping and sixty-two years of Ida's keeping before that and two thousand years of everyone else's keeping before that. The stratigraphy was clear. She had read it correctly. The sequence was correct.

The wind moved across the top of the trench and the pages shifted in her hand and she pressed them flat.

The description was enough. She knew it was enough. She had been an archaeologist for sixteen years and she understood what the evidence meant when it converged, the pendant, the stratum, the depth, the oral record, the affidavit, the name Nora was holding for her in a house on Billingsley Road eight miles from where she was standing. She understood what it meant when a chain of evidence ran in one direction and did not branch and did not contradict itself and arrived at a single point.

She understood what it meant.

She was not ready to say it.

Below her, the base layer waited in its cold and its dark and its patience, the way it had waited through every other thing, through the building of the mound above it and the layering of the strata and the colonial grants and the constables and the hearings and the designation letters and the thirty-day clocks and the bulldozers that were not yet here but were coming. It had waited through all of it. It would wait a few more days.

She folded the pages back into thirds along the soft gray creases and put them in her inside pocket and pressed her hand flat against her chest for a moment, over the pocket, over the paper, over the thing she was not yet saying.

From the equipment shed, she heard the pump fitting seat with a clean metallic click.

"Got it," Wren called, to no one in particular, to the shed, to the morning.

Tayak took her hand away from her chest.

She took out her field notebook, number eleven, the cover worn at the corner where she always held it, uncapped her pen, and wrote the date and the time and the weather and the depth of the base layer and the name of every person on the site. She wrote it all correctly. She wrote *base layer: undisturbed, confirmed* and she wrote the depth and she wrote the dimensions of the section and she wrote *water table: monitor continuously, pump on standby.*

She did not write the other thing.

She capped the pen and put the notebook in her bag and looked down into the trench one more time.

The soil at the base was very dark. Dense. Still. It held the cold the way old ground holds cold, not as absence of warmth but as presence of something that had been there longer than warmth and would be there after.

She had twenty-two days.

She turned and walked toward the equipment shed to help Wren with the pump.

The trowel was a Marshalltown, the same one she had carried since her second field year, the handle worn smooth and dark at the grip from sixteen years of palms. She climbed down into the trench at the south section using the aluminum ladder, three rungs, her boots finding the plywood stepping board at the base without looking. The board kept her weight off the floor of the cut. She had laid it herself that morning before the others arrived.

The trench was four feet deep here and the walls were clean. She had cut them clean herself over the past three days, working with the small flat brush and the wooden pick, reading the stratigraphy as she went, the glass bead layer, the colonial document layer, the pipe bowl layer, the gorget layers, the antler figure layer, each one distinct, each one logged and photographed and measured and drawn. Below the antler figure layer was the base. She was standing in front of it now.

The soil was dark. Not the dark of disturbed fill or decomposed organic matter but something older and denser, the kind of dark that takes centuries to compact. She had seen it before in other trenches, the undisturbed A-horizon beneath a mound's construction fill, the original ground surface, the ground that had been there before anyone put anything on top of it. She knew what it was. She had written it in the notebook already: *base layer, undisturbed, confirmed.*

She had not yet touched it.

She knelt on the plywood board. The cold came up through the wood and through the knees of her field trousers, the particular cold of deep soil in January, the kind that settles into the joint and stays. The walls of the trench were close on both sides, close enough that she could have pressed her palms flat against both at once, and the light came down from above in a pale, even column, no shadows at this hour, the sun still low and the sky overcast, a good light for reading stratigraphy, no glare, no angle.

She uncapped the trowel from her vest pocket. She had not done that yet. She had been holding the notebook and looking at the base layer for four minutes by her watch, which was longer than she usually looked, but she had not been ready four minutes ago and she was ready now.

Behind her and above, from the east transect, she heard Wren moving. The sound of a tape measure pulling out, the small metal click of the lock. Then Wren's voice, not to her, to herself or to the east wall: "East face, upper contact, clean." She was logging the stratum six boundary on her side. She was doing her own work. She was not watching.

Except that she was. Tayak could feel it without looking, the quality of attention from the east transect that was different from the quality of attention of someone doing their own work. Wren had been on this site for two months. She knew what the base layer meant. She was not going to say anything. She was going to keep measuring and logging and she was going to feel Tayak's first cut the way you feel a door open in the next room, through the air, through the wood, through the fact of it.

From the north, Delia's voice: "South section, base contact. I'm reading two-thirty-one point four from the datum."

Tayak did not look up. "Log it."

"Logged."

The measurement was consistent with what she had been tracking. The base layer sat at two hundred and thirty-one centimeters below the datum peg at the mound's crown, which put it at approximately the original ground surface elevation of the river terrace, which was consistent with the geomorphology Priya had modeled in November, which was consistent with everything. The stratigraphy was consistent. The sequence was consistent. The evidence ran in one direction.

She set the trowel edge against the soil.

She did not press yet. She held it there, the steel just touching the surface, and she looked at the point of contact the way Marant had taught her to look. Not at the trowel, not at the soil, but at the line between them, the precise boundary where the instrument met the thing it was about to enter. *Listen with your hands.* She had been twenty-four years old in that trench on the Patuxent lowlands, and she had not understood what Marant meant until the third day, when her hands had stopped trying to find something and had simply begun to feel what was there.

She pressed.

The trowel went in a centimeter, perhaps less. The soil was dense and offered resistance, not the resistance of rock or root but the resistance of undisturbed matrix, tightly packed, the particles close together, the way soil gets when nothing has moved through it for a very long time. She drew the blade horizontally, a single stroke, four centimeters, and the soil curled up in a thin, even peel and fell away to the left onto the collection tray she had set at the base of the south wall.

Dark. Very dark. The cut surface was darker than the face, the way old wood is darker at the heart than at the skin.

She leaned in and looked at the cut surface with the hand lens she wore on a cord around her neck, the ten-power loupe she had bought in her first year and replaced twice and still used because the optics were right, the weight was right, the cord was the right length. The matrix was homogeneous at this scale, no visible inclusions, no charcoal flecks, no bone fragments, no cultural material in this first centimeter. Which was correct. The cultural material, if it was there, would be deeper. The pendant had been at two hundred and twelve centimeters. The base layer began at two hundred and thirty-one. There were nineteen centimeters between them, and the base layer was not a single deposit. It was a sequence, and she had not yet read the sequence.

She withdrew the trowel. She took out the notebook, number eleven, the corner worn, and wrote the time, which was 9:47, and the depth of the first cut, which was one centimeter, and the character of the matrix, which she wrote as: *dense, homogeneous, no visible inclusions, no cultural material at surface, cut surface dark, consistent with undisturbed pre-construction A-horizon.* She wrote it in the precise, affectless language Carver had taught her. She wrote it correctly.

She did not write: *I am standing at the bottom of my family's ground.*

She did not write it because she was not certain. She was close to certain, close enough that her hand had gone still on the pen for a moment before she wrote the description, but she was not certain, and she did not write things in the field notebook that she was not certain of. The notebook was a factual record, not a place for feelings. Carver had said that. Marant had said it differently: *the notebook is for what you found, not for what you were hoping to find.* She had understood both of them. She still understood both of them.

She capped the pen. She set the trowel edge back against the cut surface.

The second stroke was parallel to the first, four centimeters to the right. The soil peeled the same way, dense, even, dark. She deposited it on the tray. She leaned in with the loupe. Nothing yet.

From above, Delia's voice again, reading a second measurement from the north profile. The numbers were clean. The site was running the way a well-run site runs, the measurements coming in, the logs being kept, the work moving in its slow, deliberate order, the way it had moved for eleven months, the way it had moved through everything the state had done and everything Voss had done and everything the designation letter had done, because the work did not stop for any of that, the work kept its own time, the work moved at the pace the ground set and not at the pace of thirty-day clocks or institutional hearings or bulldozer schedules.

Twenty-two days.

She made the third stroke.

The trowel met something.

Not resistance. The soil's resistance was uniform and she had already learned its feel. This was different. The blade touched something at approximately two centimeters below the cut surface, something that did not give the way compacted soil gives, something that held. She stopped. She withdrew the trowel. She set it down on the collection tray and picked up the wooden pick instead.

She worked slowly. She always worked slowly when something held, because the thing that held was the thing that mattered, and the thing that mattered could not be undone once it was broken. She moved a centimeter of soil from around the resistance point, then another, moving from the outside in, letting the object define itself rather than defining it from above. The wooden pick left no marks on anything softer than stone. It was the right tool.

A curve appeared. Dark reddish-brown, the color of old bark or old leather or old bone.

She stopped.

She picked up the brush, the soft-bristled one, the one she used for surfaces that might be organic, and swept the loose soil away from the curve in short, careful strokes. The curve extended. It was not stone. It was not ceramic. It was not metal. The texture was fibrous, the surface slightly irregular, the color consistent across the exposed area.

Bark.

She sat back on her heels and looked at it. Bark wrapping. Burial wrapping. She had read the description in Nora's affidavit: *the unnamed man at the second depth, wrapped in bark.* But this was not the second depth. This was the base layer, below the second depth, below everything. This was the depth she had not yet read.

She took out the notebook. She wrote the time, which was 9:53, and the depth, which was two centimeters below the cut surface, and the description: *organic material, possible bark wrapping, color consistent with tulip poplar or similar, texture fibrous, surface irregular, condition TBD, context secure.* She wrote it correctly. She wrote it in the affectless language.

Her hand was steady.

She looked up for the first time since she had climbed down the ladder. Above the trench wall, the sky was the same pale overcast it had been all morning, the light even and cold and clear. From the east transect she could hear Wren's tape measure, the small sounds of careful work. She could feel the quality of Wren's attention from here, the same quality she had felt before, the awareness of what was happening in the south section, the held breath of it.

She did not call out to Wren. She did not call out to anyone.

She looked back down at the curve of bark in the dark soil, the small exposed arc of it, the way it held its shape after two thousand years the way things hold their shape when they are kept carefully, when the ground is cold and dense and does not let the air in, when the people above know what is below them and do not disturb it and pass that knowledge forward, name by name by name, until the chain reaches a woman kneeling in a trench in Charles County, Maryland, in the twenty-second year of a century that was running out of time.

She picked up the brush again.

She did not look up.

The work had begun.
