The trailer smelled of old coffee and sun-heated aluminum, and Wren had been sitting in it for forty minutes before she admitted she wasn't going home.

The official log lay closed on the folding table to her left, spiral-bound, countersigned, every entry written in the careful neutral language Tayak had drilled into her first semester. *Recovered: one glass bead, cobalt blue, approximately 4mm diameter. Context: Stratum One, Unit 4, 23cm below datum. Condition: intact.* She had written it that afternoon in her best field hand, pressed hard enough to leave an impression on the page beneath.

Her personal notebook was smaller, cloth-covered in a dark green that had faded to the color of old moss. She had bought six of them at once from a shop in Baltimore and kept them in a locked box under her bed at the rental house, the kind of precaution that had seemed paranoid in her first year of the program and now seemed barely sufficient.

She set it open on the table and uncapped her drawing pen.

The bead lived in its evidence bag three feet away, tagged and sealed, but she didn't need to look at it. She had looked at it for twenty minutes through the hand lens before Tayak came back to the unit, and the image had burned itself somewhere behind her eyes the way certain things did. The way her mother's face looked when Wren told her she was staying in the program. The way the first stratum break had appeared in the soil wall, that clean dark line between centuries.

She began to draw.

The bead's profile first, then the perforation, slightly off-center, the drill having wandered left before correcting, the kind of small imperfection that meant a human hand and not a machine. She labeled the iridescent surface weathering along the upper curve, the place where the glass had gone milky and faintly rainbow-sheened from centuries underground, the condition the conservation literature called *glass corrosion* in a phrase that always made her think of something alive and suffering. She noted the color: cobalt, but not uniform, deeper at the core where the glass was thickest, paling toward the edges in a way the official log's single word *blue* did not begin to capture.

In the margin, in smaller script, she wrote: *Document everything twice means once for the record and once for the truth.*

She looked at the sentence. Left it.

The trailer's single window showed the site in the last of the evening light, the staked grid of the excavation, the tarps weighted with sandbags at the corners, the mound's low profile rising behind it all like a held breath. The sweetgum trees at the edge of the clearing had gone dark against a sky the color of a bruise healing. Somewhere in the treeline, something with a voice like a rusted hinge was calling and calling without answer.

She turned back to the drawing and added a cross-section view, then a detail of the perforation's interior wall. She was stalling and she knew it. The drawing was done. The drawing had been done ten minutes ago.

She wrote: *Question: is the bead Saya's? (Tayak's framework, oral tradition account not yet shared with me in full. Listen for.)*

Then she wrote: *What did I miss on day one?*

This was the fear she had been carrying since Tayak said *document everything twice*. Not the instruction itself, which was reasonable, but the implication behind it. That the first pass was already compromised. That Wren had been present at the moment of discovery and had still somehow failed to see something that mattered.

She thought about the soil around the bead. The color had shifted two centimeters above the find level. She had noted it in the official log as *possible disturbance, organic staining* and moved on because Tayak had been watching and the light was going and there had been a pressure to finish the unit before dark. She wrote it again now, slower: *soil color shift 2cm above find level. Not disturbance. Deliberate deposit?*

She stared at that.

Then she pulled her laptop across the table and opened the Maryland State Archives portal.

She had been here before, for her thesis research, chasing eighteenth-century plantation records through scanned folios of secretary hand so faded the letters looked like they were trying to disappear. The interface was the same, utilitarian, slightly broken, the kind of website that had been designed in 2003 and updated just enough to keep running. She navigated to the Charles County land records, patent books, 1680 to 1700.

The first result was a scan of a page so brown it looked like it had been brewed. She zoomed in.

The handwriting was the compressed, looping cursive of a county clerk who had written the same legal formulas so many times his hand moved without him, *to have and to hold the said land and premises unto him the said*, and Wren had to slow down and read each word separately, the way you read a foreign language when you are not quite fluent, the long *s* shapes that looked like *f* until you stopped seeing them as letters and started seeing them as a code.

She found the first mention of the Piscataway on the third folio. A boundary description for a tract called *Friendship*, granted to one Thomas Calvert. She stopped. Looked again. *Calvert.* Her own name. She knew it was a common name in Maryland, knew it meant nothing, but her hand went still on the trackpad for a moment anyway.

The description read: *beginning at a bounded white oak ſtanding near the head of a ſmall branch, being the eaſtern bounds of a Piſcattaway ſettlement, thence running Weſt by South forty perches to a bounded pine.*

She read it twice.

*The eastern bounds of a Piscattaway settlement.*

She opened a new page in the notebook and wrote the phrase out by hand, slowly, as if the act of copying it would tell her something the screen didn't. Then she wrote the tract name: *Friendship.* Then the grantee: *Thomas Calvert.* Then the date: *1687.*

She sat back.

The settlement the document described as a boundary marker, the thing being bounded *against*, would have sat to the west of this oak tree, on the rise above the creek branch. She didn't have the GPS coordinates yet. She didn't have the survey translation. But she knew this county's topography from three months of walking it, and she knew where the rises were above the creek branches, and she knew where they were standing when Tayak's trowel found the bead.

She wrote: *Is this the same ground?*

Then, underneath: *I don't know enough to know if I'm right.*

The official log would never contain this page. The official log contained measurements and conditions and context codes. It did not contain the feeling of sitting in a trailer at nine in the evening with a folio of 1687 land records on your screen and a drawing of a glass bead in your notebook and the slow, vertiginous sense that the distance between those two things was smaller than it should be.

She capped the pen. She looked at the drawing of the bead, the off-center perforation, the milky iridescence, the depth of color at the core, and thought about the hand that had drilled it, the hand that had worn it, the hand that had put it in the ground.

She didn't know whose hand. She didn't know whose family.

She closed the notebook and sat in the aluminum-smelling dark, and the thing in the treeline kept calling.

The Maryland State Archives search interface had a field labeled *Keywords* that accepted free text, and another labeled *County* with a dropdown, and a date range with two boxes. Wren had used all three before. She knew its quirks: the way it timed out after twenty minutes of inactivity, the way scanned folios sometimes loaded sideways, the way the OCR layer on the older documents was essentially decorative, the machine having tried to read the secretary hand and produced strings of nonsense that bore no relationship to the actual words on the page.

She typed: *Charles County. 1685 to 1700. Piscataway.*

The interface thought about it. A spinning circle. Then: *3 results.*

Three. She had expected more, or none. She opened the first.

It was a deed dispute from 1698, two colonists arguing about a boundary line, and the Piscataway appeared only once, in a phrase so offhand it was almost insulting: *the land formerly used by the Pascattaway Indians, now being in the possession of the said Thomas Leigh.* She read it twice and wrote nothing. The word *formerly* sat in the sentence like a closed door.

The second result was a court record, a complaint about trespass, and the Piscataway weren't mentioned in the body of the document at all. The OCR had picked up a marginal notation in a different hand, nearly illegible, that might have said *Piscattaway* or might have said something else entirely. She zoomed in until the pixels went soft. The long *s* in the notation looked like an *f*, and the *tt* looked like *ll*, and she couldn't be certain. She wrote: *Possible ref, marginal note, 1696 trespass complaint. Unverifiable from scan. Flag for in-person review.*

The third result was a land grant.

The document was in better condition than most. The ink still dark in the center of each stroke, fading only at the hairlines where the quill had lifted. The folio had been stored flat, or stored lucky. She could read it without zooming, which meant she read it faster than she meant to, and had to go back.

The grant was dated *the fourteenth day of March, Anno Domini 1691.* The grantee was a man named William Sorrell. The tract was named, she read it twice to be sure, *Mount Friendship.*

*Mount Friendship.* She wrote it down. Then she looked back at the previous page of her notebook, at the entry she had made twenty minutes ago: *Friendship. Thomas Calvert. 1687.*

Two tracts. Four years apart. Adjacent names.

She went back to the document.

The survey description began in the standard form, *beginning at a bounded white oak ſtanding at the head of a ſmall branch of the Weſt branch of Port Tobacco Creek*, and she felt the ground shift slightly under her, the way it had earlier with the first document, because she knew Port Tobacco Creek, had walked its upper branches three times in the past month, knew the way the land rose on its western side toward the knoll where the mound sat. She kept reading.

*thence running South forty and two perches to a bounded cheſtnut, thence Weſt by South thirty perches, thence North thirty and eight perches, thence Eaſt to the firſt bounded tree, the whole containing and now laid out for one hundred and twenty acres more or leſs, bounded on the North by the Piſcattaway ſettlement at the high ground, being a tract of land, *

She stopped.

She read it again.

*bounded on the North by the Piſcattaway ſettlement at the high ground.*

Her finger went to the screen and she traced the phrase without touching the glass, the way you trace a word in a language you are still learning. The *s* shapes that looked like *f*. *Piſcattaway.* The settlement at the high ground.

The high ground.

She knew one high ground above the West branch of Port Tobacco Creek. She had stood on it. She had watched Tayak's trowel go into it. She had measured the depth of a glass bead in it and drawn the perforation in the bead's wall and written the depth in two separate logs because Tayak had told her to document everything twice.

Her hands were not shaking. She noticed this with a kind of clinical detachment, they were perfectly still on the keyboard, and then she noticed that this was because she had pressed both palms flat against the table without realizing it.

She took a screenshot. Then she took another one, cropped tighter on the survey description. Then she opened the Maryland State Archives' image download function, the one that gave you a full-resolution TIFF instead of the compressed JPEG the screenshot would produce, and she requested the download and watched the progress bar move across the screen.

While it moved, she opened a new browser tab and pulled up the county GIS portal. She had bookmarked it in September, the first week of the dig, when she was trying to understand the parcel boundaries. She typed in the GPS coordinates of the mound's center point, the numbers she had memorized the way you memorize a phone number you dial often, and the map loaded, satellite view, the knoll visible as a slightly darker oval in the tree cover.

She switched to the historical overlay. The county had digitized a set of 1930s survey plats, and those plats had been georeferenced against modern coordinates, imprecisely but usably. She zoomed to the knoll.

The 1930s plat showed a parcel boundary running east-west along the northern edge of the knoll. The parcel to the south was labeled *Sorrell's Addition, 1712.* She stared at that. *Sorrell.* The grantee of the 1691 document was William Sorrell. The 1712 parcel was almost certainly an extension of the same family's holdings, the same land, resurveyed and renamed, the way Maryland land titles tended to accumulate names the way a boat accumulated barnacles.

If the 1712 parcel's southern boundary was the northern edge of the knoll, and if the 1691 grant described the Piscataway settlement as lying to the north of the tract, then the settlement described in the grant was not to the north of the knoll. It *was* the knoll.

She stopped herself. She was moving too fast. She was connecting dots that might not connect.

She opened her private notebook to a fresh page and wrote the date and time at the top, then wrote:

*1691 grant, William Sorrell, "Mount Friendship," 120 acres, survey description places northern boundary adjacent to "Piſcattaway ſettlement at the high ground."*

*GIS overlay: Sorrell's Addition (1712) southern boundary aligns with northern edge of knoll, same knoll as mound site.*

*If the settlement described in 1691 is the same high ground as the mound site, this is not a general reference to Piscataway territory. This is a specific, surveyed coordinate.*

She paused. Then she wrote:

*This is not incidental. This is the site.*

She looked at the sentence. It was the most definitive thing she had written in three months of field notes, and she had written it in a private notebook that no one would read unless she chose to show it, and she was not yet sure she was right, and she was not yet sure what being right would mean.

The TIFF finished downloading. She opened it and zoomed to the survey description again, the full-resolution image sharp enough that she could see the individual quill strokes, the slight tremor in the clerk's hand where he had re-dipped the pen and come back with too much ink, the brown blot that had spread under the word *ſettlement* like a shadow.

She looked at the blot for a moment. Three hundred and thirty-three years ago, a man had pressed a quill to paper and described a Piscataway settlement as a boundary marker, not as a place, not as a community, but as a feature of the landscape, the same way he had described the white oak and the chestnut and the creek branch. The settlement had been useful to him only insofar as it told him where his client's land stopped.

And in doing so, he had put it in the record.

She wrote: *The document does not name anyone. Does not name the settlement. Does not describe it beyond "high ground." But it places it. That may be enough.*

Then, below that, smaller: *Whose family? Whose ground?*

She didn't know. She had a land grant and a GPS coordinate and a glass bead and a soil color shift two centimeters above the find level, and she had the word *Piscattaway* written in iron-gall ink by a county clerk who was long dead, and she did not yet know whose family had lived on that high ground, or what had happened to them, or whether the woman who had worn the bead and the people who had buried it here were the same people whose settlement this man had used as a surveyor's landmark the way he had used a tree.

She looked at the drawing of the bead again, the off-center perforation, the iridescence at the surface, the deep blue at the core that the drawing could only suggest.

She wrote: *Ask Tayak what she knows about the 1691 period on this site. She said document everything twice. She knew there was something to find.*

She crossed out *Ask Tayak* and wrote *Tell Tayak.* Then she crossed that out too and wrote simply: *Show her.*

Outside, something moved in the brush at the edge of the site, a deer probably, or the wind in the sweetgum saplings, and the motion-sensor light on the equipment trailer clicked on and flooded the clearing with white, and Wren sat for a moment in the sudden brightness coming through the window, the document still open on her screen, the notebook open on the table, and thought about the word *bounded.*

In the 1691 survey, *bounded* meant defined. Contained. Marked by a tree someone had notched to say: here is where one thing ends and another begins.

The settlement had been a boundary. Now it was a question.

And somewhere in the tree cover beyond the light's reach, the knoll held whatever it held, and it had been holding it for a very long time, and it had not finished giving it up.

The TIFF was still open on her screen when she noticed the second document.

She had been about to close the Maryland Archives tab, she had what she came for, or thought she did, when the sidebar showed a linked folio. The archive's interface flagged it as a related record: *See also: Petition filed 14 March 1691/2, Charles County Court, ref. Land Office vol. 7, fol. 88r.*

She clicked it.

The scan loaded slowly, the progress bar crawling. While she waited, she poured the last of the cold coffee into her cup, drank it without tasting it, and set the cup down on top of the site map. The trailer smelled of damp canvas and the faint mineral sharpness of the soil samples lined up on the shelf above the door, twelve small jars, each labeled in her own hand, each holding a tablespoon of Charles County earth from a different depth.

The scan opened.

This one was worse than the grant document. The 1691 grant had been clear enough once she adjusted the contrast. This folio had been wet at some point, the lower third of the page warped and brown-stained in a spreading tide that had eaten the ink in patches, leaving pale voids where words had been. The upper portion was legible, the same secretary hand, the same long-s formations, the same iron-gall brown going faint at the line ends where the clerk had stretched his ink too far before re-dipping.

She read the heading first.

*A Petition presented to the Court of Charles County in the Province of Maryland, this fourteenth day of March in the year of our Lord 1691, concerning the Tract of land known as Mount Friendſhip, granted to William Sorrell, *

She sat up straighter.

The same tract. The same year, almost. The grant had been issued earlier in 1691, and this petition had been filed the following March, designated 1691/2 in the old-style double-dating the colony used before the calendar reform. Someone had moved fast. Someone had known about the grant and gone to the county court within months of its recording.

She zoomed in on the body of the petition. The handwriting here was different from the grant document, less practiced, the letters slightly uneven, as if the clerk had been transcribing from speech or from a document written by someone unfamiliar with legal forms. Or perhaps the petition itself had been written by the petitioner. She couldn't tell.

The first full paragraph was legible:

*Whereas the ſaid Tract of land called Mount Friendſhip hath been granted and ſurveyed upon ground which hath been in the poſſeſſion and uſe of the Piſcattaway people for time beyond memory, and whereas the northern boundary of the ſaid Tract doth abut upon and encroach upon the high ground where the ſaid people have maintained their ſettlement and their dead, *

Wren stopped.

*Where the said people have maintained their settlement and their dead.*

She read it again. Then she wrote it out in full in her private notebook, copying the spelling exactly, including the long-s forms that she rendered as *ſ* because she wanted the record to be precise. She underlined *their dead.*

This was not a boundary dispute about fields or timber rights. This was a petition about a burial site.

She went back to the scan and kept reading. The next paragraph was where the water damage began. The brown tide crept in from the left margin and took out a full line, then retreated, then came back worse. She could make out fragments: *...the ſaid ground being ſacred to our people and not to be diſturbed...* and then a gap, and then *...we do therefore petition this Court to conſider...*

And then the damage took the rest of the middle section entirely.

She zoomed to maximum resolution and leaned toward the screen until her nose was eight inches from it. The brown stain had dried in a wavy line, and at the edge of the stain, where the ink had been wet when the water hit, the words had run and blurred into dark smears. She could see letter shapes under the staining, ghost-forms, the ink not entirely destroyed but mixed with the brown of the water damage so that text and stain were the same color. She tried adjusting the contrast, pushing it to the edge of legibility, and a few more letters emerged from the murk like objects surfacing in cloudy water.

She could make out what looked like a name.

It was near the bottom of the legible section, just above where the damage became total. The line read, she squinted, tilted her head, zoomed again: *...on behalf of the ſaid community, I, Sioſ, *

And then the stain.

She stared at it. *Sios-.* Four letters and a dash made of water. The name continued into the brown tide and did not come back out.

She tried every contrast setting the archive's interface offered. She tried inverting the image, which sometimes helped with faded ink. The ghost-forms went white on black and were, if anything, harder to read. She tried downloading the TIFF and opening it in the image editor on her laptop, pushing the curves until the image was almost entirely blown out, looking for the shape of letters in the overexposed noise.

There was something there. A curve that might be an *a.* Or an *o.* She couldn't be sure.

She wrote in her notebook: *Petition against Sorrell grant, 14 March 1691/2, filed by a named Piscataway individual, name partially legible: "Sioſ, ," rest water-damaged, unrecoverable from this scan.*

She looked at what she had written. *Sios-.* She said it aloud quietly, testing it. The trailer was silent except for the hum of her laptop fan and the distant sound of something moving in the trees, the same deer maybe, still out there in the sweetgum dark.

She opened a new browser tab and searched: *Piscataway colonial-era names 1690s.* Then: *Piscataway personal names 17th century Maryland.* Then: *Conoy names colonial records.*

The results gave her general histories, a digitized copy of a 1699 treaty, two academic articles about Piscataway political structure in the contact period, and a state heritage website with a photograph of a reconstructed longhouse that looked nothing like the site outside her window.

Nothing that matched the name fragment.

She tried the Maryland Archives' own index, searching the name field for *Sios* with a wildcard. Three results came back, all of them from the nineteenth century, a German immigrant family named Siossberg, unrelated.

She tried the secondary literature. She had three monographs on her laptop, Rountree and Davidson's *Eastern Shore Indians,* a photocopied chapter from a 1970s survey of Charles County archaeology, and a more recent article on Piscataway survivance in the colonial period that Tayak had sent her in the first week of the project with a note that said *background, not gospel.* She searched all three for any name beginning with *Sios.* Nothing.

She sat back.

The name was there, partially, stubbornly, damaged past full recovery, and it was attached to a petition filed against the grant that described the mound site as a Piscataway settlement. Someone named *Sios-something* had gone to the Charles County Court and put their name on a legal document arguing that this ground was sacred and that it held their dead.

She wrote: *Name not found in secondary literature. Not in treaty records, not in county indices, not in Rountree/Davidson. Either obscure, or recorded only in documents not yet digitized, or the name as written is a clerk's phonetic approximation of something different.*

She paused, then added: *Or the scan quality is too poor and I'm misreading the first four letters entirely.*

She looked at the image again. No. She was reading it right. *Sioſ-.* The long-s was unmistakable, the *i* clear, the *o* slightly compressed but present.

She wrote, below everything else: *Need to ask Tayak if this name means anything to the community. She may know a variant. She may know a family.*

She drew a small box around that sentence, the way she marked things in her notes that needed to become actions and not just observations.

Outside, the motion-sensor light clicked off. The clearing went dark again, and the window became a black mirror, and Wren could see herself reflected in it: bent over the notebook, the laptop's glow catching the side of her face, the drawing of the bead visible on the open page beside the document scan.

She looked at the reflection for a moment, then looked back at the screen.

The petition was incomplete. The name was incomplete. The story the document was trying to tell had been interrupted by water, by time, by damage, by the ordinary entropy that took things apart, and what remained was a fragment: four letters, a dash, a voice that had gone to a colonial court and said *this ground is ours and our dead are here* and had not been heard, or had been heard and overruled, because the tract had been granted and the grant had stood and the land had passed to Sorrell and then to whoever came after Sorrell, and the settlement on the high ground had become a boundary marker and then a footnote and then nothing at all in the written record.

Except for this. This one damaged folio. This one name that the water had almost taken.

Wren wrote the date and time at the bottom of the page, as she always did when she closed a session of notes. Then she wrote, in the margin beside the boxed sentence: *This person fought for this ground. Three hundred and thirty years ago. Someone should know her name.*

She closed the notebook. She did not close the laptop. She left the scan open, the brown-stained folio, the ghost-text at the edge of the damage, the four letters that were all that remained of whoever had stood in the Charles County Court in the spring of 1692 and tried to hold a line.

She would show it to Tayak in the morning. She didn't know what Tayak would make of it. She didn't know if the name would mean anything, or if it was just another fragment that the archive had preserved without explanation, the way the ground preserved objects without explanation, holding them, keeping them, waiting for someone to ask the right question.

She turned off the lamp. The laptop screen was the only light left in the trailer, and it cast the scan in pale blue, the brown stain going gray in the screen's cold glow, and *Sioſ, * sat at the edge of the damage like something just about to surface.

She had meant to close the notebook.

Instead she opened it again, turned back three pages, and looked at the bead sketch.

She had drawn it that afternoon, just after Tayak had re-bagged the find and handed it to her for logging. The sketch took up a quarter of the page: a small circle, slightly irregular on the left side where the glass had a faint flat spot, with a drilled hole she had rendered as a tight oval rather than a perfect circle because the hole was not centered. It ran about a millimeter off-axis, toward the upper edge. She had noted the color in the margin: *deep cobalt, opaque, no visible inclusions from naked eye. Possible surface iridescence on lower curve, check under loupe.* Below that: *diameter approx. 4mm. Weight not yet recorded.*

Small. Deliberate. Someone had made this with care and someone else had worn it.

She picked up her phone and opened the PDF she had downloaded three weeks ago when Tayak first described the stratum, a trade-bead typology compiled by a researcher at the Smithsonian, one hundred and twelve pages of photographs and cross-sections and provenance tables organized by manufacturing technique and probable origin. She had skimmed it then. Now she read it.

She found cobalt drawn beads in the Dutch section, pages forty-one through forty-seven. The manufacturing process left specific signatures: a long bubble of glass pulled over a wire mandrel, then cut into lengths, the cut ends sometimes fire-polished smooth and sometimes left with a slight sharpness at the rim. The off-center drill hole was not a drill hole at all. It was the wire channel, and it ran off-axis when the mandrel had been slightly crooked during the draw. She looked at her sketch again. The oval she had drawn, tilted toward the upper edge.

She read the provenance table. Dutch manufacture, probable origin Murano or Amsterdam workshops, distributed through West India Company trading networks from the 1620s onward, with peak distribution in the mid-Atlantic corridor between roughly 1650 and 1710. The Lenape had been the primary intermediary traders in the Chesapeake region, carrying Dutch goods south and east along the river networks, exchanging them for furs, food, and relationships.

She set the phone down and looked at the sketch.

The bead had not come from England. The colonists who had taken this land, who had written the grant that named the mound site a Piscataway settlement and then granted it anyway, had been trading in English goods, English pipes, English ceramics. This bead was Dutch. It had moved through Lenape hands before it reached this ground.

She wrote: *Cobalt drawn bead. Probable Dutch manufacture, Amsterdam or Murano workshop. Distributed via WIC trading networks, peak mid-Atlantic corridor 1650 to 1710. Lenape intermediary trade strongly suggested. Not English colonial deposit. Indigenous receipt and retention.*

She underlined *Indigenous receipt and retention.*

Then she looked at the stratigraphy sketch she had made of the trench wall, the clean horizontal bands, the slight rise of the mound beneath them, the position of the bead marked with a small X at the upper edge of the cultural layer. The bead had not been in the disturbed topsoil. It had been in the layer, at a depth consistent with intentional placement rather than accidental loss. It had been found on the high ground, above the floodplain, at the same coordinates the land grant described as a Piscataway settlement.

She wrote: *The bead is not lost. It was placed.*

She underlined it. Then she underlined it again, pressing hard enough that the second line had a slight ridge she could feel with her fingertip.

She sat with that for a moment.

A bead placed deliberately on high ground at a documented Piscataway settlement site, received through Lenape trade networks, in a stratum that dated by context and association to the late seventeenth century. The same period as the land grant. The same period as the petition. The same period as *Sioſ, ,* whoever she was, standing in the Charles County Court and saying *this ground is ours.*

The bead was a material fact. The grant was a documentary fact. The petition was a documentary fact. Taken separately, each was interesting. Together, they were something else. They were a pattern, and patterns in archaeology did not emerge by accident. Patterns meant people. Patterns meant intention.

She turned to a fresh page and wrote at the top: *Convergence, Stratum One.*

Below that, in the spare shorthand she used when she needed to think faster than she could write in full sentences:

*Bead: Dutch manufacture. Lenape trade network. Deliberate deposit. High ground.*
*Grant: names site as Piscataway settlement. 1691.*
*Petition: filed 1691/2. Name partially damaged, Sioſ, . Argues ground is sacred, holds the dead.*
*Bead placement consistent with votive or mortuary deposit.*

She stopped at *mortuary deposit* and looked at it.

If the bead had been placed with a burial, if the high ground held human remains, which the petition explicitly claimed, then this was not just an archaeological site with Indigenous association. This was a burial ground. And a burial ground changed everything about the legal situation, about NAGPRA, about the state's ability to move against the permit, about the timeline Tayak was working against.

She did not know yet if there were remains. The stratigraphy had not gone deep enough. But the bead's position, the petition's language, the location, all of it pointed in the same direction, and she had learned from Tayak that pointing in the same direction was how evidence worked. You did not wait for certainty. You followed the convergence.

She wrote: *If mortuary context confirmed, NAGPRA protections apply regardless of state permit status. Tayak needs this connection documented before Voss or the state can reframe the site as non-funerary.*

Her hand had tightened on the pen without her noticing. She made herself loosen it.

The state was already moving. She had heard enough of Tayak's end of the phone call that afternoon, the clipped answers, the careful language, the way Tayak had set down the phone and stood very still for a moment before she said anything, to know that the pressure was not abstract. Someone with authority was watching this excavation and looking for a reason to interrupt it, and the reason they would use was not malice. It would be procedure: insufficient documentation, uncertain provenance, the site's significance not yet established to the required standard.

Every hour she did not connect the bead to the grant to the petition to the possible mortuary context was an hour the state could use to argue the site was not yet proven significant.

She looked at the sketch again. The small imperfect circle, the off-axis hole, the note about iridescence she had not yet checked.

She picked up the loupe from the edge of the table, a ten-power jeweler's loupe she had bought secondhand at an estate sale three years ago, its brass housing gone green at the seams, and crossed to the specimen tray where the re-bagged bead sat in its numbered zip-lock. She did not open the bag. She held the loupe to the plastic and leaned close.

The iridescence was there. A thin film of weathering on the lower curve, the glass surface gone slightly milky and then, at certain angles, faintly rainbow, the way old glass went when it had been underground long enough for the surface layers to delaminate, a process that took decades at minimum and more often centuries. The conservation literature called it glass corrosion, and the bead had been in the ground long enough to earn the name.

She went back to the typology. The weathering pattern was consistent with extended burial in slightly acidic soil, the sandy loam of a well-drained knoll, for instance. The kind of soil you found on a rise above a floodplain.

She wrote: *Surface iridescence confirmed under loupe. Glass corrosion consistent with extended burial, likely 200 years minimum, possibly significantly longer. Soil type at site supports this timeline.*

She looked at the time. Past midnight. The lamp made a warm yellow circle on the notebook, and outside the trailer the night was loud with frogs, a wet, layered sound that had been going since dusk, the kind of sound that made the darkness feel full rather than empty.

She turned back to the convergence page and read what she had written.

The bead was Dutch. It had come through Lenape hands. It had been placed deliberately on high ground that a 1691 land grant named as a Piscataway settlement. Someone named *Sioſ, * had filed a petition months later arguing that the ground was sacred and held the dead. The bead showed weathering consistent with centuries of burial. The stratigraphy placed it in an intact cultural layer.

She wrote, at the bottom of the page, in the careful block letters she used when she was writing something she intended to be read: *This is not a random deposit at an incidental location. This is a deliberate act at a known place. Someone brought this bead to this specific high ground and left it with intention. The question is not whether this site has significance. The question is whether we can document that significance faster than the state can argue we haven't.*

She drew a line under it.

Then she wrote, below the line: *Show Tayak the convergence page first. Then the petition. Then ask about the name.*

She had been treating the name as a separate problem, something to research in the secondary literature, something to cross-reference against treaty records and county indices. But the name was not separate. The name was the person who had placed the bead, or who had known the person who placed the bead, or who had stood at this mound and understood what it was. The name was the thread that connected the object in the ground to the voice in the document, and she had been looking for it in the wrong places.

Tayak would know. Or Tayak would know someone who would know. The community carried things that the archive did not.

She wrote that down too, because she had learned not to trust herself to remember the important things, only the procedural ones: *The archive has limits. The community may not.*

She closed the notebook.

Outside, something moved in the brush at the edge of the clearing, a deer probably, or a raccoon working the compost bin she kept meaning to secure. The frogs did not stop. The motion-sensor light did not trip. Whatever it was, it was small and it passed.

She looked at the bead in its bag on the specimen tray. In the lamplight it was the color of deep water, the kind of blue that did not occur in nature in this part of Maryland, the kind of blue that had been made somewhere else and carried a long way by people who understood its value. Someone had worn this. Someone had held it in their hand, as she was holding it now through the thin plastic of the evidence bag, and had decided that this ground was worth marking.

She set it back in the tray.

The question that kept her from sleep was not whether she was right. She was reasonably confident she was right, or right enough that the next steps were clear. The question was whether right would be fast enough. The state did not need to disprove the site's significance. It only needed to create enough procedural uncertainty to justify a pause, and a pause at this stage, with the permit already under pressure, could become a closure, and a closure could become a transfer, and a transfer could become the end of the excavation before the deeper strata were ever reached.

She had been in the field long enough to know that evidence did not protect itself.

She turned off the lamp. In the dark, the laptop screen lit the trailer with its cold blue, and the bead sketch was just visible on the open notebook, the small circle, the off-axis hole, the note about iridescence, and she thought: someone left this here on purpose, and someone else spent three hundred years trying to erase the fact that they had, and now it is sitting in a zip-lock bag on a folding table in a rented trailer, and the only thing standing between it and another erasure is documentation.

She opened the laptop and began to type.

The email to Tayak was already half-written.

*Subject: Bead provenance, need to talk. Found something in the 1691 grant that, *

She stopped.

Read it back. Read it again.

The cursor blinked at the end of the sentence like a question she hadn't finished asking.

The problem was not the email. The problem was the word *found*, because found implied a channel, and a channel implied a record, and a record in the university system meant a timestamp, and a timestamp meant that the moment she sent this, the discovery existed officially. Not in her private notebook. Not in the careful hand she used for things she intended to control. In the system. Logged. Searchable. Subject to the same mandatory reporting protocols that governed every significant find on a state-permitted excavation in Maryland.

She had read the permit conditions three times. She had read them the way she read everything Tayak gave her: slowly, with a pencil, marking the clauses that seemed designed to be forgotten.

*Any discovery of material evidence bearing on the historical or cultural significance of the permitted site shall be reported to the State Historic Preservation Office within five business days of identification.*

She had looked up the five-business-day threshold when she first read the permit, confirmed it against the Maryland Historical Trust's own guidance document, and written it in the inside cover of her field notebook in red pen. Five business days. Which sounded reasonable until you understood that Slade's office was the SHPO, and that Slade had been looking for a jurisdictional hook since the permit was issued, and that a formal report of a land grant naming a Piscataway settlement at the exact coordinates of the mound, filed by a graduate student at eleven-fifteen on a Wednesday night, before Tayak had a legal strategy, before Marcus had briefed the council, before anyone had talked to a lawyer, would hand him exactly what he needed.

Not a reason to stop the excavation. He didn't need a reason. He needed a *process*. A review. A pause while the review was conducted. A second review because the first one raised questions. A jurisdictional question about whether the university's permit covered a site of this apparent significance or whether a new permit was required. A new permit that would take six months and might not be granted.

She deleted the email.

Not archived. Deleted. She watched the draft folder count drop by one and felt something she could not quite call relief.

The frogs were still going outside. She had stopped hearing them an hour ago, the way you stopped hearing traffic if you grew up near a road, but now they came back, layered, wet, insistent, the sound of the marsh at the bottom of the hill doing whatever marshes did at this hour. One of the research notes she had pulled that afternoon mentioned that the mound sat on a well-drained knoll above the floodplain, the kind of rise that Indigenous communities in this region had preferred for habitation and burial both, high enough to see the creek but dry enough to dig. She had written that down in the convergence page. She had not yet thought about what it meant to be sitting, right now, on that same rise, in a rented aluminum trailer, listening to the same water that had been running past this ground for two thousand years.

She thought about it now.

The lamp was off. The laptop screen was the only light, and it made the trailer feel like the inside of a fish tank, everything visible but nothing warm. She pulled her field jacket off the back of the chair and put it on without standing up, a maneuver she had perfected over three field seasons, and looked at the bead in its bag on the specimen tray.

She could call Tayak instead of emailing. A phone call left no institutional record. But Tayak had been in back-to-back meetings since seven that morning, the Voss call, then Marcus, then whatever had happened after, and it was past eleven, and even if Tayak was awake, which she almost certainly was, a phone call at this hour would feel like an emergency. And this was not an emergency. This was the opposite of an emergency. This was something that needed to be handled slowly, carefully, in the right order, with the right people in the room.

Wren had a talent for recognizing the difference between things that were urgent and things that felt urgent. Her mother had never understood this about her. *You sit on things*, her mother said, meaning it as a criticism, meaning Wren was slow, overcautious, congenitally unable to act. What her mother had never grasped was that sitting on something and doing nothing were not the same. Sitting on something was a decision. It required knowing what you had, knowing what it could cost if you moved too fast, and choosing to hold it until the moment was right.

She was holding this.

Morning. She would go to Tayak's office before the building filled up, before anyone was at their desk, and she would put the convergence page on the table between them and say: I found something last night in the 1691 grant and I haven't reported it yet and I need you to tell me how to proceed before I do. Not through email. Not through the system. In person, off the record, the way Tayak had taught her to do the things that mattered.

*Document everything twice,* Tayak had said, standing at the edge of the trench with her hands in her pockets, watching the bead come up in the trowel. *Once for the record. Once for us.*

This was the second documentation.

She pulled the notebook toward her and opened it to the last page she had written on, the convergence page, the block letters, the line she had drawn under the conclusion. Below the line, below *The archive has limits. The community may not,* she wrote one more line. She wrote it in the same block letters, the ones she used for things she intended to be read:

*I know something I am not yet sure how to say.*

She looked at it for a moment. Then she closed the notebook.

The laptop she shut without putting it to sleep, so the screen went dark all at once and the trailer was suddenly very black. She sat in it. Through the aluminum wall she could hear the frogs, and further off, the low sound of a car on the county road, someone coming home late or starting early, the headlights sweeping once across the tree line and then gone. The night settled back.

The bead was in its bag on the tray. The notebook was closed on the table. The petition scan was still open on the darkened screen she could no longer see, that partial name preserved in its damaged folio, four letters and a dash and then the brown tide of three centuries taking the rest. Somewhere in that name was a person who had stood on this ground and understood what it was, who had taken a bead that had traveled from a Dutch furnace through Lenape hands to a Piscataway wrist and placed it deliberately in the earth of a rise above a floodplain creek, who had then walked to the county court and filed a petition arguing that the ground was sacred and held the dead, and who had lost.

Had lost then. The petition had failed. The land had been granted. The settlement had been named in the document and then, presumably, erased from the documents that followed, the way things were erased, not by fire, not by a single act, but by omission, by the slow accumulation of records that referred to the land without referring to the people who had lived on it, until the people became a phrase in a boundary description and the phrase became a landmark and the landmark became a coordinate on a map that no longer carried any memory of what it marked.

Three hundred years of that.

And now the bead was in a zip-lock bag on a folding table, and she knew what she knew, and she was choosing to wait until morning to say it.

She hoped morning would be soon enough.

She did not know if it would be.
