The trench was three feet deep at the south end and smelled of wet clay and something older underneath, a dark, almost mineral sweetness that Tayak had learned to read the way a doctor reads a patient's color. Good soil. Undisturbed soil. The kind that held things.

She was on her knees at the profile wall, a Marshalltown trowel in her right hand and a magnifying loupe pushed up on her forehead, when she heard the footsteps.

Not the measured tread of someone crossing a field to do ordinary work. Too quick. Too purposeful. The grass between the equipment trailer and the trench was still wet from last night's rain, and whoever was walking through it was not watching their feet.

Tayak sat back on her heels and looked up.

Wren was forty yards out and closing fast, her green field notebook pressed against her chest with both arms, the way you carry something you don't want to drop. Her hair had come loose on one side. She hadn't stopped to pull on her site vest.

Tayak's first thought was: she found something in the profile photographs. Her second thought, arriving half a second behind the first and crowding it out, was: something is wrong with the permit.

She had been living with that second thought for three weeks. It surfaced every time a vehicle she didn't recognize turned down the access road, every time her phone buzzed with an unknown number, every time Voss sent an email that opened with *Just checking in.* The Section 106 consultation was still technically open. Slade had not returned her last two calls. The tribal council's recognition petition was sitting in a federal inbox somewhere, waiting, and the dig, her dig, the one she had spent four years designing, was the only living evidence that her people had been here long enough for the petition to mean anything.

She set the trowel on the unit form and climbed out of the trench.

The August heat hit her across the shoulders like a hand. The field ran flat toward the tree line, and the air above it shimmered in a way that made Wren's approaching figure look briefly uncertain, like something seen through old glass. The smell of tidal marsh came in from the east, salt and rot and the faint diesel note of a boat somewhere on the Potomac.

Wren was twenty yards out now. Her face was doing something Tayak couldn't quite read. Not panic. Not the particular blankness that came with bad news about bones or permits. Something more inward than that.

"You're not on the schedule until nine," Tayak said.

Wren stopped in front of her. She was breathing through her nose in a controlled way, the way you breathed when you'd been moving fast and didn't want to show it.

"I went to the archive yesterday afternoon." She held the notebook out. "I found something. I didn't want to put it in a text."

Tayak looked at the notebook. She did not take it yet.

"What kind of something?"

"Land records." Wren's jaw tightened. "Old ones."

The marsh smell came again, thicker this time. Somewhere in the tree line a red-winged blackbird gave its two-note call and went quiet.

Tayak took the notebook.

Wren had flagged the page with a strip torn from a paper bag. The notebook fell open there.

The handwriting was Wren's, small and vertical, the kind that pressed hard enough to show through to the next page. She'd copied the key lines out by hand, the way she always did when she didn't trust a photograph to hold the detail. The Maryland State Archives kept the colonial deed series in bound volumes, their spines cracked and their pages foxed brown at the edges, and Wren had noted the call number at the top of the page in the same careful hand she used for everything. Below that, in a box she'd drawn with a ruler:

*Patent Book, Liber 38, folio 214. Province of Maryland. Granted by authority of Charles Calvert, 3rd Lord Baltimore, to Thomas Calvert, his cousin, in the year of Our Lord 1697. Three hundred and forty acres, bounded on the north by the head of the tributary called Nanjemoy Creek, on the east by the great pine standing at the ridge, on the south by the course of the river, on the west by Calvert's own line.*

Tayak read it twice. Then she looked up.

Wren was watching her with the careful attention of someone who has delivered news before and learned not to speak into the silence that follows.

"Calvert's own line," Tayak said.

"That's the western boundary. It references an earlier survey, the proprietary survey from 1680, which I haven't found yet, but the call is clear." Wren reached over and tapped the page without touching the writing. "Three hundred and forty acres. I pulled the current parcel map on my phone in the parking lot. The overlap isn't exact, the creek has shifted, and the great pine is obviously gone, but the center of the grant sits about sixty yards north-northwest of our datum stake."

Tayak looked back at the notebook. She was aware of her own breathing, slow and even, and she made a point to keep it that way.

"Your name," she said.

"Yes."

"Calvert."

"My mother's family. Her maiden name. She never used it. She married young and was glad to drop it, honestly, but." Wren stopped. She pressed her lips together. "The Thomas Calvert in the record. I cross-referenced the probate index. He had two sons. The line runs straight. I checked it three times because I didn't want it to be true."

The red-winged blackbird called again from the tree line. The sound was bright and indifferent.

Tayak handed the notebook back. She did it carefully, the way you set down something that belongs to someone else.

What she did not do: say *I'm sorry.* What she did not do: reach for Wren's arm, or let her face do the thing it wanted to do, which was show the particular horror of a person watching a clean problem become a complicated one. She had four seconds, maybe five, before Wren would read her silence as coldness, and she needed those seconds.

*Three hundred and forty acres. Calvert line. 1697.*

The year sat in her chest like a stone.

1697 was the year Saya's petition failed. Tayak knew this because she had read the oilskin document herself, in this trench, six weeks ago. The petition had been filed, denied, and the land around the mound had passed, she had assumed, into the grey administrative silence of a colonial government that did not record what it took. But colonial governments did record what they gave. They were meticulous about that.

She thought: *If Thomas Calvert received a grant in 1697, and if the chain of title from 1697 to now has any gap in it, any probate irregularity, any unrecorded transfer, any heirship question, then the current owner's title is clouded.*

She thought: *If the title is clouded, the university's CRM contract is on an encumbered parcel.*

She thought: *If the parcel is encumbered, Slade has grounds to flag the Section 106 consultation as procedurally incomplete.*

She thought: *If the consultation stalls, the recognition petition stalls with it, because the petition cites this site as the primary evidence of continuous presence, and continuous presence on a piece of land that is suddenly in legal dispute looks, to a federal examiner who wants a reason to table the file, like exactly the kind of contested claim they are not required to resolve.*

All of this arrived in the time it took Wren to tuck the notebook back against her chest.

"How long have you known?" Tayak asked.

"Since yesterday afternoon. I drove out this morning. I didn't sleep much."

Tayak could see that. Wren's eyes had the particular flatness of someone who had spent the night running the same thought in circles.

"Did you tell anyone else?"

"No. I came straight here."

"Did you write it down anywhere besides that notebook?"

Wren's expression shifted, not offense exactly, but something adjacent. "I took a photograph of the original record. It's on my phone."

"Okay." Tayak looked out across the field. The morning was already hot enough that the survey stakes threw no shadow. The neon orange flags on the nearest one had gone limp in the still air. "Walk me through the chain. Start at 1697."

Wren opened the notebook again. Her hands were steadier now. She was better with a task in front of her.

"The grant goes to Thomas Calvert in 1697. He dies in 1714. Probate in Charles County. I found the will index entry, but I haven't pulled the full document. He leaves the land to his elder son, William. William's line I can trace through deed records to 1763, when there's a sale, or what looks like a sale. The record is a conveyance, but the consideration listed is one shilling, which in Maryland colonial practice signals a family transfer, not an arm's-length transaction."

"Who receives it in 1763?"

"A James Calvert. I don't know yet if he's a son or a nephew. After 1763 the trail goes quiet until 1819, when there's a recorded deed from a Calvert estate to a family named Hollis. The Hollis family held it through the Civil War. Then a series of transfers in the 1880s and 1890s, and eventually it ends up in a trust that the current landowner." Wren paused. "The person who gave us site access. Their family trust."

Tayak said nothing.

"There's a gap," Wren said. "Between 1763 and 1819. Fifty-six years. No recorded deed. It could be a probate that was never entered, or a transfer by will that wasn't registered, or a family transfer that everyone at the time understood and nobody wrote down."

"Or it could be challenged," Tayak said.

The word sat between them in the hot air.

Wren closed the notebook. "I know what this does to the petition."

"Do you."

"The site is your primary evidence. If the title is in dispute, any quiet title action would name the state, potentially the university, potentially." She stopped. "Potentially the tribe. As an interested party."

"As an interested party," Tayak repeated. The phrase was clean and legal and it meant: *your people get pulled into a courtroom over a piece of ground they never stopped living on, and the courtroom gets to decide whether their presence on it counts as a claim or a complication.*

She had been through one version of this before, in a different county, with a different site, and a different outcome. The outcome had taken six years and cost two careers and produced a settlement that gave the tribe consultative status over a parking lot.

She was not going to let that happen here.

"I need you to not say anything," Tayak said. "To anyone. Not Marcus, not Kimi, not Voss. Not in an email, not in a text."

Wren looked at her. "For how long?"

"Until I figure out what we're dealing with."

A long pause. The marsh smell came in again, and with it the sound of a truck somewhere on the access road, distant, probably nothing, but Tayak's attention went to it before she could stop herself.

"Okay," Wren said finally.

It was not a comfortable agreement. Tayak could hear that. Wren's voice had the particular flatness of someone who has said yes to a thing they are not sure they should say yes to, and who knows that they know it.

Tayak felt the same way.

She picked up her trowel from where she'd left it on the edge of the trench. The handle was warm from the sun. She turned it once in her hand, not because she needed to, but because she needed something to hold.

"Go get a water bottle," she said. "It's going to be a long morning."

Tayak did not put the trowel down.

She stood at the edge of the trench and turned it in her hand, and then she said it aloud, because she had learned long ago that the things she kept inside her head stayed soft and manageable until they became catastrophic, and she could not afford catastrophe right now.

"If someone challenges the title," she said, "they file a quiet title action in the circuit court. Charles County. The state gets notice because the state is always a party when the chain goes back to a proprietary grant. Slade gets notice because Slade is the SHPO and the site is already flagged under Section 106."

Wren had not moved toward the water. She stood three feet away with the notebook against her chest and watched Tayak.

"Slade doesn't need to file anything himself," Tayak continued. "He just needs to know the title is clouded. Once he knows, he has grounds to say the Section 106 consultation is procedurally incomplete because the landowner's authority to grant site access is in question. He can freeze the consultation. Legally. No drama, no press conference. He just stops returning calls and cites procedural uncertainty and waits."

The marsh smell was stronger now. Somewhere behind the tree line a red-winged blackbird called once and went quiet.

"And if the Section 106 consultation freezes," Tayak said, "the petition freezes with it. Because the petition cites this site. This specific site, this specific ground, as the primary material evidence of continuous presence. That's the architecture of the whole argument. We have the oral record, we have Nora's testimony, we have the genealogy, but the ground is what makes it unassailable. The ground is what you can't argue with." She paused. "Except now someone can argue with the ground."

She heard herself say it and it sounded worse than it had inside her head.

Wren said, quietly: "The federal examiner."

"The federal examiner gets a file that says: *Piscataway Nation petitioning for recognition on the basis of continuous ancestral presence at a site currently subject to a quiet title dispute.* And the examiner, who has forty-seven other petitions on the desk and a mandate to table anything with unresolved legal complexity, puts the file in a different pile. A pile that does not move." Tayak looked at the trowel. "That pile is where petitions go to die. I have watched it happen twice."

She had not meant to say that last part. It came out anyway.

Wren's face had changed. Tayak registered it without looking directly at her, the slight shift in posture, the way the notebook came down an inch from her chest. Wren had expected something. Concern, maybe. Or the kind of measured professional response that acknowledged the difficulty before pivoting to solutions. What she had not expected was this: Tayak standing at the edge of a trench, speaking in a flat, precise voice, working through the geometry of a legal disaster the way you work through the geometry of a collapsed unit wall. Not with feeling, but with method, because method is the only thing that helps.

Tayak knew what she looked like. She kept going anyway.

"The gap in the chain is 1763 to 1819. Fifty-six years. That's long enough to matter. A probate that was never entered, a will that was never registered, a family transfer that everyone at the time understood and nobody wrote down. Any of those explanations could be true. Probably one of them is true. But *probably* is not a legal standard, and in a quiet title action, the party with the most to gain from uncertainty is the party who files first."

"Who has the most to gain?" Wren asked.

Tayak looked at her then. "Whoever wants the site tied up long enough for the recognition window to close."

The word *Slade* did not need to be said. It moved between them anyway, in the hot still air, in the smell of tidal mud and cut grass and the faint chemical bite of the survey marker paint on the nearest stake.

Wren said: "I didn't find this to hurt the petition."

"I know that."

"I found it because it's in the record and I was doing my job and."

"Wren." Tayak's voice was not unkind. "I know that."

A pause. The blackbird called again from the tree line, two notes, and stopped.

"You're not asking me if I'm all right," Wren said. It was not an accusation. It was an observation, careful and precise, the way Wren made all her observations.

Tayak looked at her steadily. "No."

"Because you're not all right either."

"No."

"And because." Wren stopped. Worked something out behind her eyes. "Because if you stop to think about what this means for me, you have to stop thinking about what it means for the petition. And you can't do that right now."

Tayak did not answer. The silence was its own answer, and they both knew it, and neither of them pretended otherwise.

This was the part that no one told you about. Not the fieldwork, not the permits, not the legal briefs or the council meetings or the nights reading NAGPRA case law on a laptop with a failing battery. This part: standing in a field in the August heat, holding a trowel you didn't need to hold, realizing that the person across from you had just handed you a problem that was also a person, and that you were treating it as the problem first. That you were correct to treat it as the problem first. That being correct did not make it feel any better.

Wren's family name was on the grant.

Thomas Calvert, 1697.

Tayak had not said that part aloud yet. She had not needed to. Wren had found the record herself, had driven out here herself, had not slept. She understood what her name meant in this context. She was standing here anyway.

That was worth something. Tayak was not sure yet what.

"The gap," Tayak said. "1763 to 1819. Is there anything in the record that explains it, or is it just absent?"

Wren straightened slightly. The question was something she could answer, and answering was easier than the silence. "Just absent. The deed index for Charles County has entries on either side, the 1763 conveyance to James Calvert, and then the 1819 deed to Hollis, but nothing between. It could be in the probate records. Or in a deed book that was damaged. There was a courthouse fire in 1892."

"So the gap might have a documentary explanation that just hasn't survived."

"Possibly."

"Or the gap might be a real gap. A transfer that was never recorded. Which means the 1819 deed to Hollis could be."

"Defective," Wren said. "Yes."

Tayak turned the trowel once more. The handle had a worn spot where the grip tape had peeled back, and the bare metal there was cooler than the rest, despite the heat. She had bought this trowel in her second year of graduate school. She had used it on seven sites. She had never used it on a site that mattered this much.

"A defective deed in 1819 means every transfer after it is potentially encumbered," she said. "Which means the trust that holds the land now."

"Could have a cloud on the title going back two hundred years," Wren said. "Yes. I know."

"And the landowner doesn't know this."

"I don't think so. No."

"And Slade doesn't know."

"Not yet."

*Not yet.* The words sat there in the air between them, doing the work that neither of them wanted to name.

Tayak set the trowel down on the edge of the trench. She set it down carefully, blade-side away from the exposed soil, the way she always did, because even now, even in this, the habit of care was the one thing she could control.

She thought about Marcus. She thought about Nora. She thought about the recognition petition sitting in a federal examiner's inbox in Washington, forty-two miles north, with its genealogies and its oral testimonies and its maps and its photographs of this ground, this specific ground, this mound that her family had been tending for two thousand years without anyone's permission and without anyone's record, because they had not needed a record, because they had been here.

She thought about the pile that does not move.

"Nobody knows," she said. "That's where we are right now. Nobody knows except you and me."

Wren nodded. Her face was careful and still, the way it got when she was holding something she had not decided what to do with yet.

"I need time to think about how this gets handled," Tayak said. "Not days. Hours, maybe. But I need to think before anyone else is in this conversation, because once someone else is in it, I can't control what happens next."

"What are you going to do?" Wren asked.

Tayak picked up the trowel again. She did not have an answer yet, and she was not going to invent one to fill the silence.

"I'm going to dig," she said. "And you're going to get a water bottle. And we are going to act, for the rest of this morning, like nothing has changed."

Wren looked at her for a moment, not with resentment exactly, but with the clear-eyed recognition of someone who has just understood something about the person in front of them that they had not understood before.

"Okay," Wren said. The same word she had used before. The same flatness. But there was something else in it now, not quite resignation, not quite respect. Something in between, that had not had time yet to decide which way it would fall.

She turned and walked back toward the equipment trailer. Tayak watched her go, then looked back down into the trench.

The soil was dark at the bottom where the morning's work had stopped. In the far corner, where the profile met the north wall, there was a faint change in color, a slightly deeper stain, reddish-brown at the edges, that she had been meaning to look at more closely before Wren had appeared at the field's edge with her notebook and her sleepless eyes and her family name written in a 1697 colonial record.

Tayak climbed down into the trench.

The soil was cool against her knees. She picked up the smaller brush from the tool kit at the base of the north wall and began to work at the stain, slowly, the way she always did, because the ground did not care about the legal geometry above it. The ground had been keeping its secrets for a long time. It would keep them a little longer.

But the stain was there. She could see it clearly now, in the low-angled morning light.

Something was under it.

Wren came back with two water bottles. She set one on the trench edge without a word and walked to her own unit, twenty meters north, and picked up her trowel. Tayak watched her from below, then looked back at the stain.

She worked for forty minutes without speaking. The brush moved in short strokes, and the reddish-brown margin held its shape, not a root trace, not a mottling from old plowzone disturbance. A feature. Something deliberate. She photographed it with her phone, set a scale bar, photographed it again, and wrote four lines in her context sheet. Then she climbed out of the trench and walked to Wren's unit.

"Come look at this."

Wren set her trowel down and followed without asking why. That was one of the things Tayak valued about her. She did not perform curiosity. She either had it or she did not, and right now she did.

They stood at the edge of the trench together. Tayak pointed at the stain with the cap of her Sharpie.

"Feature," Wren said.

"Yes."

"Pit or post?"

"I don't know yet. The margins are too regular for a root. And look at the color, that red-brown at the edge. That's oxidized organic material. Something was in there that decayed slowly."

Wren crouched. She tilted her head the way she did when she was reading soil the way other people read text. "Could be a burial preparation deposit. Ochre scatter, maybe."

"Maybe."

Neither of them said what they were both thinking, that if it was, the site's significance just doubled, and the legal geometry above it became twice as dangerous.

Tayak straightened. "I need to talk to you about the other thing."

Wren stood. She looked at Tayak steadily, the way she had all morning, that careful, unreadable stillness.

"The chain of title," Tayak said. "The gap. What you found."

"All right."

"Nobody else can know. Not yet." She kept her voice level. "Not Slade. Not Voss. Not Marcus. Not Kimi."

Wren said nothing.

"If Slade finds out there's a cloud on the title, he has grounds to freeze the permit. He's been looking for grounds since the day we sank the first stake. You know that."

"I know that."

"If Voss finds out, he goes to university counsel, and counsel recommends we suspend fieldwork pending legal review. Which is what counsel always recommends, because counsel's job is to manage exposure, not to excavate a two-thousand-year-old burial site before the state takes it."

"And Marcus?"

Tayak hesitated. That one was harder to say. "If Marcus knows, the council knows. And if the council knows, it becomes a political question, who controls the information, who gets to decide how it's used, whether to go public with it as a counter-claim or sit on it. And we lose the ability to choose our moment."

Wren looked at her for a long time. The sun had moved while they were talking, and now it came in low over the tree line to the east, and the trench threw a clean shadow across the feature, making the reddish stain look darker and more deliberate than it had before.

"You're asking me to keep this from the people who have the most stake in it," Wren said.

"I'm asking you to keep it until I understand what it means legally. Until I've talked to someone who can tell me whether this is a real cloud or a resolvable gap. There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"Yes." Tayak kept her eyes on Wren's. "A missing deed book destroyed in an 1892 courthouse fire is not the same as a fraudulent transfer. One is a documentation problem. The other is a title problem. I need to know which one we're dealing with before I put it in front of anyone who has to act on it."

Wren looked down at the feature. "And if it's a real title problem?"

"Then I'll figure out how to handle it."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Tayak said. "It's not."

The marsh smell came in off the creek, brackish, faintly sulphurous, the particular rot-and-salt of a tidal flat at low water that Tayak had known since childhood and had never been able to decide if she loved or hated. It was just the smell of here. Of this specific ground.

"I'm not asking you to lie," she said. "If someone asks you directly what you found in the archive, you tell them you found the Calvert family in the county deed index. That's true. That's all you say. You don't volunteer the gap. You don't mention the 1819 deed. You don't speculate about the chain of title."

"And if someone asks me directly about the gap?"

"Nobody's going to ask you directly about the gap, because nobody else knows there is one."

Wren picked up a piece of oyster shell from the spoil heap beside the unit and turned it in her fingers, bleached white from decades in the soil, the kind that turned up in every shovel-test pit on this side of the county. It was a habit Tayak had noticed before, something she did when she was thinking.

"For how long?" Wren asked.

Tayak looked at her.

"How long do I not say anything? A day? A week? Until the petition is decided? Until the dig is done?" Wren's voice was not angry. It was something more precise than that, a graduate student who had learned to ask the question the supervisor had not answered because the supervisor had not wanted to answer it. "I need to know what I'm agreeing to."

Tayak wanted to give her a number. She understood the impulse. Wren was a person who worked in defined units, who labeled her bags and logged her context sheets in real time and never left a provenience code blank. She needed a boundary. She needed to know the shape of the thing she was holding.

"I don't know yet," Tayak said.

Wren set the oyster shell down on the trench edge. She did not throw it. She set it down deliberately, the way Tayak had set down the trowel earlier, and the similarity was not lost on either of them.

"Okay," Wren said.

That word again. Flat and careful and carrying more weight than it showed.

"I need you to trust me," Tayak said. She did not say it easily. It was not the kind of sentence she said easily.

"I do trust you," Wren said. "That's why I came to you first instead of sending an email."

The sentence landed with a precision that Tayak felt in her sternum. Wren had come to her first. Had walked across a field with her notebook and her sleepless eyes and handed her a live wire, because she trusted Tayak to know what to do with it. And Tayak had taken it and was now asking her to help keep it quiet, and they were both standing here in the smell of the marsh pretending that this was a temporary measure and not the beginning of something that would be very hard to walk back.

"Thank you," Tayak said. "For coming to me."

Wren nodded. She picked up her trowel and looked at the feature one more time. "Do you want me to start a separate context sheet for this? Or hold off until you've talked to whoever you're going to talk to?"

"Start the sheet," Tayak said. "Document everything normally. The feature is real regardless of what's above it."

"Right."

Wren went back to her own unit. Tayak stood at the edge of the trench and looked down at the stain, at its careful reddish margins, at the thing it was holding that had been waiting in the dark for a very long time.

She thought about Marcus. She thought about what she had just asked Wren to do, and what she had not been able to tell her. The *for how long* had no answer yet because the answer depended on whether the petition survived, and the petition surviving depended on nobody pulling the thread that Wren had found in a county deed index in a building that smelled of old paper and fluorescent light. The silence was not a temporary measure. It might become permanent. She was already, right now, in this moment, making the kind of decision that looked like strategy from the inside and looked like something else entirely from the outside.

She climbed back into the trench. She picked up the brush. She began to work the margin of the stain, slowly, the way she always did.

The ground kept its secrets. She was asking Wren to keep one too.

She did not let herself think about whether Wren would.

Wren walked back toward the equipment tent without looking over her shoulder.

Tayak watched her go, the set of her shoulders, the notebook tucked under her arm, the way she picked her path around the flagged stakes without having to look down. Eight months on site. She knew where every stake was.

Tayak lowered herself into the trench.

The brush was where she had left it, handle resting against the unit wall, bristles just touching the soil. She picked it up. She did not use it.

The stain in front of her was perhaps forty centimeters across, a slightly darker smear in the subsoil, reddish at the margins where iron had leached into the surrounding matrix, its edges soft enough that she would have to map them twice to be sure. A feature. Real and patient and entirely indifferent to what was happening above it.

She turned the brush in her hand.

What she had just done was ask a twenty-eight-year-old woman, who had driven to an archive on her own time, who had found something real, who had come across a field with it because she trusted the person she brought it to, to fold that thing up and put it somewhere quiet and not look at it again. For an unspecified period. For reasons that were sound, legally, and that Tayak could not fully explain without making them sound worse than they were.

She touched the brush to the stain's upper margin and drew it once, lightly, across the soil. A small curl of dark earth came free.

The petition. The chain of title. The Calvert name in a deed index and a gap between 1763 and 1819, a gap that could mean nothing, that could mean a clerical failure, that could mean an inheritance nobody wrote down. Or it could mean exactly what it looked like, which was an unresolved proprietary interest running back to a royal charter that predated the state of Maryland by a hundred and fifty years. A gap that a university lawyer would describe as an encumbrance. That a title insurer would flag as an exception. That an opponent, Slade, or someone behind Slade, or someone Tayak hadn't thought of yet, could use to argue that the ground beneath this trench was in active dispute and that any recognition petition tied to it was therefore premature.

She drew the brush again. Another curl of soil.

The irony was not subtle. She was excavating a burial mound to prove that her people had never left this land, that they had buried their dead here across two thousand years of continuous presence, that the ground itself was the evidence. And she had just asked her graduate student to bury a discovery. Not in the ground. In the space between what Wren knew and what Wren said. Which was a different kind of burial and not a better one.

From the equipment tent, she heard the zip of a bag, the scrape of a folding chair. Wren settling in. Starting the context sheet, probably, the way Tayak had told her to, documenting the feature, logging the soil color, writing the provenience code in the top right corner in the small, precise hand she used for everything. Doing her job. Doing it correctly. Doing it while carrying the thing Tayak had handed back to her and called a temporary measure.

*For how long?*

Tayak had not answered that question. She had said *I don't know yet*, which was true, but it was not the whole truth. The whole truth was that the answer depended on variables she did not control: the petition timeline, the state's next move, whether Marcus had already said something to someone, whether Slade had a researcher of his own. The whole truth was that there was a version of this where Wren's silence became permanent, where the petition was decided, and the dig was done, and the Calvert gap was a thing that had happened in a county archive and been set aside and never spoken of again, and Wren would carry that for the rest of her career.

A graduate student who had come to her first instead of sending an email.

Tayak set the brush down.

The marsh smell moved through the trench, that low, particular rot-and-salt that meant low tide, that meant the creek was pulling back from the flat and leaving the dark mud exposed to the morning sun. She had grown up with that smell. It was the smell of this county, this watershed, this specific ground. It did not ask her anything.

She thought about Wren's dissertation. Three years of work, still in its early chapters. A study of ceramic continuity in the upper Potomac watershed, careful and methodical, a project that would become a careful, methodical monograph, and which depended on this dig, on the data from these units, on the artifacts in these bags, on the institutional relationship between Wren and the university and the tribe. All of it sitting on the same ground as the petition. All of it, now, sitting on the same ground as the gap.

If the title dispute surfaced, when it surfaced, because it would, because these things always surfaced, the question of when Wren had known would matter. And the question of when Tayak had known. And the question of what they had done with that knowledge.

She had told Wren: *if someone asks you directly, you tell them you found the Calvert family in the county deed index.* Which was true. Which was all Wren needed to say. Which was not a lie and was also not the whole truth, and Tayak knew the difference, and she had asked Wren to live in that difference for an unspecified period of time, and Wren had said *okay* and meant it, and that was the part that sat in Tayak's chest like a stone.

She picked up the brush again.

The feature waited. The soil held its color, that reddish margin, that darker center, the faint suggestion of something deliberate beneath. Someone had done something here. Someone had made a choice about what to put in the ground and what to keep above it, and that choice had lasted long enough that Tayak was now brushing its edges with a soft-bristled tool in the morning heat, two thousand years or two hundred years or some span of time she did not yet know, and the ground had kept it.

She worked the margin slowly, the way she always did. The brush moved in short strokes. Soil came free in small amounts. The feature's edge sharpened slightly, its boundary becoming more legible, the line between disturbed and undisturbed soil growing cleaner with each pass.

She did not let herself think about whether Wren would hold.

She thought about it anyway.
