The truck smelled of creek mud and old coffee, and Tayak had not invited Marcus inside it.

He'd climbed in anyway — passenger door, no hesitation, the way men who've chaired committees for twenty-two years climb into spaces they haven't been offered. He set his clipboard on the dash and folded his hands in his lap, and the field gate stood twenty feet ahead of them, padlock open, Dawes's soybean rows running flat and brown to the tree line beyond.

Tayak left the engine running. Heat was heat.

"I talked to someone at the BIA field office," Marcus said. "Informal call. Off the record."

"When."

"Tuesday."

She'd found the colonial document on Monday. She kept her eyes on the gate.

"There's a pathway," he said. "If a tribe in active petition voluntarily submits material evidence of continuous occupation to the relevant state authority — and the submission is documented as voluntary — the OFA can flag the file for expedited review. Not guaranteed. But the pathway exists."

The defroster was pushing warm air across the bottom of the windshield, and the glass was still fogging at the corners. Outside, a crow dropped onto the gate's crossbar, looked at nothing in particular, and left.

"The relevant state authority," Tayak said.

"The State Historic Preservation Office."

"Slade's office."

"His office handles it, yes. He wouldn't personally—"

"He would personally." She turned to look at Marcus for the first time. He was wearing his pressed field shirt, the one he seemed to own in multiples, and his clipboard had a yellow legal pad clipped to it with three lines of notes she couldn't read from this angle. "What does the submission actually mean? Legally. What does it mean for the document."

Marcus picked up the clipboard and set it down again. "It means the state takes custody."

"Custody."

"For the purposes of the evidentiary record. It stays in the file as—"

"Where is the file kept."

"The SHPO archive. Annapolis."

Tayak looked back at the gate. The padlock was hanging open on its hasp, swinging slightly in a wind she couldn't feel from inside the truck. Dawes had left it that way three weeks ago and she didn't think he'd noticed it was still open. That was the kind of man Dawes was — polite, incurious, and entirely unaware of what he was sitting on top of.

"There's a 1742 Nanticoke land petition in the SHPO archive," she said. "Submitted voluntarily by the Chicone community as part of a boundary dispute. You know where it is now?"

"Tayak—"

"Box fourteen, shelf C, building three. I know because I spent four hours with it in 2019. You know what's in the finding aid? 'Unverified claim, status: inactive.' That document has been in that box for sixty years and no one has touched it. No one has acted on it. It is not evidence of anything to anyone." She stopped. "That's what voluntary submission means."

Marcus was quiet for a moment. A truck passed on the county road behind them — someone's contractor, heading south — and the sound faded.

"The 1742 petition wasn't submitted during an active recognition review," he said. "The OFA pathway is different. The file is already open. The submission goes directly into an active evidentiary record, not into general archive."

"And you believe that."

"I believe the person I spoke to at the BIA field office was telling me the truth about how the pathway works."

"Did this person put anything in writing."

The pause was its own answer.

"Marcus." She said it without heat, the way you say the name of someone you've known long enough to skip past anger. "If I submit that document — if I hand it over — I lose it. I lose the only piece of physical evidence we have found in three weeks of work that speaks directly to continuous occupation of this site. The document doesn't say 'Piscataway people were here.' It says a named Piscataway individual filed a legal petition in 1697 arguing this ground was sacred and held the community's dead. That's not a bead. That's a name. That's a legal act. That's two thousand years of presence collapsed into one sentence in someone's handwriting."

She stopped again. The windshield had cleared.

"If I give that to Slade's office, I will never see it again."

Marcus looked at the gate. "The alternative is Slade uses the permit oversight clause to freeze the excavation, and we spend the next eight months in administrative review while Garrett Wills's bulldozers sit on the county road waiting for the injunction to expire." He said it evenly, the way he said most things — not cruel, just accurate. "The forty-eight days don't stop, Tayak. The highway doesn't stop."

She knew that. She'd known it since the morning she'd pulled the document from the soil in its oilskin wrapping, the paper inside still legible, the partial name still there in brown iron-gall ink — *Sioſ—* — the rest of the line gone to water damage but the petition language intact, the phrase *ancient possessors* intact, the date intact. She'd photographed it six times before she touched it. She'd logged it before she breathed.

"The document is fragile," she said. "It's been in controlled humidity for three hundred years. If I submit it to the state, it goes into their handling process. Standard archival sleeve, maybe, if someone remembers to use one. Maybe not. Every fold is a stress fracture. Every fluorescent light in that building is degrading the ink."

"I know."

"Do you."

"I know," Marcus said again, and this time he meant it differently — not *I understand the archival science* but *I know what you are telling me and I am asking you to do it anyway*, and that was the thing she couldn't answer yet.

Outside, the wind moved through the dead soybean stalks at the field's edge, a dry papery sound, and the gate's padlock swung again on its hasp.

She thought about the woman who had buried that document. She thought about Okwi, whose name she'd read in Nora's telling, holding a candle steady at a camp table while someone copied the petition in the dark. She thought about what it meant to entrust a thing to a system that had never once, in three hundred years, given anything back.

"I need to think," she said.

"I know that too." Marcus reached for the door handle. "But I need an answer by Friday. If we're going to use the pathway, we have to move before Slade files the oversight request. Once he files, the voluntary submission reads as reactive. It loses the good-faith value."

"How do you know he hasn't already filed."

Marcus opened the door and cold air came in off the field. "I don't," he said. "That's the other reason Friday matters."

He got out. He shut the door with the careful, considered pressure of a man who has learned not to slam things. She watched him walk back to his own car — a gray sedan, no plates visible from this angle, parked behind her on the shoulder — and she watched him go without calling him back.

The gate was still open. The field beyond it was hers for thirty-nine more days, and the mound was in there, and the second stratum was waiting, and somewhere in that ground there were more names.

She put the truck in gear.

She drove back through the gate instead of away from it.

The track was frozen in the ruts Dawes's tractor had cut last autumn, and the truck bucked and steadied as she followed it toward the tree line. Behind her, through the rear window, Marcus's gray sedan sat on the shoulder of the county road for a moment, then pulled away south. She didn't watch it go.

The mound was where she'd left it.

That was the thing about mounds. They waited. They had been waiting longer than any of the systems she was being asked to trust, and they would wait longer than any of the systems that might destroy them, and the ground didn't care about Friday or about Garrett Wills's bulldozers or about the good-faith value of a voluntary submission. The ground just held what it held.

She parked at the edge of the cleared area and sat for a moment with the engine off.

*Who receives the document.* She hadn't asked it cleanly enough. She'd let Marcus say *the Trust* and then let him qualify it twice — *the Trust's archival division*, then *the Trust's archival division in coordination with the OFA field liaison* — and she'd let both qualifications pass without pressing the one question underneath them: whose name goes on the custody form.

Because that was the thing. Not the pathway. Not the evidentiary record. Not the active file number at the BIA field office. The name on the custody form was the answer to everything else, and Marcus hadn't given it to her, and she didn't think he knew it.

She got out of the truck.

The air smelled of iron and dead grass and the faint mineral cold of soil that had been frozen and thawed and frozen again. Her boots crunched on the frost-stiffened ground as she walked to the equipment shelter — a canvas lean-to staked at the mound's eastern edge — and unzipped the side panel and pulled out her field notebook. Red cover. Carver's rule, still holding after fifteen years: red is easiest to find in a site bucket.

She stood at the edge of the excavation unit and looked down into the open trench.

The second stratum was six inches below the first. She could see the color change in the profile wall — the darker band of occupation soil, the compressed organics, the faint line where one era's living had settled into another era's ground. The section drawing was clipped to the board at the trench edge: careful pencil hatching, every layer labeled, the cord-marked sherd's position marked with a small arrow and the notation *18CH-447-14, context 2B*.

Tayak opened the notebook to a clean page.

She wrote three names at the top.

*Nanticoke. Pamunkey. Rappahannock/Mattaponi corridor, 2011.*

She'd said them to Marcus like weapons, which they were, but she hadn't said everything she knew about them. She hadn't said, for instance, that the Nanticoke submission had been made by a woman she knew — a woman who had trusted the same language Marcus had used today, the language of active files and OFA pathways and good-faith value — and that the woman had spent four years trying to get a response before she stopped trying. The document was in the finding aid. *Unverified claim, status: inactive.* The same language. The same box.

She hadn't said that the Pamunkey case was different in form but identical in result: a ceramic assemblage, voluntarily submitted to a state heritage office during a recognition review, correctly logged, correctly accessioned, and then routed to a storage facility in Richmond during a reorganization that no one had authorized and no one had reversed. The assemblage was still there. The recognition petition had been decided without it.

She hadn't said that the Virginia site — the one she'd worked in 2011 under a CRM contract she'd taken because she needed the money and because the principal investigator had promised community consultation — had produced a burial feature with associated grave goods that the PI had submitted to the state SHPO office on the same day he'd filed the final report. She had been the one who'd found the feature. She had been the one who'd documented it. Her name was in the report. She had never been consulted about the submission, and the grave goods were now in a university teaching collection in Charlottesville, listed under the PI's name as finder, with a NAGPRA compliance note that said *affiliation: undetermined.*

Marcus had not disputed any of it.

He'd said: *The forty-eight days don't stop.*

She wrote that in the notebook too, under the three names.

*48 days. Wills. Oversight clause. Friday.*

The wind moved through the soybean stubble at the field's edge — that dry, papery sound again, like someone turning old pages — and she looked up from the notebook and looked at the mound.

It was not a dramatic thing to look at. That was always the first surprise, for people who hadn't worked mounds. It was a low rise in the ground, maybe four feet at its highest point, grass-covered on the undisturbed sections, open soil in the two excavation units. The western flank showed the scrape damage from Dawes's equipment — a long diagonal compression where the tractor had crossed it without knowing what it was crossing. She'd documented the damage in the Phase I assessment. Forty-three pages, and two of them were about that scrape.

She walked around to the northern face and crouched at the edge of unit one.

The document had come out of this unit. Eighteen inches down, in a soil matrix that had shown no disturbance since deposition. The oilskin wrapping had been deteriorated at the edges but intact at the center, and the paper inside had been folded twice — a folio fold, the kind a colonial clerk would recognize — and when she'd opened it under the field loupe, the iron-gall ink had been brown and clear and legible, the letters formed in the careful, slightly uneven hand of someone who had learned to write from a document rather than from a teacher.

*Sioſ—*

The rest of the name gone. But the petition language intact. *Ancient possessors.* The date: March 1697. The ground described: *the high ground in Charles County known to the Piscataway people as the place of the community's dead.*

She had photographed it six times. She had logged it before she breathed.

Now Marcus was asking her to hand it to an office where the person who would receive it might or might not use an archival sleeve, where the chain of custody would run through at least two institutional handoffs before it reached anyone with authority over the recognition file, and where Garrett Slade's name was on the oversight structure of her excavation permit.

She sat back on her heels.

The custody question. She hadn't pushed it hard enough in the truck. She'd let Marcus say *the Trust* and move on, and she should have stopped him there and made him say the name. Not the institution. The name of the person whose signature would go on the intake form. Because that was the answer. If the name was someone she knew — someone with a record she could check — it was one calculation. If the name was a program specialist she'd never heard of, it was another. If the name routed back through Slade's office in any form, it was a different thing entirely, and Marcus hadn't been able to tell her it wouldn't.

He'd said: *I believe the person I spoke to at the BIA field office was telling me the truth.*

He hadn't said: *I know who receives the document.*

She stood up and walked back to the equipment shelter and looked at her phone. Four bars. The county road was close enough that the signal held.

She could call Marcus back. She could ask the custody question directly, make him find out, make him give her a name. That was the procedural move. That was what you did when the process was in good faith and the institution was trustworthy and the forty-eight days were the only variable.

She looked at the mound instead.

Somewhere in the second stratum — under the careful pencil lines, under the cord-marked sherd, under the compressed organic layer — there were more names. She didn't know whose yet. She didn't know what form they'd take. But the woman who had buried the 1697 document had buried it here, in this ground, in this mound, because this was the place the community's dead were kept. She hadn't submitted it to a system. She had put it in the earth, in the place where the earth held things, because she had understood — in 1697, in the middle of a colonial legal process that had already failed her — that the earth was more reliable than the institutions of the people who were taking her land.

Tayak understood that too.

The question was whether understanding it was a luxury she could afford in the next forty-eight days.

She put the phone in her pocket and picked up the trowel from the equipment shelf.

She would dig. She would work the second stratum while the light held, and she would log everything she found, and she would photograph it, and she would put the photographs in three places — the university server, her own drive, and the encrypted folder she'd set up the week after the Voss conversation, the one with the automatic off-site backup. And she would think about Friday. And she would think about the custody question, and the name that Marcus didn't have, and the forty-eight days that didn't stop.

But first she would dig.

Because the mound was here, and the second stratum was waiting, and whatever was in it had been waiting longer than any of them, and it deserved to be found by someone who was paying attention.

She stepped down into the trench.

The trench was cold.

She had not expected that — the way the air inside the unit held its chill even after the field above had softened in the late sun. She had been in enough trenches to know this, but knowing it and feeling it were different things, and she stood at the bottom of unit one with her trowel in her hand and felt the cold come up through the soles of her boots from the packed subsoil and thought: this is what the document felt like. Three hundred and twenty-seven years in this exact temperature. Give or take.

She did not begin to dig.

She stood for a moment and then she sat — not on the equipment shelf, but on the floor of the trench itself, her back against the southern wall, her knees drawn up, the trowel across her thighs. The northern face was directly in front of her at eye level. She could read it the way she read a page.

The surface layer was dark, almost black where the moisture had concentrated — a dense organic mat compacted by decades of soybean root systems and tractor weight. Below it, the color shifted: a warm reddish-brown, the colonial-era disturbed layer, looser in texture where it had been turned at some point and resettled. Below that, the layer she had come to think of as the document's layer — the tight, undisturbed matrix of fine sandy loam and decomposed leaf material that had held the oilskin wrap for three centuries without moving. The void the document had left was not visible to the eye, but she knew where it was. She had measured it. Fourteen inches down, six inches from the northern face, a rectangular compression in the surrounding soil that corresponded to a folded folio approximately eight inches by five.

The bead's void was higher: nine inches down, three inches from the face. Smaller. Round. The kind of absence that looked like nothing until you understood what had been there.

She looked at both voids — the places where things had been — and tried to think clearly.

She could not think clearly. That was why she was sitting on the floor of a cold trench instead of eating dinner.

The thing she had not said to Marcus was this: she was not sure she would submit the document at all.

Not delay submission. Not negotiate custody terms. Not hold it pending a named intake officer. *Not submit it.* Keep it in the field kit, log it as field evidence, use it in the Phase I assessment as a contextual find, and let the recognition case build around it without ever putting the original in a room where Garrett Slade's oversight structure could reach it.

She had not said this because she did not yet know if she believed it. It was a position that would put her in direct conflict with the council's legal strategy if it ever came out. Marcus had spent four months building the BIA filing around the principle that voluntary submission demonstrated good faith. If she withheld the most significant document in the collection, she would be making a unilateral decision that overrode twenty-two years of council work. She would be doing it alone, in a field in Charles County, without telling anyone.

She pressed her thumb against the trowel's worn wooden handle.

The thing was: Okwi had done exactly that.

She had not planned to think about Okwi. She had been thinking about custody chains and intake forms and the name Marcus didn't have. But Nora's voice was in her head now, the way it got sometimes when she was in the field — not as memory exactly, more as a texture, the particular pace of Nora at the kitchen table with her hands moving through the quilt squares, the way she told a story as though it had been waiting in the fabric all along and she was only reading it out.

*He wrapped it in oilskin and put it in the earth.* That was what Nora had said about Okwi. Not: he hid it. Not: he gave up. He put it in the earth, in the place where the earth held things, because the colonial petition had failed and the assembly had used their petition language against them and the only institution Okwi trusted at the end of 1697 was the ground.

And the ground had kept it.

Three hundred and twenty-seven years. Two inches of compressed organic matter above it. The oilskin gone at the edges, intact at the center. The iron-gall ink brown and legible and clear.

The ground had kept it better than any archive.

She thought about Nora's quilt — not the one on the guest bed, the one Nora kept folded on the cedar chest in her bedroom, the one she never put out for company. The pattern was called a bear's paw in the English quilting tradition, but Nora had always called it by the Piscataway name her grandmother had used, a word that meant something closer to *the track that shows where the animal has been.* The pattern had come from Nora's grandmother's grandmother, passed through women who had no reason to expect the state would preserve anything they made, who had therefore made things that did not require the state's preservation. Quilts. Songs. Stories told at fires and on deathbeds and in kitchens, the kind of transmission that left no paper trail and therefore could not be seized, could not be rerouted through an oversight structure, could not be lost in an institutional handoff between a program specialist and a compliance office.

Nora had kept the story of the bead for sixty-two years without telling a single institution.

Tayak had known about the mound her whole life — had picked watercress in the Mattawoman Creek bottom with her grandmother when she was eight, had stood at the field's edge and felt the ground's particular density without knowing what it was — and Nora had not told her what was in it until Tayak was ready to excavate it herself. Until the chain of custody would run directly from the ground to Tayak's hands, with no intermediary who might lose it or use it wrong.

That was not sentiment. That was strategy. It had always been strategy.

The question Tayak had not let herself finish in the truck was whether her resistance to submission was the same thing — the same instinct, the same calculation — or whether it was something smaller and less defensible. Whether she was protecting the document or protecting her own control over it. Whether the distinction mattered.

She did not know. She thought she might not know for a while.

A crow crossed the field from the tree line to the mound and landed on the western face, where the tractor damage had left a long diagonal compression in the soil. It walked two steps and stopped and looked at nothing in particular with the alert, assessing quality crows had. Then it lifted and was gone.

She stood up. Her knees were stiff from the cold and she put one hand on the trench wall to steady herself — felt the grit of the sandy loam against her palm, the faint chill of it, the particular roughness of undisturbed matrix that told her hands, before her eyes could confirm it, that nothing in this layer had been touched.

The light was going amber at the field's edge, the long October light that made everything look more permanent than it was. She picked up the trowel and felt its familiar weight — the handle worn smooth at the grip, the blade edge still sharp from the whetstone she'd used Monday morning. A Marshalltown, the same brand she'd used since graduate school, the kind of tool that became part of your hand after enough seasons.

She did not dig.

She understood, standing there, that she had already been making a decision without knowing she was making it. The field log said *document.* It did not say *petition.* It did not say *1697.* She had written *document* because *document* was what the field log required and because Dr. Marant had taught her that you logged what you found, not what you thought it meant, and you kept those two things separate until you were certain enough to defend the interpretation in print.

She was certain enough now. She had been certain since she'd read the date.

But she had not amended the log.

The unamended log was a choice. It kept the document's full significance out of the official record for a few more days, which kept it out of the Trust's monitoring system, which kept Slade from knowing what she had until she decided what to do with it.

She had done that without thinking. The way Okwi had done it without — she stopped herself. She didn't know what Okwi had thought. She only had what Nora had told her, and Nora had only what Ida had told her, and Ida had only what her grandmother had said before she lost the words. The chain was real but it was not a transcript. It was a shape, not a record.

Still. The shape was recognizable.

She covered the unit with the polyethylene sheeting, weighting the edges with the sandbags she kept stacked at the trench corner, and smoothed the plastic flat with both hands the way she always did — a habit from her first field season, when Dr. Marant had told her that the last thing you did at a site each day was the first thing the site would remember about you.

She climbed out of the trench.

The field was dark now, or nearly. The mound rose against the sky behind her, its low profile barely distinguishable from the surrounding ground — a slight thickening of the horizon, a held breath in the landscape. She had driven past mounds like this for years before she learned to see them. Now she could not stop seeing them.

She walked back toward the truck. The stubble crackled under her boots. Somewhere in the tree line a whippoorwill started up — the first she'd heard this season, late for October, calling anyway into the cooling dark as if the lateness of it was not its problem.

She got in the truck. She did not start the engine.

The field kit was locked in the equipment box in the truck bed. The document was inside it, in its archival sleeve, in its acid-free folder, in the dark. The log was on her laptop, unamended. The question was still open.

She thought about what Carver had told her in her second field year: *You do not get to want things faster than the ground can give them.* He had meant it as instruction about excavation technique. She had spent fifteen years understanding it as something else.

She started the engine. The headlights came on and swept across the mound's western face, and for one second the diagonal compression left by the tractor was visible — a long, pale scar across the darker soil — and then she turned the wheel and the light moved on and the mound went dark behind her.

She would amend the log tomorrow. She would write *petition, 1697, oilskin wrap, folio fold, see photo log 14-A through 14-F, see also field notes 14 October.* She would write what she had found and what she believed it was. She would log it correctly.

And then she would decide what to do with it.

She drove out through the gate and turned onto the county road and headed south, the headlights opening the dark ahead of her in a long, narrow corridor, and the mound behind her holding everything it held, and the question fully open, and the night coming down.
