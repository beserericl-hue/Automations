The mound was taller than he remembered.

That was the first thing Pell noticed, standing at its base in the grey light before the sun had cleared the eastern ridge. He had built it. Or rather, he had directed its building, had walked its circumference with a knotted cord, had driven stakes and stretched lines between them, had stood back and judged the profile against the treeline the way a man judges a wall for plumb. He knew this mound the way he knew the drainage channel behind the grain store, or the timber framing of the new storehouse: by its measurements, its angles, its relationship to the slope of the ground beneath. He had built it to last.

And yet this morning it looked taller.

The dew was heavy. His leather shoes had soaked through before he reached the mound's base, and he could feel the cold working up through the soles, settling in the bones of his feet. He had come out without his cloak. He had not thought to take it from the peg by the door. He had simply walked out of the house when the grey light came through the shutter-gap and he found he could not lie still any longer. Now the morning air pressed through his tunic, wool that smelled of the hearth and of sleep, and he stood with his arms at his sides and looked at what he had built.

Behind him, ten paces to the east, the earth was raw.

He had not looked at it directly yet. He was aware of it the way you are aware of a wound. Its location was precise in his body's knowledge. The looking-at-it was a separate act he had not yet chosen to perform. The soil there was darker than the surrounding ground, piled in a low ridge above the level of the field, still loose where the digging sticks had turned it yesterday. By midday it would begin to settle. By the end of the week, grass roots would start their slow work. By the next season you would not know it from the rest of the field unless you knew where to look.

He knew where to look. He would always know where to look.

Pell was a broad man, built close to the ground, with hands that had grown thick and scarred from forty years of rope and timber and stone. He was not given to standing still. Even in conversation he tended to shift his weight, to pick up whatever was nearest and turn it in his hands, to find the angle of a problem by moving around it. Essa, the woman from the eastern band who laughed easily and drew laughter from him in ways he had never managed on his own, had told him this for twenty-three years. *You think with your feet.* She had not been wrong.

But this morning he stood still.

The mound rose before him in the early light, its profile clean against the paling sky. He had seen to that, had shaped the crown deliberately, had tamped the surface in overlapping passes so that rain would shed outward rather than pool and soften the core. He understood mounds. He understood how water moved through soil, how the weight of added fill compacted the layers beneath, how a mound built too steep would slump and one built too shallow would spread and lose its form. He had spent three seasons on this one, directing the work, measuring, adjusting. Kessik had stood beside him through all of it, pointing to where she wanted the line to fall, telling him what the mound was for.

He had not, until this morning, understood what she meant.

*Not inside*, she had said. He could hear her voice precisely. She had a way of speaking that left no room for misunderstanding, each word placed like a stone in a wall. *I don't want to be inside it. I want to be near it. Do you understand the difference?*

He had said yes. He had thought he understood.

He looked at the raw earth behind him now. He made himself look.

She was there. Wrapped in woven plant fiber, the good cloth she had kept folded in the bark storage chest for eleven years, the cloth that smelled of the dried herbs she packed against moths. He had carried her himself. He had refused to let the younger men do it, had taken her weight across his arms and walked the ten paces from the mound's base to the place she had chosen, the place she had shown him two winters ago when she was still well enough to walk out here in the cold and point with one hand at the specific patch of ground. *There*, she had said. *That's where.* He had marked it in his memory the way he marked measurements: precisely, without sentiment, because precision was the only form of respect he knew how to give a thing.

He had dug the grave himself, too. Had sent the others away and dug it alone in the afternoon before the burial, the digging stick and his hands turning up dark topsoil and then the pale clay beneath, the contrast sharp and clean, the freshly opened earth releasing a damp, iron-rich smell that mingled with crushed leaf litter and the resin from nicked roots. His palms had blistered by the depth of two feet. He had kept digging. The physical work had been a mercy. Something to do with his hands. Something that required only his body and not his mind.

Now there was nothing to do with his hands.

He turned back to the mound and walked to its base and placed one palm flat against the turf. The grass was wet with dew. The soil beneath held the cold of the night, deep and steady, the way stored things hold their temperature. He pressed his hand harder, feeling the resistance of packed earth, the slight give of the turf layer over the firm fill beneath.

*The mound is not for the dead.*

He did not know where the thought came from. It arrived the way a measurement sometimes arrives: not worked out step by step, but simply there, the answer present before the working. He stood with his hand against the mound and the thought settled into him and he turned it over the way he turned a problem.

She had not wanted to be inside it. She had wanted to be beside it.

She had wanted to be where she could be visited.

The mound was for the living who came after. It was a place to come to. A place to stand and put your hand against and feel the cold of the stored earth and know that something had been kept here, that someone had thought it worth keeping, that the keeping itself was an answer to the question of whether any of this mattered. The dead inside the mound were not the point. The point was the mound. The point was the standing beside it.

He understood this now. He understood it the way he understood a well-made joint: not because someone had explained it, but because he had put his hands on it and felt how it held.

Behind him, the first smoke rose from the settlement's morning fires. He heard the distant sound of a child, one of his grandchildren probably. They were always first awake, spilling out of the house at first light with their small catastrophic energy, their breath clouding in the cool air. Essa would be up, stoking the hearth, heating the pot of grain porridge she made every morning without variation. The smell of it reached even out here when the wind was right.

He did not move.

The sun had not yet cleared the ridge, but the sky above the eastern hills had gone from grey to a pale, luminous blue, and the dew on the grass around the mound caught the growing light and held it in small bright points that shifted when the breeze moved through. He watched this. He was not a man who watched light on grass. He was a man who looked at the angle of a hill and calculated how the water would run off it in a heavy rain. But he watched this.

The raw earth behind him would settle. The grass would grow back. By the next season you would not know it from the rest of the field unless you knew where to look.

He would know.

He would tell his children. He would tell them what she had asked for and what she had meant by it, the difference between being inside the mound and being beside it. He would tell them the way he told them anything that mattered: plainly, without ornament, the way you state a measurement. He was not a storyteller. He knew this about himself. He had watched Kessik tell stories for thirty-one years and he had never been able to do what she did, never been able to find the shape of a thing in words the way she could, turning it so the light fell differently on each face of it.

But he could state a measurement. He could say: *she chose to be beside it, not inside it, and this is what that means.*

He could say it correctly. He could say it every time.

The sun broke the ridge. The light came across the field in a single long movement, gold and sudden, and the dew on the grass blazed for a moment before beginning to burn off, and the mound cast a shadow westward that was longer than he expected. Longer than he had calculated, which meant the crown was higher than his measurements had recorded, which meant the soil had settled less than he had predicted, which meant he had built it better than he knew.

He stood in the mound's shadow with his wet shoes and his hearth-smelling tunic and his blistered palms and he did not move.

Behind him, his granddaughter called his name.

He did not answer her right away.

Not from cruelty. He simply needed one more moment with the shadow, with the length of it across the wet grass, with the fact that he had built something that cast more shadow than he had calculated. He had spent thirty-one years calculating. He had been wrong in the right direction, which was the only kind of wrong a builder could afford.

His granddaughter called again. Litta, six years old, with her mother's voice already, that particular rising note at the end of a word that made everything sound like a question even when it was not. He turned.

She was standing at the edge of the field where the grass gave way to the packed earth of the settlement path, still in her sleeping tunic, a wool blanket pulled around her shoulders and trailing in the dew behind her. Her feet were bare. She had come out the moment she woke and not found him at the hearth and followed the path he always took in the morning, which was the path to the mound, which she knew because she had followed him before.

"Your feet," he said.

She looked down at them as though noticing them for the first time. "They're wet."

"Yes."

"Why are you standing there?"

He looked back at the mound. The shadow had shortened already. The sun was moving, as it always moved, indifferent to what it illuminated. In another hour the shadow would be gone and the mound would stand in full light, a low rise of turf and packed earth that a stranger might take for a natural feature of the field, a glacial hump, a collapsed bank. It was not a natural feature. He had built it. Kessik had told him what it should hold and where it should stand and how high it should rise above the river terrace, and he had built it the way she described, and then they had built it higher together over the years, layer by layer, each layer placed when someone new went into the earth beside it or below it, each layer a kind of answer to the accumulation of the dead.

"Come here," he said.

Litta crossed the wet grass on her bare feet without complaint. She was a child who accepted physical discomfort with the same equanimity she brought to everything, which was either a gift or something she had learned from watching him. He could not tell which. She stopped beside him and looked up at the mound. It was not much taller than she was. She had stood beside it before and not found it remarkable.

"Your grandmother chose to be buried there," he said. He pointed to the disturbed earth on the mound's south face, the raw soil dark against the older turf, already beginning to lose its freshness in the morning air. "Not inside. Beside it."

Litta looked at the raw earth. "Why?"

He had known she would ask this. She asked *why* about everything, with a persistence that was either going to make her formidable or exhausting, probably both. He had spent the last hour working out how to answer it.

"Because the mound is not for the dead," he said. "It is for the living who come back to it."

Litta considered this. The blanket slipped off one shoulder and she hitched it back up without looking away from the mound. "But dead people are inside it."

"Yes."

"So it is for the dead."

"The dead are kept there," he said. "That is not the same thing. A grain pit keeps grain. The grain is not the point. The point is that you can come back to it in winter and there will be something there. The point is the coming back."

She looked at the mound for a long moment. He watched her face. She had Kessik's habit of going very still when she was thinking, that particular quality of attention that made it seem as though the thinking was happening somewhere behind the eyes, in a room he could not see into. He had loved that quality in Kessik. He found it harder to love in a six-year-old because it made him feel that he was being evaluated.

"Who comes back?" Litta asked.

"We do," he said. "And after us, your children. And after them, theirs."

"What do they come back for?"

He looked at the mound. The dew had burned off the upper crown now, the grass there dry and ordinary in the morning light, but the lower slopes still held their brightness, small points of light shifting in the breeze off the river. He had stood at this mound in rain and in drought, in the cold of deep winter when the ground was iron-hard and the river ran grey under ice, and in the heat of midsummer when the air above the field shimmered and the turf smelled of baked earth and dry grass. He had stood here with Kessik and he had stood here alone. He had come because she came, and then because she had made him understand why it mattered, and then because he could not imagine a morning without it.

"To remember," he said. "To know that something was kept. To know that the keeping was worth doing."

Litta looked at the raw earth where her grandmother lay. Her face was serious in the way children's faces are serious when they are trying to decide whether what they have been told is true or only what an adult believes. He did not rush her. He had learned from Kessik that you could not rush the moment when a thing settled into someone. You could only say it clearly and wait.

"She's not inside," Litta said finally.

"No."

"She's beside it. So we can visit her."

"So we can visit all of them," he said. "She is the newest. The others go back further than I can count."

Litta turned to look at him. "How far?"

He thought of the copper pendant, of the woman whose name Kessik had spoken over the mound on the day they first broke ground, the name she said with a pause before the second syllable that changed its meaning. He thought of all the names Kessik had carried and taught him, the chain of them going back through the generations to that first woman, the one who had started it when the mound was only a small rise above the river terrace with the forest close on all sides. He had learned the names the way he learned measurements: by repetition, by accuracy, by the understanding that a wrong number in a calculation would cost you later. He had not thought of them as stories. He had thought of them as a record.

"Further than your grandmother could count," he said. "And she could count further than anyone."

The smoke from the settlement's fires had thickened now, and with it came the smell of the morning porridge. Dried maize and water, the same every morning, the smell of it as fixed in his understanding of the day as the angle of the light. Essa would be wondering where he was. The children would be awake. There would be the ordinary work of the morning, the ordinary work of the day, the ordinary work of all the days that came after a death, which was the hardest kind of ordinary work because it required you to do it while carrying the weight of the absence.

He had not yet said Kessik's name aloud since they had laid her in the earth. He had said it over her while they worked, the way she had taught him: her name, her mother's name, her mother's mother's name, the chain of them going back. But he had not said it to himself, for himself, as a man says the name of someone he has lost and will not stop losing.

He said it now, quietly, to the mound. Not to her. She was not inside it. She had been clear about that. She was beside it, in the earth beside it, where she could be visited. But the mound was where you came to say the names, and so he said it to the mound, and it was enough.

Litta did not ask what he was doing. She stood beside him with the blanket around her shoulders and her bare feet in the dew-wet grass and she was quiet, which was the right thing to be.

After a moment she slipped her hand into his. Her hand was small and cold from the morning air, and his palm was blistered from the digging, and the contact was uncomfortable in the specific way that mattered. It reminded you that you were present, that you were here, that the day was real and continuing.

He held her hand.

"She told me something," he said. "Before she went. I want to tell you what she told me."

Litta looked up at him.

"She said the mound is not a place for the dead to sleep." He looked at the mound's crown, where the light was now full and ordinary, the grass dry and green and unremarkable in the way that well-made things are unremarkable. They do not announce themselves. They simply hold. "She said it is a place for the living to return to. I did not understand her when she said it."

"Do you understand now?"

He looked at the shadow the mound cast: shorter now, the sun climbing, but still there, still measurable, still longer than he had calculated. He had built it better than he knew. He had built it with her, and she had known what they were building, and he had thought he knew and had not fully known until this morning, standing in the shadow of it with his blistered hands and his grief and his granddaughter's cold fingers in his.

"Yes," he said. "Now I do."

His other children came before the porridge had finished cooking.

He heard them on the path: his eldest son first, heavy tread unmistakable, then the lighter steps of Essa's two boys, then the sound of his youngest daughter calling Litta's name in the particular tone that meant she had been sent to find her rather than choosing to come herself. They came around the stand of oak in a loose group and stopped when they saw him still standing at the mound.

His eldest was twenty-three and built like Pell had been at that age, broad through the chest, with a quality of stillness in his face that he had somehow taken from Kessik's household without ever being her blood. He looked at the raw earth where Kessik lay and then at his father and did not speak. That was right. That was the son Pell knew.

His youngest daughter was eighteen and had Essa's way of filling a silence with movement. She went to Litta immediately, put an arm around her, looked at the mound, looked at Pell. "Your wife says the porridge will burn."

"Let it burn," Pell said.

She blinked. In twenty years of mornings he had never said that.

Essa's boys, ten and eight, hung back at the oak's edge, watching the way children watch when they understand that something large has happened but not yet what it means.

Pell looked at all of them. He looked at the mound. He thought of Kessik's voice, patient and unhurried, the way she had told him the names the first time and the second time and the tenth time until he had them without effort, the way a good measurement becomes part of the hand that takes it.

He was not a storyteller. He knew this about himself the way he knew the weight of a stone and the angle at which a wall would hold or fail. He had never told a story in his life that he had not been required to tell. He built things. He measured things. He was good at knowing what would stand.

But Kessik was gone, and she had been the one who told the stories, and there was no one else.

"Come here," he said. "All of you. Sit down."

They sat in the grass before the mound the way they had sat at fires: his eldest cross-legged, his youngest daughter pulling Litta into her lap, the boys dropping onto their haunches with the boneless ease of children. Pell remained standing. He did not know how to begin sitting down for this.

He began badly.

"The mound was built in layers," he said. "The first course was river stone and packed clay. You need a stable base or the whole thing shifts in wet seasons, the water gets underneath and heaves it. We dug down two feet first to find solid ground. That took four days. Then the stone course, which took, "

"Father," his eldest said.

Pell stopped.

"Who is *we*?" his son said.

Pell looked at him. The word had come out before he had thought about it, and now it hung in the air between them, and he understood what he had done wrong. He had begun with the building. He had begun with the part he understood.

He started again.

"Your grandmother came to me when I was younger than you," he said to his eldest. "I was building the drainage channel on the north field, the one that still runs, you know the one, and she came and stood at the edge of it and watched me work. She watched for a long time without speaking. I thought she wanted something repaired." He paused. The memory of it was so clear he could smell the turned earth, the particular dark smell of the subsoil where the clay gave way to gravel. "She said: *I need someone who understands how things are built to last.* I said I could fix whatever needed fixing. She said: *It is not fixing I need.*"

His youngest daughter had gone still. Even the boys were watching him now.

"She took me to this place," he said. "There was a rise here. Not much, knee height, maybe. Grass-covered. Old. You could see it had been here a long time because the grass on it was different from the grass around it, thicker, the roots going down deep. She told me what it was." He stopped again, because he was getting ahead of himself, because the story wanted to go in the order it had happened but he kept pulling toward the structure, the engineering, the part that made sense to him. He made himself slow down. He thought of Kessik's voice saying *say the names in order, always in order, or the chain breaks*. "She told me who was in it. She said the names. She said them from the newest to the oldest: her mother, her mother's mother, back and back, all the way to the first woman, the one who started it. The one who buried the copper pendant." He touched the mound's side without thinking, the way you touch a wall you have built to check that it is solid. The grass was still wet with dew. "She said the pendant had come from far away, from the lake country in the north, carried down by a trader. A woman received it. She wore it. She passed it on. And when her daughter died, " He stopped. The word caught on something in his chest. He had not expected that. He had known the story for years, had said it many times in his own head, but saying it aloud now, the morning after laying Kessik in the earth, was different. He waited a moment. "When her daughter died, she buried the pendant in the mound. And the mound was the place after that. The place you came back to. The place where the names were kept."

He told them the rest of it: Kessik's vision, the way she had come to him, the years of building, the layers, the names she had taught him to say over each course of earth. He told it in the wrong order twice. He skipped the part about the names and then had to go back to it. He spent too long on the drainage problem in the second year of building and had to be recalled to himself by the look on his youngest daughter's face, which was the look of someone waiting for the thing that matters.

When he finished, the sun was well up and the shadow of the mound had shortened to almost nothing.

Litta, still in her aunt's lap, said: "Tell it again."

Pell looked at her.

"You left out Grandmother," she said. "You told us about the building and the names and the woman with the pendant. But you didn't tell us about Grandmother. What she was like when she came to you. What she said the first time she showed you the mound."

He stood with the morning light full on him and understood that his granddaughter was right. He had told the story the way he built things: foundation first, then structure, then surface. He had buried Kessik in the middle of it the way you bury a cornerstone: essential, invisible, load-bearing, and completely hidden.

He sat down.

The boys shifted to make room for him without being asked. He sat in the grass before the mound with his blistered hands on his knees and his wool tunic damp at the hem from the dew, and he began again.

This time he began with Kessik.

He told them how she had stood at the edge of the drainage channel with her hands loose at her sides and her eyes on him with the particular quality of attention she gave to things she had already decided about. He told them the way she had spoken: not asking, not commanding, but stating, the way you state a fact about the ground. *This is clay here, you will need to go deeper.* He told them what she had looked like in the early years of the building, when she was still strong, when she would climb the mound's side to check his work and stand at the crown with her arms out like a man checking a wall's plumb. He told them what she had looked like in the last years, when the climbing was too much and she would stand at the base and look up and say nothing, which was its own kind of checking.

He told them about the names. This time he said them in order, the way she had taught him, and his voice did not catch because he was not thinking about his own grief. He was thinking about the chain, about the accuracy of it, about the way a wrong number in a calculation would cost you later. He said the names clearly, from the oldest to the newest, and when he came to Kessik's name he said it without stopping.

He told them what she had said to him the night before she died. He told them what he had understood from it that morning, standing in the shadow of the mound with Litta's cold hand in his.

"She said the mound is not for the dead," he said. "She said it is for us. For the ones who are still here. So we know where to come when we need to remember that we are not the first ones, and we will not be the last."

The boys were very quiet. The older one was looking at the mound with the focused expression of someone filing a thing away.

His eldest said nothing for a long time. Then: "The names. Will you teach them to us?"

"Yes," Pell said. "That is why I am telling you this."

"All of them?" his youngest daughter said.

"All of them. From the woman at the bottom to your grandmother." He looked at Litta. "And someday, Kessik's name will not be the newest. Someone will add it to the chain and say it before the name that comes after. That is what the chain is. That is how it holds."

Litta had been very still through the second telling. Now she said, with the careful precision of a child who has been listening to every word: "Who will say your name?"

He had not thought about this. He thought about it now, sitting in the grass with the sun warm on his face and the smell of the porridge drifting from the settlement: burned, as it turned out, the smell of it slightly sharp and dark, which he did not mind. He had built the mound. He had learned the names. He would teach them to his children and his grandchildren and whoever came after, and one of them would say his name when the time came, and one of them would say it after that.

"One of you," he said. "Whichever of you holds it best."

He looked at Litta when he said it. She was watching him with Kessik's eyes: the same quality of attention, the same sense of a thing already decided.

He had not known, until that moment, that he was looking at the next keeper.

The first winter after Kessik's burial, Pell told the story three times.

Once at the fire of his neighbor Brus, when Brus's wife asked why the mound stood where it did and not closer to the settlement. Pell set down his cup, rough clay with a handle cracked and repaired with pine pitch, and told it without preparation, standing at the edge of the firelight with his arms at his sides. He told it badly. He skipped the names entirely and had to go back, and he lost the thread of the pendant's history twice and had to retrace it. Brus's wife listened with the patient expression of someone waiting for the useful part. When he finished, she said, "So it is a grave-mound." "No," Pell said. "It is not for the dead. The dead are already gone." She looked at him. "Then what is it for?" "For us," he said. "For knowing where we come from. For knowing we are not the first ones." She thought about this. "Then it is a kind of memory," she said. "Yes," Pell said. "That is it exactly."

He walked home in the cold thinking that she had understood it faster than he had.

The second time was at his own hearth, to his eldest son, who had just turned twenty-four and had begun asking the kind of questions that come when a man starts to understand that the world existed before he arrived in it. His son sat on the low bench with his elbows on his knees and listened without interrupting, which was unusual. When Pell finished, his son said, "Do you believe the woman at the bottom of the chain was real? Not the names. Do you believe the first one was a real woman who lived and made the choice?" Pell said, "Yes." "How do you know?" Pell thought about this. "Because someone remembered her. You don't remember something that was never there." His son was quiet for a long time. Then: "All right."

The third time was at the deathbed of old Simus, who had helped lay the mound's outer skin of river stones in the third year of the building and who had never asked what it was for. He lay in his wool blankets with his breath coming slow and his eyes still clear, and he said to Pell, "Tell me about the thing we built." So Pell told him. He told it better this time. The names came in order, the pendant's history held its sequence, and when he reached Kessik's name he said it without hesitation. Simus listened with his eyes half-closed and his hands flat on the blanket. When Pell finished, Simus said, "I knew it was for something. I could feel it in the work." He died four days later, and Pell stood at his grave in the cold morning air and understood that the telling had been a gift. Not from Pell to Simus, but from the mound itself, through Pell, to a man who had spent three years carrying stone and never known why.

After that, Pell stopped thinking of the story as something he owned. He told it when it was needed. He did not embellish it. He did not shorten it for the sake of the listener's patience. He told it the way he had learned to read a drainage problem: from the source, following the water's path, arriving at the place where the ground told you what had to be done.

He was not a storyteller. Everyone who heard him knew this. His voice had no music in it. He did not vary his pace for effect or pause at the moments of highest feeling to let the silence do the work. He told the story the way he laid stone: each piece placed correctly before the next was set, no piece moved once it was down. When he reached the part about Kessik standing at the mound's base in the last years, looking up without speaking, he did not dwell on it. He stated it and moved on, because the chain required accuracy, not grief. The grief was his own business.

But something happened to his listeners when he told it. Not always. Sometimes a man would nod politely and go back to his work. But often enough that Pell noticed. A stillness came over people. The kind of stillness that is not passivity but attention. He had seen it in Litta that first morning after the burial, and he saw it again and again in the years that followed: a woman pausing at her loom with her hands resting on the warp threads; a young man setting down his adze and looking at the mound from across the field; an elder who had lived at the settlement her whole life saying, for the first time, "I did not know the names went back so far."

The names were the center of it. He understood this gradually. People could accept a mound as a landmark, a grave-rise, a feature of the landscape. What they could not easily accept, what stopped them and made them still, was the chain. The idea that the woman at the bottom had been real, had made a choice, had been remembered by someone who was remembered by someone who was remembered until the memory arrived here, at this fire, in this man's imperfect telling. The chain made the distance crossable. It made the past not past.

He taught the names to his eldest son first, then to the older of Essa's boys, then to his youngest daughter, then to Litta, who already knew them from the morning of the first telling and corrected him once when he reversed the order of two middle names. He did not mind being corrected. He corrected himself in the same flat tone and continued.

He taught the names at planting time, when the children were old enough to follow him to the field and he could talk while he worked, his voice level over the sound of the hoe breaking the soil. He taught them at the births of grandchildren, because a birth was a good time to say the names. The new one needed to know the chain it was entering. He taught them at the deaths of neighbors, for the same reason Simus had wanted to hear the story: because a death clarified what was worth holding.

He added names as they came. Essa, the woman from the eastern band who had laughed easily and drawn laughter from him, died in the late summer of the twenty-second year after Kessik's burial, after a short illness that moved faster than anyone expected. He buried her near the mound, as she had asked. Not in it, but near it, in the earth, in the way Kessik had chosen. He stood at the grave with the smell of turned clay sharp in the morning air and the standing corn tall in the field behind him, and he added her name to the end of the chain, after Kessik's. He said both names aloud. Then he went back to the settlement and told his children and grandchildren that the chain was longer now.

He was not a man who wept easily. He had wept the morning of Kessik's burial, and he had wept the morning of Essa's, and those were the two mornings he permitted himself. After that he worked. He repaired the mound's eastern face where a wet winter had carved a small gully in the soil, packing new clay into the gap and smoothing it with his palm until the profile was clean. He walked the mound's perimeter as Kessik had taught him, counting paces, checking the grade, noting where the grass grew thick and where it thinned. He was past sixty by then, and the walking hurt his knees in the cold months, but he did it anyway because the mound required maintenance the way a drainage channel required maintenance: not because anything was wrong, but because neglect was how things failed.

In the thirty-first year after Kessik's burial, his grandson, the older of Essa's boys, now twenty-four, broad in the shoulders, with a way of standing very still that was Kessik's way though he had never known her, came to him at the mound on a still autumn morning and said, "I want to know the full history. Not just the names. The history of how each name was added. Who taught whom. How the chain was kept." Pell looked at him. "That will take several mornings," Pell said. "I know," the young man said. "I have several mornings." So they sat at the mound's base in the autumn light, with the oak leaves coming down in the still air and the smell of woodsmoke from the settlement drifting south, and Pell told it all. Not just the names: the whole structure. How Kessik had learned the chain from the woman before her, and that woman from the one before, back to the woman at the bottom who had buried the pendant and spoken the first name into the ground. How the chain had thinned in some generations and been held carefully in others. How the mound itself was a kind of memory made solid, earth and stone and time, so that the chain had somewhere to live that was not only inside a person's head, where it could be lost.

The young man listened without interrupting. When Pell finished, he said, "You are afraid the chain will break." Pell considered this. "I am afraid the chain will thin," he said. "The way a cord thins when you pull it too tight over too many years. It does not break all at once. It frays." The young man looked at the mound. "Then I will hold it," he said. "And teach my children to hold it." "Yes," Pell said. "That is the work."

He told the story for the last time in the thirty-seventh year after Kessik's burial. He was past seventy, and his hands had lost their steadiness, and his voice had roughened to something closer to a rasp than a speaking voice, but his memory was still clear. He told it at the fire of his youngest daughter's household, where her children and grandchildren sat in the firelight with their eyes on him. He told it from the beginning, the way he always did: the woman at the bottom of the chain, the pendant, the names in order. He said Kessik's name. He said Essa's name. He said the names of the ones he had added in the years between, the neighbors and kin who had died near the mound and asked to be remembered in the chain.

When he finished, the youngest child, a girl of perhaps five, sitting close to the fire with her knees drawn up, said, "Is the mound still there?" "Yes," Pell said. "It will be there after I am gone. After you are gone. That is why we built it." "Who will take care of it?" He thought of his grandson, who already walked the perimeter in the mornings. He thought of Litta, who knew the names in perfect order and had corrected him only once in thirty-seven years, which was a better record than his own. "The ones who come after," he said. "The ones who learn the names. That is all the care it needs." The girl looked at the fire. "What if they forget?" It was the question he had been asking himself for thirty-seven years. He had not found a better answer than the one Kessik had given him, so he gave her that one. "That is why we tell it," he said. "Every time. Correctly. So it is harder to forget than to remember."

He died the following spring, before the first planting, in the cool grey light of an early morning with the shutters open and the smell of the fields coming in: damp earth and new growth and the faint sweetness of the flowering hawthorn along the field's edge. Litta was with him. She held his hand, which was still large and rough and permanently marked by the work of his life, and she said the names in order, all of them, from the woman at the bottom to Kessik to Essa to the ones added after. When she reached the end, she paused. Then she said his name. She said it clearly, the way he had taught her to say all the names: without hesitation, without ornament, as a fact. He heard it. She was certain of this. Then she added it to the chain, where it would stay.

The morning Pell brought his great-granddaughter to the mound, she was seven years old and had not yet learned to be afraid of silence.

He had watched her for two years, deciding. She had Kessik's way of going still when she was thinking: not the stillness of a child who had stopped paying attention, but the stillness of a child who was paying attention more completely than usual. She asked questions that arrived at the center of a thing without circling it first. When her older brother dropped a clay bowl and it shattered and everyone else flinched, she looked at the pieces on the floor with an expression of mild interest, as though she were cataloguing what she had learned.

He had not told anyone he was bringing her. He woke before first light, dressed in the dark, tunic of undyed wool, the old cloak pinned at his shoulder with the bone pin he had carried since his middle years, and went to the sleeping room where his youngest daughter's children lay on their straw-filled pallets under wool blankets that smelled of the hearth. He touched the girl's shoulder. She was awake before his hand landed.

They walked in the blue-grey light before the sun cleared the hills, the dew soaking the hem of Pell's tunic, the girl's small feet in her leather shoes making almost no sound on the wet grass. His own shoes were heavy-soled and loud. He had always been loud, in motion. Kessik had teased him for it.

The mound stood where it always stood, darker than the surrounding ground in the early light, its profile unchanged from the morning he had stood here in his grief and understood what she had meant. The oak at its northern face had grown taller in the years since. The grass on the mound's southern slope was thick and close and still beaded with moisture.

The girl stood beside him and looked at it.

"You know what this is," he said.

"The mound," she said. "Where the dead are remembered."

"Not where the dead are. Where the living come to remember them. There is a difference. Your great-grandmother's teacher taught me the difference."

She looked at him. She had Kessik's quality of attention behind her eyes: not the color, which was her mother's, but the sense that she was taking a measurement.

"I am going to tell you something," Pell said. "And then you are going to tell it back to me. And we will do this until you have it correctly. Do you understand?"

"Yes," she said.

He sat down on the mound's southern face, his old knees protesting the descent, and she sat beside him without being asked. The dew soaked through his cloak immediately. He did not move.

"There is a chain," he said. "Not a chain you can hold. A chain made of names and what each name means. It goes back further than I can see. I will tell you as far back as I know, and you will hold what I give you, and one day you will give it to someone else."

She said nothing. She was listening the way he had learned, over a long life, that very few people listened: with her whole body turned toward him, her hands flat on her knees.

"The woman at the bottom," he said. "She is the beginning. She buried something in this mound a long time ago, something she had carried and could not carry anymore, and she spoke a name over the earth. That name is the first name in the chain."

He said the name. He said it with the pause before the second syllable, the way Kessik had taught him, the pause that changed it from a common word to something else.

"Say it," he said.

The girl said it. She got the pause wrong.

"Again."

She said it again. The pause was closer.

"Again."

The third time she had it. He felt something loosen in his chest.

They went through it that way: name by name, each one said and repeated and said again until she had it right. The sun came up while they worked, the light moving from blue-grey to gold, the dew burning off the grass around them. A crow called from the oak. The smell of woodsmoke reached them from the settlement, meaning the morning fires were lit, meaning his daughter was awake and would notice the sleeping room was short by one.

He did not hurry.

When they reached Kessik's name he paused longer than usual.

"This one," he said, "is the one who taught me. She was not my blood. She was my teacher. She built this mound with me, not just the earth of it, the shape of it, but the meaning of it. When she died I understood for the first time what the mound was for."

"What is it for?" the girl asked.

He had been answering this question for thirty-seven years and the answer had not changed. "The dead do not need it," he said. "The dead are past needing. The mound is for the ones who come after. So they know they are not the first. So they know the ground they stand on has been stood on before, by people who also loved and also grieved and also built things they hoped would last." He looked at the southern face of the mound, at the grass that had grown over the place where he and his grandson had laid Kessik into the earth: not in the mound, as she had asked, but near it, in the ground beside it, so that she was part of the land the mound stood on. "When you know that," he said, "you are less afraid."

The girl considered this with the seriousness she brought to everything. "Were you afraid?"

"Yes," he said. "I was afraid she would be forgotten. I was afraid the chain would break and all the names would be lost and it would be as though none of them had lived." He looked at the child beside him. "That is why I am telling you."

She nodded once, the way a grown person nods when they have understood something that cost the teller something to say.

They went through the names again from the beginning. She missed one, a name from the middle of the chain, an older woman whose name had a difficult consonant cluster, and he corrected her without impatience, and she said it again, and again, until she had it.

When she reached the end of the chain she stopped and looked at him.

"Is that all of them?"

"All that I know," he said. "There may be names before the first one I carry. The chain goes back further than anyone living can see. But you hold what I give you, and the one who comes after you will hold what you give them, and that is how it works."

He got to his feet slowly, his knees aching, and stood looking at the mound in the full morning light. The oak cast a long shadow northward. The grass on the southern face was bright with the last of the dew.

He reached down and took the girl's hand, small and warm in his large, rough one, and placed it flat against the mound's southern face, palm down, fingers spread, the way you would press your hand to a wall to feel whether it was sound.

The earth was cool and slightly damp under her palm. She did not pull away.

He held her hand there for a moment, feeling the mound beneath both their hands: the solidity of it, the weight of what it held, the long patience of earth that had been shaped by people and had outlasted them and would outlast him too, and this girl, and her children, and their children's children going forward into a future he could not imagine but that he trusted, in the way he trusted the drainage channels he had built to carry water in the right direction long after he was gone.

Then he let go.

She kept her hand there a moment longer, on her own, and he did not tell her to move it.

He did not know, standing in the morning light with the smell of woodsmoke and wet grass around him and the crow calling again from the oak, that the hand he had just guided to the mound's face would guide other hands in turn, that the names he had spent the morning pressing into her memory would travel forward through generations he could not count, carried in voices he would never hear, until they reached a woman who would stand in a trench cut into this same earth and understand, from a chain of spoken words two thousand years long, that she was home.

He only knew that Kessik had asked him to remember, and that he had remembered, and that now the remembering would go on without him.

That was enough. That was, he thought, exactly enough.

He turned south toward the settlement, toward the smell of the morning fires and his daughter's household and the day's work waiting. After a moment, the girl fell into step beside him. Her hand found his again: not to be led, but the way a person reaches for something solid when they are carrying something new and do not yet know its weight.

He held it.

They walked home through the bright morning, and the mound stood behind them, and the chain held.
