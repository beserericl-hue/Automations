The sky above the tree line was the color of old iron when Tayak parked the truck on the gravel apron and killed the headlights. Five-seventeen. She sat for a moment with the engine ticking and watched the field.

Nothing moved.

She had been telling herself since La Plata that the feeling in her chest was not fear. It was not. It was the particular sharpening that came before work that mattered — the same tightening she'd felt in Georgia the morning she opened a burial pit and found a woman interred with her hands folded over a ceramic bowl that still held the ghost of red ochre. That had been good work. This was good work. The feeling was the same.

She got out of the truck and stood in the cold.

The field smelled of turned earth and something older underneath — the sweet rot of the tidal flat a mile east, carried on a wind that had been moving across this watershed for longer than the tobacco fields had existed. Dawes's soybean stubble ran away to the south in pale rows, catching the first grey light. The mound rose from the center of the field the way it always did, patient and unremarkable, a low shoulder of earth that most people would have driven past their whole lives without registering.

Slade was monitoring. That was the fact she had carried here in the dark.

She'd gotten the call from Marcus Redcloud at nine-fifteen the previous night — short, careful, the way Marcus talked when he thought someone might be keeping records. *The reporter's inquiry went to his office. He's been asking questions about the permit review timeline.* A pause. *He's not hostile yet. But he's watching.* She had said thank you and hung up and stood in her kitchen for a while looking at the permit binder on the counter.

Slade's name appeared on the permit in two places. He had the authority to trigger a mandatory review. If he triggered a review, the excavation would freeze — not for days, for months, possibly for the full forty-eight days remaining before Garrett Wills's bulldozers arrived at the Route 301 connector. Freeze meant loss. Loss meant the mound went under highway fill and the petition went to the OFA without the stratum evidence and the tribe spent another decade filing paper into a silence that never answered.

She lifted her field kit from the truck bed and walked toward the mound.

The gate padlock was open as Dawes had left it. The chain looped through the post in a loose figure eight, and she paused to re-fasten it behind her — not locked, but closed. A small thing. A thing that said: this site is attended.

The trench was where she had left it, covered with the orange safety mesh and weighted at the corners with half-bricks she'd carried from the truck three days ago. She pulled the mesh back and clipped on her headlamp and climbed down.

The trench was ninety centimeters wide and currently sixty deep in the southwest corner, where she'd been working the most promising profile. The walls were clean — she had spent two evenings shaving them back with a flat-edged trowel until the stratigraphy read clearly: topsoil, disturbed plow zone, a thin lens of orange-brown subsoil, and then the darker fill of the mound's deliberate construction layer. She had drawn the profile three times. She could close her eyes and see it.

She knelt on the kneeling pad and picked up her trowel.

The work was slow in the cold. Her fingers had not yet warmed and the trowel handle was damp from the morning air. She worked the construction layer with short strokes, pulling the loosened soil toward her and transferring it to the screen bucket. The layer was dense — compacted over centuries, threaded through with root ghosts and the occasional fleck of charcoal that she bagged without stopping. The charcoal would date. Everything would date, eventually.

She was thinking about Slade. She was thinking about what Marcus Redcloud had said — *not hostile yet* — and what that qualifier meant, and whether Slade had already made a phone call to Voss, and whether Voss's name in the permit binder was a resource or a liability. She was thinking about this and not about the trowel, and so when the trowel struck something at forty centimeters below the construction layer's upper surface, the sound surprised her.

Not the dull thud of a stone. Not the scrape of a root. A different resistance — something that gave slightly under pressure and then held, a give-and-hold that she had felt before in a river-bottom site in Virginia and once in a tobacco barn site in South Carolina. Organic. Compressed. Not soil.

She stopped.

She set the trowel down on the edge of the screen bucket and picked up the brush — a two-inch horsehair, soft enough for shell, stiff enough for compacted clay — and began to work.

The soil came away in thin layers. She was breathing through her nose, slow and deliberate, the way Cecile Marant had taught her in the Howard field methods lab: *when you feel something, slow down, slow down, slow down.* The headlamp threw a hard circle of light on the floor of the trench and she moved her face close to the work and brushed.

An edge emerged.

Dark. Folded. The surface had a faint sheen that was not mineral — not mica, not wet clay — but the particular dull gleam of something that had been treated. She brushed another centimeter clear and the edge resolved into a fold line, and then another fold line parallel to the first, and the shape of the thing became plain: a packet. Rectangular. Perhaps fifteen centimeters on the longer axis, perhaps ten on the shorter. Folded once lengthwise and once across, and the outer surface sealed at the seam with something dark and resinous that had hardened to a crust over the pitch-black of the surrounding soil.

Pine pitch. The smell reached her now — faint, almost gone after three centuries, but there. Pine and something underneath it, animal fat or beeswax, the kind of layered waterproofing that a person used when they needed a thing to survive.

Oilskin.

The word arrived in her mind with a clarity that made her sit back on her heels.

She did not touch it.

She looked at it for a long moment — the folded packet, intact, the pitch seal unbroken, the surface dark with soil and time — and she felt the hope rise in her chest like a physical thing, warm and dangerous. *This is it.* The thought was immediate and certain and she did not trust it. She had felt certainty before in the field and been wrong. She had also felt certainty and been right, and the difference between those two moments was not the feeling itself but what she did next.

What she did next mattered.

She was alone in the trench. Five forty-one in the morning, by her watch. The sky above the trench walls had gone from iron to pewter. Elspeth was not here. Wren Calvert was not here. There was no one in the field, no one at the gate, no one who could stand beside her and say: *I was present, I saw the context, I saw the depth, I saw the seal intact.* No one who could testify to the chain of custody that Slade — or any attorney Slade chose to direct — would require before a court or a review board or the OFA would accept what this packet might contain.

She could photograph it. She had her phone and her field camera both. She could photograph the context and the depth and the seal and the fold lines and the pitch, and the photographs would be time-stamped and GPS-tagged and entered into the field log with the stratum notation and the soil description and the compass bearing and all the rest of it. That was procedure. That was what the procedure was for.

But a photograph was not a witness.

And Slade was watching.

She reached for her phone and stopped with her hand in the air, not touching anything. The packet lay in the floor of the trench, half-exposed, the pine pitch seal catching the headlamp's light. Three hundred and twenty-seven years in the ground, give or take, if the stratum depth was what she thought it was. Someone had wrapped this in oilskin and sealed it with pitch and buried it in the construction layer of a mound that was already old when they did it. Someone had wanted it to survive.

It had survived.

And she was alone, and Slade was watching, and her trowel was resting on the edge of the screen bucket, and the sky was getting lighter.

She picked up the phone.

She started with the overview shot — trench wall, scale bar propped against the south face, the packet visible at the bottom of the frame as a small dark rectangle in the pale clay. Then she moved closer. Closer again. She worked the angles the way Cecile had drilled into her: north-south axis, east-west axis, oblique from the upper left, oblique from the upper right, straight down with the phone braced against both trench walls so her shadow fell outside the frame. She photographed the pitch seal from three centimeters away. She photographed the fold lines. She photographed the junction of the packet's lower edge with the undisturbed clay beneath it, where the soil had compressed around the thing over centuries and left a faint impression — a ghost outline that would disappear the moment she lifted it. She photographed that twice.

Seventeen photographs. She counted them in the camera roll and then called Wren Calvert.

It rang four times. Tayak was watching the packet while it rang, as if it might move.

"Yeah." Wren's voice was thick with sleep.

"How far out are you?"

A pause. The sound of sheets. "Twenty minutes. Maybe fifteen if — what happened?"

"I found something in the construction layer. Forty centimeters below the upper surface."

Another pause, different from the first. "What kind of something?"

"Oilskin packet. Pitch seal intact. Rectangular, about fifteen by ten centimeters. I've photographed it in context. I need a witness before I go further."

Wren said, "I'm getting up right now."

"Drive carefully."

"I'm getting up *right now*."

Tayak ended the call and put the phone in her jacket pocket. She settled back on her heels, looked at the packet, and then — because there was nothing else to do and she had learned long ago that forcing field work made you sloppy — she sat down on the trench floor with her back against the north wall and her knees drawn up and the packet between her feet and she waited.

The light changed while she waited. That was the only way to describe it: it changed. The iron-gray above the trench walls went to pewter and then to something that was not quite color yet but was no longer absence of color, the particular pre-dawn quality of southern Maryland in October when the sky held the night's cold like a debt it was not ready to pay. The soybean field beyond the trench was invisible. She could hear it — a faint rustle of dry stalks in the wind off the creek — but the trench walls cut her sight to a rectangle of sky and a square of pale clay and the packet.

She put her hands in her lap and kept them there.

She had worked sites where the most important thing she had ever found was a scatter of fire-cracked rock. She had worked sites where a single post mold had redrawn a published map. She had learned not to build the story before she had the evidence, because the story you built in the field, in the dark, alone, was always the story you wanted and not necessarily the story the ground was telling. She knew this. She had taught it.

She looked at the packet.

The pitch seal was unbroken. That was real. The fold lines were deliberate — not a scrap of material crumpled and discarded but something folded with intention, folded the way you folded a thing you meant to keep. That was real. The depth was real, the stratum was real, the faint smell of pine and beeswax that had reached her when she first brushed it clear was real, though it was already fading in the open air and by the time she wrote the field notes it would be a memory rather than a datum.

She heard Dawes's cattle somewhere to the north, a low, indifferent sound. Then silence. Then the dry stalks again.

She did not touch the packet.

Wren's truck came down the field road at seventeen minutes, headlights cutting across the furrows, and Tayak heard the door and then boots on hard ground and then Wren Calvert's face appeared at the trench edge, hair loose, green canvas jacket half-zipped, the torn left pocket catching on the zipper tab.

"Show me."

Tayak pointed.

Wren looked for a long moment without speaking. Then she climbed down into the trench and crouched beside Tayak and looked at the packet from the same angle Tayak had been using, the headlamp throwing both their shadows up the south wall.

"Pitch seal," Wren said.

"Yes."

"That's not accidental deposition."

"No."

Wren reached into her jacket and pulled out her own phone. "Can I —"

"Go ahead."

Wren photographed it. She was methodical — Tayak had trained her and it showed — working the same angles without being told, adding a second scale bar she pulled from her jacket pocket and propped against the east face. When she was done she looked at the time stamp and read it aloud for the record, and Tayak noted it in the field log: *0602. W. Calvert present. Context documented. Proceeding to recovery.*

Tayak picked up the bamboo spatula.

The spatula was a tool she had made herself, years ago, from a bamboo garden stake split lengthwise and sanded smooth — thin enough to work under fragile edges without cutting, stiff enough to lever gently. Cecile had used one exactly like it. Tayak had never found a commercial substitute she trusted.

She worked the lower edge first, sliding the spatula into the gap between the packet and the compressed clay beneath it. The clay gave a little. The packet did not move yet — it was seated in its own impression, three centuries of soil pressure having shaped the ground around it — and she worked the spatula along the full lower edge before trying to lift, feeling for the resistance that would tell her something was still attached below.

Nothing attached. The impression was a cradle, not a grip.

She worked the spatula to the left edge. Then the right. The pitch seal cracked slightly along one corner as the packet shifted — a sound like a dry twig, very small — and Wren made a sound in her throat.

"It's the seal cracking, not the packet," Tayak said. "The oilskin underneath is still intact."

She could see that now: beneath the hardened pitch, the surface of the packet had a slight give to it, the particular resilience of treated cloth that had survived because someone had done the waterproofing well and the soil had been anaerobic and the mound's clay cap had held. Someone three hundred years ago had wanted this to last. They had gotten their wish.

Tayak lifted the packet.

It came free of its impression with a soft sound — the sound of a seal breaking after a very long time — and she held it in both palms and felt its weight. Light. Perhaps the weight of three or four folded sheets of rag paper. Perhaps less. The outer surface was dark with soil and the old pitch, but the fold lines were clear: folded once lengthwise, once across, and then once more in each direction, so that the packet was perhaps a quarter of the original sheet's size. Four folds. The outer surface was illegible — dark, mottled, the surface fibers compressed and stained — but the packet had not collapsed, had not crumbled, had not come apart in her hands.

Wren had the evidence bag open.

Tayak set the packet inside without turning it over, without trying to open it, without doing anything except placing it flat in the center of the bag so the fold lines would not be stressed further. Wren sealed the bag. Tayak wrote the log number on the label in her own hand — *18CH-447-L14-002* — and Wren held the bag while Tayak photographed the label and the bag and the bag's contents visible through the plastic, the dark rectangle of the packet against the clear interior.

Then Tayak sat back on her heels and looked at the impression the packet had left in the clay floor of the trench. The ghost outline. It was already beginning to blur at the edges as the exposed clay dried in the morning air.

She photographed that too.

"Log entry," she said, and Wren had the field notebook open. "Stratum one, construction layer, forty centimeters below upper surface. Rectangular organic packet, approximately fifteen by ten centimeters, folded oilskin or treated cloth with hardened pitch seal at primary fold. Recovered intact. Outer surface illegible. Interior contents unknown. Condition: fragile, anaerobic storage, no visible insect damage or mold on exterior. Requires UV examination under controlled conditions before opening."

Wren wrote it in her small, careful hand without looking up.

"Time of recovery," Tayak said.

"Zero-six-fourteen."

"Zero-six-fourteen." Tayak looked at the evidence bag in Wren's hands. The packet inside it was dark and still and gave nothing away. "We take it to the lab today."

Wren looked up. "Voss's lab or the conservation suite?"

It was a real question. Voss's lab was faster, better equipped, and Voss would know about it by noon. The conservation suite was slower, more careful, and reported to the department's collections manager rather than directly to the chair.

"Conservation suite," Tayak said. "Call ahead. Tell them we need UV access this afternoon."

Wren nodded and reached for her phone.

Tayak climbed out of the trench and stood in the field and looked at the soybean stubble and the pale sky and the dark tree line along the creek, and she thought about the pitch seal and the four folds and the weight of the thing in her hands.

This was the second artifact from this layer. The cobalt bead — 18CH-447-L14-001, logged and transferred to Adaeze Okonkwo in College Park on the fourteenth of September — had come out of the same stratum eleven days ago. The bead had been the first concrete evidence of legal significance. This packet was the second. She did not yet know what the second would say.

She called Marcus Redcloud.

He answered on the second ring, which meant he had been awake.

"It's me," she said. "I found something."

A pause. She could hear him breathing.

"Something significant?" he said.

She looked at the trench. The impression in the clay was still visible from where she stood — a rectangular shadow in the pale floor, already fading.

"I don't know yet," she said. "I won't know until this afternoon. But Marcus —" She stopped. She had been about to say *I think it's the petition.* She did not say it. She had no basis for saying it and every reason not to. "Come to the university at three," she said. "Conservation suite, second floor of Hurst Hall. Don't tell anyone where you're going."

A pause. Longer than the first.

"Slade called Voss," Marcus said. "Last night. Voss called me at ten."

The field was very quiet.

"What did Voss say?"

"That the site was producing material of potential legal significance and that the state preservation office had been made aware and that he wanted to be kept informed of any finds before they were logged."

*Before they were logged.* Tayak looked at the evidence bag in Wren's hands, twenty feet away, the log number already written on the label in her own hand.

"Three o'clock," she said. "Second floor."

She ended the call and stood in the field with the phone in her hand and the sky going slowly from pewter to gray-white above the tree line, and she thought about what Voss had asked for and what she had already done, and whether the gap between those two things was a problem she could manage or the beginning of something she could not.

Wren was watching her from beside the trench.

"Conservation suite confirmed," Wren said. "They can take us at two-thirty."

"Good." Tayak walked back to the trench and picked up her field camera and her trowel and the horsehair brush and set them in the site bucket in their proper order. "Log the trench closed for this morning. We'll come back to the north quadrant this afternoon after the lab."

Wren made a note. Then she held up the evidence bag and looked at the dark packet through the plastic, tilting it slightly in the growing light.

"What do you think is in it?" she said.

Tayak took the bag from her and held it flat in both palms, the way she had held the packet itself, feeling the weight of it.

"Something someone needed to survive," she said.

The conservation suite smelled of isopropyl alcohol and old paper — a thin, medicinal smell that caught in the back of the throat. The room was small and windowless, lit by overhead fluorescents that hummed at a frequency Tayak had never been able to tune out. Two long stainless tables ran the length of the space, and above the nearer one, a rack of UV lamps hung on adjustable arms, their bulbs dark and waiting.

Adaeze Okonkwo had laid out a sheet of acid-free blotter on the examination table and set out the tools without being asked: cotton swabs in a glass jar, a magnifying loupe, a flexible ruler marked in millimetres, and a digital camera on a copy stand with a UV filter already attached. She had received the cobalt bead from this same site eleven days ago and had been sending Wren precise questions about soil matrix ever since; she knew the site number and she knew what careful work looked like. She looked at the evidence bag Tayak set on the table and said nothing for a moment.

"Iron gall, if it's 1690s," she said. "Could be badly foxed. Could be gone entirely." She picked up the bag and held it at an angle to the fluorescent light. "The oilskin protected it from the worst. Depending on what was in the soil."

"Clay," Tayak said. "Anaerobic. No mold on the exterior."

Adaeze set the bag down. "Then we might have something."

Wren stood at the end of the table with her field notebook open. Tayak had called Marcus from the field road rather than the lab — he was not here, and she had not asked him to be. What she had told him was enough for now: *something is there, I won't know until this afternoon.* If the document proved significant, he would see it in the photographs. A second person in the lab who was not on the permit created complications she did not need before she knew what was on the page.

Adaeze opened the evidence bag with a pair of flat-nosed forceps and lifted the packet out by its edges, setting it on the blotter. It was smaller under the fluorescent light than it had seemed in the trench — a dark, flattened rectangle, the oilskin stiffened and slightly bowed along the primary fold. The pitch seal at the corner was intact, a bead of darkened resin no bigger than Tayak's thumbnail.

"We'll open along the secondary folds first," Adaeze said. "If the paper is intact, the fold lines will be the weakest points." She looked at Tayak. "You want to do it, or shall I?"

"You," Tayak said.

Adaeze worked slowly. She humidified the fold lines with a cotton swab barely damp with distilled water, waited, humidified again. The oilskin softened by degrees, releasing its grip on itself. Tayak watched the conservator's hands — steady, unhurried, the swab moving in short strokes along the crease — and kept her own hands flat on the table edge.

The first fold opened. Inside it: a second layer of oilskin, folded inward, and beneath that, a darkness that was not cloth.

Paper.

Adaeze set down the swab and picked up the loupe. She held it over the edge of the inner fold for a long moment without speaking.

"It's laid rag," she said. "Linen-based. The chain lines are visible." She lowered the loupe. "It's held."

Adaeze opened the inner fold in two careful stages, the paper crackling faintly at the crease — a dry sound like a moth's wing against glass — and then lying flat on the blotter, and the room went very quiet.

The page was brown at the edges, tide-stained in a pale arc along the lower third, and the surface was mottled with the ghost-rings of old moisture that had dried and dried again over three hundred years. But the center of the page was intact. The paper had not eaten itself, had not gone to powder, had not collapsed into the dark smear that iron gall could make of a page when the conditions were wrong. The writing was there. Tayak could see it even under the fluorescents — a block of text, the lines close and even, the hand controlled and small.

She could not read it. The ink had faded to a brown so pale it was almost the color of the paper itself.

"Lights," Adaeze said.

Wren found the switch. The fluorescents went out. For a moment the room was completely dark, and Tayak could hear her own breathing and the hum of the ventilation and the small sound of Wren's pen cap clicking twice against the notebook's spine.

Then Adaeze switched on the UV lamps.

The page came alive.

Not all at once. In stages, the way a developing photograph resolves — the edges first, then the body, then the fine detail emerging from the ground. Under the UV light the iron gall ink absorbed differently than the surrounding paper, the centuries-old chemistry suddenly legible again, and the text rose out of the brown field like something surfacing from deep water.

A header. Two lines, centered, the hand formal and practiced.

*To the Honourable the General Assembly of Maryland, Humbly Sheweth —*

Below it, a date. Tayak leaned in. The numerals were clear, the ink there heavier than the rest, as if the writer had pressed harder at the top of the page.

*This 4th Day of March, 1697.*

Tayak straightened. She said nothing. She reached for the camera.

"Wait," Adaeze said. She adjusted the UV lamp angle, tipping it two degrees, and the shadows under the fold lines shifted and the text in the body of the page sharpened.

Tayak looked at it.

The syntax was formal and careful — the syntax of someone who had learned English from legal documents, from petitions and writs and the careful language of colonial courts, someone who had studied the idiom of supplication the way other people studied weather. *Your petitioner most respectfully sheweth that she and her people have held and occupied the high ground at the aforesaid tract from time beyond the memory of any living person, and that the said lands have been taken and enclosed without just title or lawful purchase, and your petitioner humbly beseeches this honourable assembly to consider the ancient right of possession —*

Tayak photographed it. She photographed it again. She moved the camera to a different angle and photographed the header and the date and the body text and the tide-stain and the fold lines and the chain lines in the paper that Adaeze had pointed to, the evidence of the paper's age and origin built into the weave of the thing itself.

Seventeen photographs. She counted them without meaning to.

Then she lowered the camera and looked at the bottom of the page.

There were two things there.

The first was a signature — a single name, written in a hand different from the body text. The body text was controlled, even, the hand of someone copying carefully. The signature was faster, more personal, the letters slightly larger, as if the person who had written the body text had written it for someone else and that someone else had signed.

The second thing was the handprint.

It was pressed beside the signature, not below it — level with it, as if the two things were equal. The print was clear: the heel of the palm, the four fingers close together, the thumb slightly apart. It had been made in something darker than the iron gall ink of the body text — something that had held its color better over three centuries, a reddish-brown that under the UV light was almost black. Ochre, possibly. Or a different ink entirely. The ridges of the fingertips were visible in the print's upper edge.

Someone had pressed their hand to this paper and meant it to stay.

Tayak photographed the signature. She photographed the handprint. She photographed the signature and the handprint together, and then the handprint alone, and then the signature alone, and then the full page from above with the UV lamp at its current angle, and then with the lamp shifted two degrees back, and then with it shifted two degrees forward. She was counting without counting. She knew the number when she stopped.

Seventeen more.

She set the camera down.

The name beside the handprint was a fragment. The first letter was clear — a capital, formal, pressed with deliberate weight. The second letter resolved beside it. Then the ink thinned where the paper had absorbed it unevenly over the centuries, the middle of the name dropping into the same pale brown as the surrounding page. The final letters came back at the end of the word, firmer again, as if the writer had pressed harder in closing.

She could read the beginning. She could read the end. The middle was gone.

She did not read it aloud.

She stood at the table with the UV light turning everything in the room into its own negative — her hands pale and strange, Wren's face a blue-white oval at the edge of her vision, the page on the blotter a luminous field with the handprint at its center, the ochre holding its color against three hundred years of dark and damp as if it had been waiting for exactly this light.

"Wren," she said. "Log entry."

Wren had the field notebook open. The pen was already moving.

"Conservation examination, Hurst Hall suite, fourteen-forty-seven hours. Evidence item 18CH-447-L14-002, oilskin packet. Interior contents: single sheet laid rag paper, linen-based, consistent with late seventeenth century Chesapeake manufacture. Document type: formal petition. Addressee: General Assembly of Maryland. Date in document: fourth March, 1697. Body text: iron gall ink, faded, partially legible under UV illumination. Language: formal English petition idiom. Signature: present, different hand from body text, partially legible — first letter and terminal letters visible, middle section illegible. Associated mark: handprint, ochre or dark pigment, pressed beside signature, well-preserved. Condition: fold damage at primary and secondary creases, tide-staining lower third, no mold, no insect damage, paper structurally intact at centre."

Wren wrote without looking up. Her pen made a small, scratching sound in the quiet room.

"Photographs taken," Tayak said. "Thirty-four total. UV illumination, two lamp angles."

Adaeze, who had been standing back during the log entry, moved to the table and adjusted the UV lamp a third time, tipping it to a raking angle that threw the surface of the paper into sharp relief. The fold lines became trenches. The chain lines of the laid rag became a grid. And in the middle section of the signature, where the ink had seemed to vanish, something shifted.

Not legible. Not yet. But not entirely gone.

"Multispectral," Adaeze said. "If you want the middle letters, you'll need multispectral imaging. I can put in the request today. Turnaround is three to five days."

Three to five days. Tayak thought about the thirty-eight days remaining before Garrett Wills's bulldozers arrived at the Route 301 connector. She thought about Slade, who had called Voss last night. She thought about what Voss had asked for and what she had already done.

"Put in the request," she said.

She picked up the camera and looked at the last photograph on the screen — the full page under UV, the handprint clear and dark at the bottom, the partial signature beside it, the date at the top. The image was sharp. The handprint was unmistakable.

She thought about the woman who had pressed her hand to this paper. About what it had cost to walk to the assembly in March, in 1697, in the Chesapeake, and stand in front of men who did not know her name between one meeting and the next, and press her hand to the page anyway, and mean it to last.

It had lasted.

She looked at Wren. "I need to make a call," she said. "Outside."

In the hallway outside the conservation suite, under the fluorescent lights that were ordinary and white after the UV blue, Tayak stood with her phone and thought about who she needed to call and in what order and what she could say and what she could not say yet, and whether the partial name at the bottom of that page was the name she thought it might be — the name that had been in Nora's stories for sixty-two years, the name that connected the document in the trench to the bead in the trench to the oral history that Nora had been keeping since 1962.

She did not yet know. She had thirty-four photographs and a partial name and three to five days.

She called Marcus Redcloud.

He answered on the second ring. She could hear, faintly, the sound of traffic — he was outside somewhere, or near an open window.

"Tell me," he said.

"A document. Single sheet, laid rag paper, linen content, consistent with late seventeenth century Chesapeake manufacture." She kept it precise. Marcus would want precise. "Folded inside the oilskin packet. Formal petition in English, iron gall ink, addressed to the General Assembly of Maryland. Date in the document: fourth March, 1697."

Four seconds of silence. She counted them.

"Signed," she said. "Different hand from the body text. And there's a handprint beside the signature. Ochre or dark pigment. Clear and well-preserved."

"A Piscataway woman," Marcus said. Not a question.

"The petition is addressed to the General Assembly. The language is formal — petition idiom, supplication form. The body text is partially legible under UV. I can read the beginning and the end of the signature. The middle section is gone — ink absorbed unevenly into the paper, probably from the fold and the damp. I've put in a request for multispectral imaging. Three to five days."

"The name."

"Partially legible. I'm not reading it to you yet."

A silence. She heard him exhale — not frustration, something more careful than that. "Can you tell me if it's the name we have."

"Not yet. I need the middle letters."

"All right." He was quiet for a moment. "Is it legally admissible? As continuous-occupation evidence?"

She had known he would ask. She had been turning the question over since Adaeze adjusted the lamp and the fold lines became trenches. "I don't know yet. A document historian needs to confirm the paper dating and the ink composition independently. The chain of custody is clean from the moment it came out of the ground, but I was alone in the trench when I found it. Before Wren arrived."

She said it plainly. She needed him to understand it before someone else explained it to him differently.

"How long alone."

"Eleven minutes. I have the field log."

"Eleven minutes," Marcus said. He was not reassuring her. He was filing it. "What about the oilskin? The packet itself?"

"Intact. I have photographs of the packet in situ, the oilskin condition, the fold, the extraction. Thirty-four photographs total of the document under UV. The log entry is complete."

"Good." A pause. "Tayak. Slade called my office this morning."

She had not expected it this soon. She kept her voice flat. "What did he want."

"Nothing specific. That's what concerns me. It was brief and polite and he asked nothing. He said he was following up on the Phase One survey progress and wanted to make sure the community was being kept informed of any significant finds."

She looked at the door to the conservation suite. Through the small wired-glass window she could see the edge of the table, the UV lamp's arm, the faint blue cast of the light still on.

"He used that phrase," she said. "'Significant finds.'"

"He did."

"Before or after the Post inquiry."

"I don't know when he learned about the inquiry. The call came in at nine forty-seven. I was in a meeting. He left a message."

A polite message asking nothing specific. She understood. A direct question created a record. A polite call created a presence — established that his office had been attentive, had reached out, had noted the timeline. If something came up later, he would have a record of due diligence. If the chain of custody was ever challenged, he would have a record of the moment before anyone knew there was something to challenge.

"He's not asking yet," she said. "He's marking the date."

"That's what I think."

She turned away from the window. Down the hallway, a graduate student she didn't know was pushing a cart of flat files toward the stairwell, not looking up. The cart's wheels needed oil. The sound followed him around the corner and faded.

"I'll call David Soto," she said.

"Tonight?"

"Now. He answers when he's at his desk."

"Tayak." Marcus's voice shifted — not softer, but more careful, the way he spoke when he was about to say something he had been holding for a few sentences. "If the name on that document is the name in Nora's story. If the multispectral confirms it." He paused. "That's two thousand years of chain. Bead to document to oral history to your grandmother's kitchen table."

She knew. She had been not-thinking it since the moment the handprint came clear under the UV light. "I know."

"Does Nora know what you found."

"Not yet. I'll call her after Soto."

"She'll know," Marcus said. "She's been waiting for this longer than either of us."

Tayak said nothing. She was thinking about Slade's polite message and the eleven minutes in the trench and the three to five days before the multispectral came back, and about what Slade's office could do with a gap in the chain of custody if they decided to look at it hard. She was thinking about the handprint on the page — the woman who had pressed it there in March of 1697, in a room full of men who would not remember her name, and meant it to outlast all of them.

It had outlasted them. It was in a lab twelve feet behind her right now under a UV lamp, waiting for its middle letters to come back.

"I'll call Soto," she said. "I'll call you after."

She hung up. She stood in the hallway for another moment, the phone in her hand, the fluorescent light overhead, the distant sound of the graduate student's cart gone now and the building quiet around her.

Then she called Nora.

The phone rang four times. Then Nora's voice, careful and awake even at this hour: "Tayak."

"Nana." She kept her voice level. "I need to ask you something. About the name."

A pause. Not surprise. Something more like readiness. "Which name."

"The woman who walked to the assembly. In the story Ida told you."

"Saya," Nora said. "You know that name."

"I know it. But I need to know how it was written down. If it was ever written down. If Ida ever told you how the colonial records had it — whether the spelling was different, whether there was an English version."

The silence lasted long enough that Tayak heard the small sound of Nora setting something down — a cup, maybe, or her reading glasses. "Ida said the petition was in the ground," Nora said. "She didn't say she'd read it."

"I know."

"She said the name on it was the name she knew. That was all."

Tayak pressed her back against the hallway wall. The fluorescent light above her buzzed at a frequency she could feel in her back teeth. "All right," she said. "That's enough for now."

"Tayak." Nora's voice was steady and warm and would not be hurried. "Is it there?"

"Something is there. I don't know yet what it says."

"Then call me when you know."

She hung up. She stood in the hallway for a moment with the phone warm in her hand and the UV blue still at the edges of her vision — the afterimage of the handprint, dark and clear, pressed beside a name she could not yet fully read.

She called David Soto.

He picked up on the first ring. She could hear the particular flatness of a room where someone had been working alone for several hours — no ambient sound, no movement, just the slight compression of a line in a quiet office.

"I was wondering when you'd call," he said.

"You heard."

"Marcus texted me at four this afternoon. Didn't say what. Just: *Tayak found something. Call her tonight.* I've been at my desk since six."

She told him what she had told Marcus, in the same order, with the same precision. He did not interrupt. When she finished there was a pause of perhaps five seconds.

"Chain of custody," he said.

"Eleven minutes alone in the trench. I have the field log and seventeen photographs with timestamps before Wren arrived."

"That's defensible," he said. "Not comfortable, but defensible." A pause. "The permit language. Slade's name is on it in two places?"

"Principal reviewer and SHPO compliance contact."

"He can request documentation. He can also request a hold on further excavation pending review of any significant finds." Another pause. "If he files that before the multispectral comes back, you're in a freeze with thirty-eight days on the clock."

"I know."

"Then we need the multispectral fast. Can Adaeze push it?"

"I'll ask her in the morning."

"Ask her tonight," Soto said. "And Tayak — the document historian. You need someone whose name Slade can't argue with. Someone with a publication record on seventeenth-century Chesapeake documents specifically."

She had already been thinking about this. "I'll make some calls."

"Good." He was quiet for a moment. "The handprint. Ochre?"

"Possibly. Adaeze will test the pigment composition."

"If it's ochre and the paper dates to the 1690s and the ink composition is consistent and the multispectral gives you the name —" He stopped. She could hear him thinking. "That's a continuous-occupation chain going back to 1697 at minimum, with a physical document linking a named individual to the specific high ground. Combined with the bead and the oral history —"

"I know what it is," she said.

"Yes," he said. "I imagine you do."

She went back into the lab.

The UV lamp was still on.

She had not turned it off before the calls, and the blue-white light lay across the document like something alive, picking out the iron gall's dark edges against the rag paper's pale field. She pulled the stool close and looked at the monitor instead of the document itself — the high-resolution image Adaeze had captured, seventeen photographs stitched into a single composite that showed the full fragment at four times its actual size.

The fold lines ran like dry riverbeds across the surface. The water staining had bloomed at the lower right corner in a brown tide-mark that had eaten two lines of text entirely. The rest was there. Most of it was there.

She had been avoiding the signature block.

She moved the cursor to the lower left quadrant and enlarged it.

The handprint came up first, before the text, because it was the largest mark on the page and the UV caught it differently than the ink — the oils from the skin had soaked into the rag fiber in a way that iron gall never did, and three hundred and twenty-seven years had not fully erased the difference. Four fingers and a thumb. The thumb angled slightly left, the way a right-handed person's thumb goes when they press the whole hand flat and mean it. The fingers were close together, not splayed. It was not a smear or an accident. Someone had inked their palm — she could see the edge of the ink line, too clean for a casual touch — and pressed deliberately, the way you press when you want the impression to hold.

She had been trained to read documents before she could read sites. Dr. Marant had made her spend a semester in the Howard archive before she was allowed to touch a trowel. *Listen with your hands, but first learn what hands have said.* The handprint on the monitor said: *I was here. I knew what I was doing. I meant this to count.*

She moved the enlargement to the text beside it.

The signature line was separate from the petition body — she could see the change in pen pressure, a slightly different angle to the letters, as though the writer had paused and then signed with more care than they had drafted. The petition body was a clerk's hand, neat and practiced, the long-s forms and the looping ascenders of late seventeenth-century English legal script. The signature was different. Smaller. More deliberate. Someone who had learned to write in a context where writing was not ordinary.

She enlarged it further.

The first letter was S.

She had known that from the earlier pass. She moved the enlargement right.

The second letter was A.

She stopped.

Her hand was on the mouse and she stopped moving it.

The name in Nora's story was Saya. She had known that since she was old enough to sit at the kitchen table and listen. Saya, who had walked two days to present a petition. Saya, whose bead — her mother's bead — had come out of the ground eleven days ago and was in a conservation suite in College Park right now, logged as 18CH-447-L14-001. Saya, who according to Nora's story had wrapped the petition in oilskin and buried it in the mound when it failed, and whose daughter Lena had been four years old and learning to write her own name in the dirt with a stick.

She did not move the enlargement.

The third letter was there. She could see the top of it at the edge of the frame. A curve. It could be a Y. It could be a V. It could be a water-damaged A that had lost its crossbar.

She did not enlarge it further.

She sat with her hands in her lap and looked at the first two letters and the curve of the third, and she understood something about herself in that moment: she was afraid. Not of what the letters would say if she read them. She was afraid of what she would become the instant she knew for certain — the shift from *possibly* to *yes*, from the document's evidence to her own evidence, from the archaeologist's careful distance to something she had no professional word for.

Adaeze had said three to five days for the multispectral. The multispectral would give her the middle letters cleanly, without ambiguity, with a calibration record and a lab timestamp she could put in front of a court. If she read the name now, off a UV composite at four times magnification, alone in a lab at nine-forty in the evening, she would know it but she would not be able to use the knowing. It would just be hers.

She saved the file.

She saved it three times, under three different names, to three different locations — the lab server, her university cloud drive, and the encrypted external drive she kept in her bag. Then she photographed the monitor with her phone, because she had learned a long time ago not to trust any single system with something irreplaceable.

She turned off the UV lamp.

The document went back to what it looked like in ordinary light — a fragment of brown-edged rag paper, foxed and tide-marked, the ink faded to the color of old rust, the handprint nearly invisible. She stood over it for a moment. The oilskin had kept it for three centuries in the dark and the damp, and it had survived. It looked like almost nothing. It was almost everything.

She covered it with the acid-free tissue Adaeze had laid out, placed the archival board on top, and locked the conservation suite with the key Adaeze had signed out to her until Friday.

In the hallway the fluorescent light buzzed at a frequency she had stopped hearing an hour ago and now heard again. She pulled on her field jacket — still carrying a thin line of Charles County clay along the left cuff where she had braced herself coming out of the trench — and walked to the stairwell.

The parking lot was nearly empty. Her truck sat under the one working lamp at the far end, the locked field kit in the back with the red-covered notebook and the thirty-four photographs she had taken before Wren arrived. Eleven minutes. She had the log. She had the photographs. She had the intact oilskin packet and the chain of custody from the moment the trowel blade touched the wrapping. What she did not have was a second person in the trench.

She got in and sat for a moment without starting the engine.

Slade had called Marcus Redcloud's office at nine forty-seven that morning. A polite call, nothing specific, no direct questions. A presence in the record. She thought about what Adaeze had said: *three to five days.* She thought about what three to five days meant if Slade decided to file an inquiry through the SHPO compliance process before the multispectral came back — a request for documentation, a mandatory response window, a freeze on further excavation pending review. Standard procedure. Nothing unusual. The kind of thing that happened all the time with sites where the affiliated community lacked federal recognition and the permit language was what it was.

She started the truck.

Route 301 south was nearly empty at this hour, the pine woods pressing close on both sides, the occasional light of a farmhouse set back from the road. She had driven this stretch enough times in the past three weeks that she no longer saw it consciously — her hands knew the curve past the grain elevator, the long straight before the turnoff, the way the road dipped toward the creek bottom where her grandmother had brought her once to pick watercress, the water cold and clear over dark mud, her grandmother's hands moving through the green without disturbing it.

She had been eight years old. She had not known then what the high ground above the creek was. She had not known what her grandmother knew.

The turnoff came and she took it without slowing.

The field gate was padlocked — Dawes left it that way after dark, which she had asked him to do — and she got out in the headlights and worked the combination and drove through and locked it behind her. The mound was a low dark shape against the darker field, the stars clear above it, no moon yet. She parked and killed the engine and sat in the quiet.

The name began with S and A.

She would not read the third letter until the multispectral came back. She would call Adaeze in the morning and ask whether three to five days could become two. She would write up the chain-of-custody notes tonight, in the field notebook, by headlamp, while the details were still exact in her hands.

She reached for the notebook and the headlamp and got out of the truck.

The night smelled of turned earth and cold pine resin and the faint brackish edge of the creek a quarter mile south. Somewhere in the dark a tree frog was calling, early for the season, insistent. She walked to the edge of the trench and crouched and put her hand flat against the soil at the lip, the way she always did at the end of a field day — not ritual, just contact, the way you check that a thing is still there.

The ground was cold and damp and solid under her palm.

She opened the notebook to a fresh page and began to write.
