The rise was not much. That was the first thing she noticed every time she came here, and she noticed it again now in the grey before sunrise: it was not much. A slight swelling of the earth above the river terrace, the way a sleeping body makes a shape under a blanket. The grass grew shorter on its crown than on the surrounding ground, and the soil was paler there, thinner, as if the rise were too high to hold moisture and had given up trying. Her mother had told her this was why the place mattered. Not because it was large. Because it was *different*.

Yanu stopped at the edge of the rise and looked at it.

She had not slept. She could feel that fact the way you feel a stone in a sandal — not sharp, just constant, shifting with every step. Her eyes were dry and her hands were cold, and the copper pendant lay in both palms with its weight that was always slightly more than she expected. She had carried it from the sleeping house before Ranu woke, lifting it from the cedar box by feel, not wanting to see it by lamplight. She had seen it enough.

The sky above the tree line was the color of river water in winter, a pale grey-green that had not yet decided to be morning. The settlement behind her was quiet except for one fire already lit — she could smell the smoke, birchwood, someone else's early rising — and the sound of the river below the terrace, which was always there and which she had stopped hearing years ago except at moments like this, when everything else fell away.

She walked around the rise.

Her mother had taught her this. *Read the ground before you touch it. Walk it once, the way you walk around a sleeping person, to understand what you are in the presence of.* She had been eight years old when her mother said that, standing on this same rise, and she had not understood it then. She understood it now, or she understood something, which was not the same thing. She walked the full circle, feeling the ground through the thin leather of her shoes — the slight unevenness where the rise began, the packed earth of the crown, the way the slope fell away on the river side more steeply than on the settlement side, as if the rise were leaning toward the water. Small stones rolled under her heel. The grass was wet with dew and left dark marks on her shoes.

She stopped where she had started.

The pendant was still warm from the cedar box, from the house, from her hands. She turned it over once. The copper was dull reddish-brown with a smear of green along one edge where the patina had taken hold, and the cord that ran through the punched hole at the top was twisted sinew, dark and smooth, worn to a gloss at the knot. The surface had a pattern of incised lines — she had run her thumb along them so many times she could have drawn them in the dirt without looking — shallow grooves that formed something almost like a shape, a gesture, a word in a language she did not know. She did not know who had made it. That was its oldest trouble.

She thought: *Taya held this.*

Once. She had held it once, on the fifth day of the fever, when she was still able to close her fingers. Yanu had laid it in her daughter's palm and closed the small fingers around it and told her it was the most beautiful thing in the house, which was true. Taya had looked at it for a long time without speaking, which was not like her — Taya was always speaking, always asking — and then she had given it back. On the seventh day she was gone.

Yanu had not been able to put the pendant back in the cedar box for three days after that. She had not been able to touch it. Then she had been unable to stop touching it.

She crouched at the crown of the rise and pressed her fingers into the earth.

The top layer broke easily, dry and fine, smelling of grass and last autumn's leaves, a thin crust over something darker. She worked her fingers deeper, feeling the soil change — cooler, denser, faintly moist — and a small root caught against her nail and snapped. She scooped the earth out with her cupped hand, setting it to one side, and the smell of the deeper soil came up to her: richer, darker, a faint metallic edge she had not expected. She kept digging. The stones were small here, rounded, and they scraped against her knuckles without cutting. Fine roots brushed her fingers like hair.

She dug until her wrist was in the earth up to the first fold of her sleeve.

She stopped. She looked at the hole. It was not deep enough for a person, not deep enough for anything large, and she felt, for a moment, the full absurdity of what she was doing: a woman alone on a hill at first light, digging with her hands, holding a piece of copper that her dead daughter had touched once. She felt the cold coming up from the soil into her fingers. She felt the dew soaking through the knee of her tunic where she knelt.

She thought: *Perhaps the earth will feel nothing.*

She had thought this before, in other forms. She had thought it at the ceremony three years ago when the rain did not come. She had thought it the night Taya's breathing changed. She was not a woman who believed easily, and she knew it, and it had never seemed to her a failing until now, when she needed to believe in something and found the space inside her where belief should live still swept clean and cold.

But her mother had walked her around this rise. Her mother had said *read it*. And her mother had not been a foolish woman.

Yanu set the pendant in the hole.

She placed it face-up, the way you place a sleeping child — so that what matters faces the light, even underground, even in the dark. The copper settled against the cool soil and she felt the faint metallic smell sharpen for a moment, mixing with the smell of fresh earth. She straightened the cord so it lay flat beside the pendant rather than tangled over it. She was careful about this. She did not know why she was careful about this; there was no one to see it. She was careful anyway.

She thought of Ranu's figure, still in the fold of her cloak. She had almost forgotten it. She drew it out and held it in her palm: a small carved shape of antler, a figure with its arms slightly raised, worn smooth at the head and chest where hands had held it over years. Ranu had pressed it into her hands yesterday without speaking, which was so unlike him that she had not known what to say. Ranu, who told stories the way other people breathed. Ranu, who could not cross the settlement yard without narrating the crossing. He had handed her the figure in silence and gone back to the fire.

She placed the figure beside the pendant. Not on top — beside, the way two people sit together, close but each its own thing. The antler was paler than the copper, and smaller, and it tilted slightly in the loose soil. She pressed one finger against its base until it steadied. She angled it so its carved face turned toward the pendant rather than straight up at the sky. She did not know why she did this. She only knew that Taya had never liked to be looked at from directly above — had always turned her face away when Yanu leaned over her sleeping mat, had liked to be approached from the side, from the same level, as though you were arriving beside her rather than over her.

Yanu had not thought of that in months. She had not known she still held it.

Then she covered them.

The soil went back in layers, the darker clumps first, then the finer surface earth, and she pressed it down with both palms, smoothing it, feeling the disturbed ground give slightly under her weight and then hold. The surface was looser than the surrounding earth, darker in the early light, a small irregular patch that anyone walking here would see. By tomorrow it would not be so visible. By the end of the season the grass would begin to come back. In a year you would not know.

She sat back on her heels.

The sun had begun to clear the tree line. The light came in at a low angle, slanted and warm-colored, and it caught the disturbed soil and made it look almost red against the paler ground around it. The shadows of the grasses were long and thin. Somewhere below the terrace a bird was calling, short and repeated, and then another answered it, and then both went quiet.

She said her daughter's name.

She said it the way you say a word you are afraid to say — quietly, but all the way through, not swallowed at the end. The name sat in the air for a moment and then the wind took it.

She said it again.

The second time was harder. She did not know why. The first time you are still deciding whether to speak. The second time you know what it costs.

She said it a third time.

The third time she felt something move in her chest, not grief exactly — grief she knew, she had been carrying it for forty days — but something older than grief, something that had been waiting underneath it. She pressed her palms flat against the disturbed earth over the pendant and the figure, and the soil was cool and slightly gritty against her skin, and she held her hands there.

*The earth knows now*, she thought. *I have told it.*

She did not know if she believed that. She knew she needed it to be true, and she knew those were not the same thing, and she stayed anyway, palms flat on the small dark patch of turned soil, while the light strengthened and the shadows shortened and the settlement behind her began, slowly, to wake.

She did not move for a long time.

The sun climbed. The slanted light went from red to pale gold, and the shadows of the grasses shortened until they lay almost directly beneath the blades, and still she sat with her palms pressed against the turned soil. Her knees ached from the kneeling. The dew had soaked through the cloth at her knee and dried to a faint stiffness, and her hands, when she finally lifted them, had left two faint impressions in the looser earth, like the marks a sleeping body leaves in a bed.

She looked at her hands.

The knuckles were raw. She had not noticed this while she was digging — she had been thinking about the depth, about whether the hole was deep enough, about whether the pendant would shift when she covered it. Now she saw the skin was broken at two knuckles on the right hand and one on the left, where she had caught small stones in the dark soil. The blood had dried to a thin rust-colored crust, and the skin around each cut was pale and tight from the cold.

She pressed the knuckles of her right hand against her tunic to clean them, then stopped. There was something right about the blood being there. She left it.

Below the terrace, the settlement was waking. She could hear it without seeing it: the sound of a door being pushed open, the low complaint of the goats, a child's voice rising and then cut short by a quieter adult voice. Smoke was rising from two fires now, grey against the pale sky, bending east in the morning wind. Ranu's fire would be one of them. She knew which one without looking.

She stood. Her legs had stiffened and she rose slowly, one hand pressing briefly against the earth for balance before she straightened. The disturbed patch of soil was small. She had not realized, while she was digging, how small it was — her hands had made it feel enormous, the work of them, the cold and the resistance and the small stones scraping at her skin. But it was not large. A child could step across it without noticing.

She looked at it for a moment.

She had carried the pendant for forty days in the fold of her cloak, against her body, and now it was in the ground. She felt the absence of it. Not grief, not quite — grief she had been carrying since the seventh day of Taya's illness, when the breathing changed and she had known. This was different. This was the particular lightness of setting something down after you have carried it so long you no longer know you are carrying it.

The wind moved through the grasses on the slope. The rise was not high — she could see the tree line to the east over it, the dark shapes of the oaks still holding their dead leaves, and beyond them the glint of the river in the early light. Her mother had brought her here when she was small, walking the perimeter of the rise slowly, the way you walk around something you want a child to remember. *Read it*, her mother had said, meaning: look at the way the ground rises here and nowhere else. Look at the way the grasses grow differently on the slope, shorter and stiffer, as if the earth beneath them is different from the earth around them. Look at the way people have always gone around it rather than across it, without anyone telling them to. *Read it.*

She had been perhaps eight years old. She had not understood. She had been looking at a small hill.

She understood now.

She bent and pressed two fingers into the disturbed soil one last time, not digging — only touching. The earth was cool under her fingertips, slightly damp where she had worked it, and finer than the surface crust around it. She could feel the faint compression where she had smoothed it with her palms. The pendant was below her fingers. Ranu's figure was below her fingers. She could not feel them, but she knew they were there, and knowing was enough.

She straightened and pulled her cloak tighter across her shoulders. The morning was cold and would not warm quickly. She had a long walk back down and the fire would need tending and she was hungry in the distant, unimportant way she had been hungry most mornings since Taya died, when food had seemed beside the point.

She did not look back at the rise when she started down the slope.

She had meant to. She had thought, while she was digging, that she would look back when she left — one last look, to fix it in her mind, to carry the image of the small dark patch of turned soil in the morning light. But when the moment came, she found she did not need to. She knew where it was. She would always know where it was. That was the point.

The grass on the slope was wet against her ankles and the leather of her shoes was soft from the dew, and the ground was uneven underfoot, and she went carefully, watching where she placed her feet. The settlement came into view below her: the rooflines, the smoke, the pale morning light on the wattle walls.

She did not know what she had started.

She was only trying to keep her daughter's name alive.

She had not arranged anything for Taya in the end.

That was the thing she had not let herself think until now, crouching over the hole with the pendant warm in her palm. When Taya died there had been the washing, the wrapping, the carrying — all of it done by other hands, older hands, hands that knew the forms. Yanu had stood at the edge of it and let it happen around her because her own hands had stopped knowing what to do. She had been useless in the way that grief makes you useless: present, breathing, capable of nothing.

She set the pendant in first.

Face up, as she had decided on the walk here, though she could not have said when she decided it. The copper caught the early light for a moment — a dull reddish gleam, the green spots of old weathering dull against it — and then she had positioned it and it was only a thing in the earth, a small disc with its punched hole and its incised lines, the faint ridges she had traced with her thumb so many times she could feel them without looking. She straightened it with one finger. The hole at the top, where the cord had threaded through, she pointed toward the east, toward the river glint she could see over the rise. She did not know why. It seemed right. She had learned, in the months since Taya died, to trust the things that seemed right without being able to explain them, because explaining required a kind of energy she no longer had.

She took Ranu's figure from where she had set it in the grass beside her knee.

It was small enough to close her fist around, carved from a piece of antler the color of old cream, gone darker and smoother where his hands had worked it. She could feel the knife marks still in the less-handled places — the back, the feet — fine parallel lines where the blade had gone with the grain. The face he had given it was simple: two cuts for eyes, a shallow groove for a mouth. Not Taya's face. She had been afraid it would be Taya's face and she would not be able to set it down. But Ranu had not done that. He had made a face that was only a face, a face that could be anyone, and she was grateful for it in the way you are grateful for the specific, practical kindness that arrives without being asked for, that costs the giver something they do not mention.

She set it beside the pendant.

Not on top. Beside. She placed it so that the two objects sat together the way two people sit together — close, not touching, each one its own thing. She adjusted the figure once, tilting it slightly so its face was angled toward the pendant rather than straight up at the sky. She did not know why she did this either. She only knew that Taya had never liked to be looked at from directly above. She had always turned her face away when Yanu leaned over her sleeping mat. She had liked to be approached from the side, from the same level, as though you were arriving beside her rather than over her.

Yanu had not thought of that in months. She had not known she still held it.

She sat back on her heels and looked at what she had arranged.

The pendant and the figure lay in the small dark hollow she had dug, the soil around them loose and fine, the smell of fresh earth rising — cool and slightly metallic where the copper had rested in her hand. The disturbed earth was darker than the surface crust around it, the way a bruise is darker than the surrounding skin. She had dug to roughly elbow depth, scooping and breaking the crust with the heel of her hand first, then working her fingers in, feeling small roots snap and small stones scrape the skin of her knuckles. The soil at the bottom had been cooler, slightly damp, different from the dry upper crust in the way that deep things are always different from surface things.

She had not prayed.

She had thought, on the walk up, that she might pray — that some form of words would come to her when she needed them, the way words sometimes came when the need was great enough. They had not come. She did not know the right words for this. She knew words for planting and words for harvest and words for the dead and words for the sick and words for the rain that did not come and words for the rain that finally did. She did not know words for: *I am giving the earth something that belonged to my daughter, so that the earth will know her name.* There were no words for that. Or if there were, they were not hers.

So she used her hands instead.

She reached into the hole and straightened the pendant again, though it did not need straightening. She touched the figure's carved antler head with one finger, lightly, the way she had touched Taya's forehead in the mornings to check for fever — a habit so old it had become a kind of greeting. She touched the rim of the hole, pressing the loose soil inward slightly, shaping it. Then she began to fill.

She worked slowly. The soil came back in small handfuls, breaking against her fingers, the clumps crumbling as she pressed them down. She did not rush. She had arranged Taya's sleeping mat this carefully — spreading the deerhide flat, smoothing the edges, folding the extra length under so it would not bunch in the night. She had done it every morning for four years. She had not thought of it as a skill. It was only something her hands knew how to do.

Her hands knew how to do this too, it turned out.

She pressed the earth down in layers, not packing it hard — only settling it, the way you settle a covering over someone sleeping, firm enough to hold, gentle enough not to wake. Small stones she set aside rather than burying them with the rest. She did not know why. It seemed wrong to put stones over them. The earth itself was enough.

When the hole was filled, she smoothed the surface with her palms.

The disturbed patch was slightly raised, the way filled earth always is — it would settle with rain and time and the weight of seasons, and in a year there would be almost nothing to see. She pressed it flat as she could. Her palms were coated now with a thin paste of dust and sweat, the fine soil worked into the creases of her fingers and under her nails, and she rubbed her hands against her thighs to clean them and they were not clean, and she rubbed them again and they were still not clean, and she stopped.

The pendant was below her hands. Ranu's figure was below her hands.

She could not feel them. She knew they were there.

She sat with her hands flat on the smoothed earth and felt the slight coolness of it through her palms, and the morning light came at an angle now, slanted and warm-colored, and the long shadow of the rise stretched east across the wet grass, and somewhere below the slope a goat called and was answered.

She did not know what she had made.

She knew what she had meant: to put Taya's name somewhere it could not be lost. To give it to something older and larger and less likely to forget than she was. She was one woman. She would die. She knew this the way you know things you cannot afford to think about directly — from the side, in glimpses. When she died, Taya's name would go with her unless she had put it somewhere first.

She had put it somewhere.

Whether the earth would keep it — whether the earth kept anything, whether the earth was the kind of thing that kept — she did not know. She had acted as though it was. That was all she could do. You act as though the thing is true and you do the work the truth requires and then you wait and see what the world makes of it.

Her hands were still on the smoothed soil. The morning was brightening around her, the light going from slanted gold to something cleaner and more ordinary. The shadow of the rise shortened. A bird called from the oaks to the east — a single note, repeated twice, then silence.

She became aware that she was cold.

She had not noticed while she was working. Now she noticed: her fingers were stiff, the skin of her forearms tight where the morning air had dried the sweat. She pulled her cloak around her shoulders and held it closed with one fist. The wool smelled of smoke and the small particular smell of her own house, and it was a good smell, a living smell, and she held it close.

She did not move yet.

She was not ready to move yet. There was something unfinished — not in the ground, the ground was finished — but in herself. Something she needed to do before she stood up and walked back down the slope and became again the woman who tended the fire and fed the goats and spoke to Ranu and ate her food and slept and woke and continued.

She knew what it was.

She had known since she started up the slope this morning. She had been saving it, the way you save the last of something good.

She took a breath of the cold air — it tasted of wet grass and woodsmoke from the settlement below and the faint mineral smell of the turned earth — and she opened her mouth.

She said her daughter's name.

Taya.

The word left her mouth and the morning took it — the cold air, the oaks, the long shadow of the rise — and it was gone. She had said it thousands of times. She had said it calling the child in from the field path, said it sharp when Taya reached for the fire, said it soft in the dark when the fever climbed and she was bargaining with whatever listened. She had said it into her own cupped hands the morning after, because she had to put it somewhere. She had said it until saying it felt like pressing on a bruise — something you do not mean to do and cannot stop doing.

This was different.

She said it again.

Taya.

Louder this time. Not shouting — she was not shouting — but with weight behind it, the way you speak to someone who is some distance away and not yet looking at you. She felt the name in her chest as she said it, felt the shape of it in her mouth, the soft opening of the first sound and the way it closed at the end. Her grandmother had told her that seeds needed to be spoken over. Not prayed over — her grandmother had been careful about the distinction, had said *spoken over*, the way you speak to someone you expect to hear you. Three times, she had said. Once so the seed knows it is seen. Once so the seed knows what is wanted of it. The third time so it knows your name too, so it can find you when it grows.

Her grandmother had been talking about seeds.

She said it a third time.

*Taya.*

Her own voice surprised her. The first two times she had heard grief in it — the familiar weight, the bruise-pressure — but the third time was different. The third time came out of her mouth like something being handed over. Not a wound reopened. A task completed. She felt it leave her and enter the air and enter the earth below her palms, and she thought: *there*. The way you think *there* when you have set something down in the right place after carrying it a long way.

The goat below the slope called again. The bird in the oaks said its two notes and stopped.

She had not known it would feel like that. She had thought it would break her open — the third time, the final time, the time that made it real. She had been bracing for it the way you brace for cold water. But the third saying of the name did not break her. It settled something. Her grandmother had not explained that part. Perhaps her grandmother had not needed to explain it, because her grandmother had understood that you only learn it by doing it, and explaining would have made it smaller than it was.

She pressed her palms harder against the smoothed soil. She could feel the slight give of the freshly turned earth under her hands, the way it was still loose beneath the surface crust, the cooler damp of it working through to her skin.

*The earth knows it now,* she thought. Then she thought: *I don't know that.* Then she thought: *It doesn't matter whether I know it. I've done the work the truth requires.*

She had heard Ranu say something like that once — he said things like that constantly, the way other people breathed, and mostly she let the words move past her the way you let smoke move past — but this one had stayed. *You do the work the truth requires and then you wait and see what the world makes of it.* She had not known when he said it what work he meant, or what truth. She knew now. She did not think Ranu had known either. He said things that were true before he understood why they were true. It was his great gift and the reason she was sometimes very tired of him.

She lifted her palms from the earth.

The patch of disturbed soil was darker than the ground around it, the turned earth holding moisture the surface had lost. It would lighten as it dried. In a season it would be almost even with the surrounding ground. In several seasons there would be only a slight difference in the grass — a small circle where the turf grew a little thicker, a little greener, fed by what was below. She had seen that happen with other burials: the ground remembering in the only language it had.

She got to her feet slowly. Her knees ached from the cold and from kneeling. She straightened and stood on the crown of the rise and looked out.

The settlement below was fully awake now. Smoke from four fires, maybe five. A child's voice carrying up the slope, high and unintelligible, the way children's voices sound at a distance — all tone, no words. The river beyond the settlement caught the morning light in flat bright pieces. The oaks to the east were dark against the pale sky, their branches bare, and the grass on the slope was still wet, each blade carrying a small bead of light.

She had not looked at any of this on the way up. She had been looking at the ground. Now she looked.

It was ordinary. It was the same settlement she had walked away from this morning and would walk back to in a moment. The same smoke, the same river, the same child's voice that might be anyone's child. Nothing had changed in the world while she was on the rise doing what she had come to do.

She had not expected anything to change. She was not that kind of person — she did not expect the sky to answer, did not expect signs. She had buried the pendant and the figure and she had said Taya's name three times and the earth was the earth and the morning was the morning and she was cold and her knees hurt and her hands were still dirty and she was going to walk down the slope now and go home.

She picked up her cloak where it had slipped from one shoulder and settled it back. She looked once more at the disturbed patch of soil. Already the light was changing it, the slanted gold flattening to something cleaner. In a year it would be a small rise — barely more than the rise was already, but slightly more. In ten years, perhaps more than that.

She did not know this. She was not thinking about ten years. She was thinking about the walk down the slope and whether the fire had gone low while she was gone and whether the goats had been fed.

She turned and began to walk down.

The wet grass bent under her feet and sprang back. The settlement sounds grew as she descended — the child's voice resolved into words she still could not quite make out, and a dog barked twice and stopped, and she could smell the cookfires now, the particular smell of morning food, and her stomach answered. She had not eaten. She would eat. She would go home and eat and tend the fire and the day would continue, and the pendant would be in the ground, and Taya's name would be in the ground, and she would carry on being the woman who knew where it was.

That was enough. That was what she had come for.

She did not know she had started something. She was only trying to keep her daughter's name alive.

The sun moved west without asking her permission, the way it always did.

She had sat down beside the disturbed patch of soil meaning to rest for a moment before descending, and then she had not gotten up. The cold came up through the ground into her legs and she let it. The light changed from the flat brightness of midmorning to something more slanted, more amber, and the shadow she cast grew long across the rise, pointing east. She watched it lengthen. She did not pray. She did not speak. She had already said what needed saying, three times, to the earth that might or might not hear. There was nothing left to say and she was not a woman who filled silence with noise to prove she was still present.

The settlement below her went through its day. She could hear it in pieces — the knock of wood on wood, a woman's voice raised in what might have been argument or instruction, the same dog from earlier barking at something new and then going quiet again. Smoke thickened from the cookfires as the afternoon moved toward evening. She watched it rise and thin and disappear. A crow landed on the oak at the rise's eastern edge, looked at her with one yellow eye, decided she was not interesting, and left.

She was not thinking about Taya. She had thought about Taya all morning, coming up the slope and digging and placing the pendant and the figure and saying the name, and now she was not thinking about her. She was thinking about nothing in particular, which was its own kind of relief, like a hand that has been clenched a long time finally opening.

The pendant was in the ground. The carved antler figure was beside it. The name had been said.

She did not feel better. But there was a difference between what she had felt this morning and what she felt now, and the difference was that the name was somewhere outside of her. Before this morning, Taya's name had lived only in Yanu's chest, in the tight place below the breastbone where grief settles and does not move. Now it was also here, in this ground, in the slight rise that the community had always treated carefully without knowing exactly why — not plowing it, not building on it, bringing children to it at certain times of year and saying only *this place is old* without being able to say more than that. The name had gone somewhere that was not only inside her. That was different from better. She was trying to understand exactly how it was different, and she had not finished understanding it yet, and she did not think she would finish today.

The sun dropped another hand's width toward the tree line.

The light went amber, then deeper, and the shadow she cast merged with the longer shadow of the rise itself, and the settlement sounds below her changed — the particular shift that happened each evening when the day's work stopped and the fires were built up for the night, voices dropping in register, the rhythm of the place going slower. The river caught the last flat light and held it briefly, a long bright line, and then the light moved off the water and the river went grey.

She sat until the first star appeared above the eastern tree line, faint and then less faint as the sky darkened around it. Then a second, slightly south of the first. She watched them come out the way she always watched them, not looking directly but letting them appear at the edge of her vision, which was the only way to see the early ones.

She got to her feet slowly. Her knees ached from the cold and from sitting so long. She straightened and stood on the crown of the rise in the failing light and looked down at the settlement: the fires built up now, the smoke rising straight in the still air, the shapes of houses dark against the orange light.

She looked once more at the disturbed patch of soil. It was almost invisible in the dusk, only slightly darker than the ground around it, a shadow among shadows. She would not have been able to find it by looking if she had not known exactly where it was.

She pulled her cloak close and began to walk down the slope.

The grass was dry now where the dew had burned off during the day and the ground was harder underfoot than it had been this morning, and she went carefully, watching where she placed her feet in the fading light. The settlement sounds came up to meet her, the smell of cookfire and evening food and the particular smell of the river at dusk, cold and green and old.

Behind her, on the crown of the rise, the disturbed patch of soil was invisible in the dark. The pendant was in the ground. The carved antler figure was beside it. The name was there, in the earth, in the place the community had always treated carefully without knowing why.

She did not look back.

She walked down the slope and let the night come, and she did not know what she had started, and that not-knowing was its own kind of completion — the work done, the name placed, the earth holding what she had given it, or not holding it, and no way yet to know which.
