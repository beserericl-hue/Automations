The lamp beside Nora's chair was the only light on in the house. She had turned off the kitchen overhead at nine, the hallway at ten, and now the darkness pressed against the sewing room door like something patient. The LED bulb in the floor lamp threw a warm, flat yellow across the quilt frame, making the ochre threads glow and the navy ones go almost black.

Eleven o'clock. She knew without looking at the clock on the mantel.

She had not slept.

The quilt hung on its frame the way it always hung — the outer border facing out, the central panel folded inward against itself, the way you'd fold a letter you weren't ready to send. For thirty years she had worked on the edges first: the log cabin blocks in their rings of light and dark, the dusty mauve calicos from the early nineties gone soft at the seams, the clearer blues and greens she'd added in the 2010s when the old fabric shops stopped carrying the country colors she'd started with. Visitors who came to the house — Renata, sometimes Tayak, the women from the church circle — saw a large, unfinished quilt with a traditional pattern and said things like *beautiful* and *how long has that taken you* and she would say *a long time* and offer them coffee.

Nobody had seen the center.

She set her reading glasses on her nose and reached for the folded panel. The cotton was cool under her fingers, slightly stiff — she had not touched this part since March, when she'd washed the whole top and refolded it the same way. The fold line had gone pale, the fibers compressed into a crease she could feel with her thumbnail. She smoothed it flat with both palms, working from the center out, the way her grandmother Ida had taught her to press fabric without an iron.

*Use your hands*, Ida had said. *The heat is already in them.*

The central panel opened.

Nora sat back and looked at it.

Brown thread on cream cotton, and ochre thread in a slightly heavier gauge she'd wound from a half-used spool she'd bought in 1994 and never found again. The stitching was not decorative. Anyone looking for a pattern would find one — a kind of medallion, concentric shapes moving inward, the sort of thing you saw in old Welsh quilts or Hawaiian appliqué. But the shapes were not symmetrical. The outermost ring was irregular on its western edge, flattened, the way a hill flattens where a field machine has been run over it too many times. The next ring inward was narrower on the south side. The third ring had a break in it, a gap of two inches where she had left the cream cotton bare, and if you knew what you were looking for you understood that the gap meant an entrance — a passage, not a wall.

She had known what she was looking for since 1962.

She was twenty years old that autumn, sitting in Ida's kitchen on Billingsley Road — the same house Nora lived in now, the same road, different wallpaper — and Ida had said *I need to tell you something before I lose the words.* Ida was seventy-eight. She had been a schoolteacher for forty years and she chose her words the way some people chose clothes: carefully, for the occasion, with an awareness of how they would look from a distance. When she said *before I lose the words*, Nora understood it was not a small thing.

Ida had told her about the mound. Not the hill in Dawes's soybean field — Nora hadn't known then that it was Dawes's field, or anyone's field; it was just the high ground near the creek where her grandmother's grandmother had said the old ones were buried. Ida described the chambers the way you describe a house you have lived in: this room, then this room, then the deep one at the bottom where the first things were put. She said the story had come to her from her grandmother, who had it from her grandmother, who had it from a woman named Saya, who had been there.

*Been where?* Nora had asked.

*At the beginning*, Ida said. *At the putting-in.*

Nora had not understood then what she understood now. She had been twenty and she had listened and she had held it the way you hold something fragile when someone hands it to you unexpectedly — carefully, with both hands, afraid to move too fast.

She picked up her needle.

The thimble was on the side table — silver, her mother's mother's, the dimpled cap worn smooth on one side where it had pressed against the needle for sixty years before Nora inherited it and sixty more since. She fitted it onto her middle finger and found her place in the quilting line — a long, curving stitch that followed the outermost edge of the third chamber ring, the one with the entrance gap. She had been working this line for six weeks, moving around the ring in small sessions, an inch or two at a time. Quilting needles this short — a number ten between — required a rocking motion, the tip going down through all three layers and coming back up in one movement. Her grandmother Ida had said *stitches no bigger than a grain of rice* and Nora had spent sixty years trying to get there.

The thread pulled through with a soft scrape. The batting crinkled faintly as she shifted the frame.

She was thinking about the phone call.

Tayak's voice had been controlled — it was always controlled, that was the Moyaone discipline, Nora had watched it develop in the girl from age nine onward, that particular stillness that looked like calm and was actually something harder — but underneath the control there had been something Nora recognized. Tayak had found the 1697 petition. She had found Saya's name in a colonial record, and she was trying to understand what it meant, and she had called her grandmother because she did not know where else to call.

Nora had said *yes* and *I see* and *let me think on it* and then she had put the phone down and sat in the kitchen for forty minutes without moving.

She was not ready.

That was the truth of it, and she had been living inside it for so long that she had stopped examining the shape. Not ready to say: *I know what you'll find at each layer.* Not ready to say: *I have known since I was twenty years old.* Not ready to say: *Ida told me, and her grandmother told her, and the story goes back to a woman who walked two days in winter to file a petition that failed, and then buried the proof in the ground so that someone would find it later, and the someone is you.*

Not ready to say what it would cost.

She pushed the needle through the cream cotton and felt it catch on the batting — a thread of polyester fill snagged on the needle's eye — and she worked it free with her thumbnail and started again. The lamp hummed. It did not hum, actually; LED bulbs did not hum, she had learned that when she replaced the old incandescent two years ago. But she heard something in the silence that felt like a hum, the way a house makes sounds that are not quite sounds when you are alone in it at eleven o'clock.

Her phone was on the side table next to the thimble and the pin cushion.

She had picked it up three times since putting it down in the kitchen. Each time she had found Tayak's name in the recent calls — the call duration showing forty-one minutes, which surprised her; she had not thought they'd talked that long — and each time she had pressed the screen dark again and set it face-down.

She looked at the central panel. The third chamber ring, and below it — inward, toward the center of the medallion — the fourth, smaller, the stitching tighter because she had used a finer thread there, a long-staple cotton she'd bought in 2019 that was almost too fine for hand quilting but was the closest color to the thread Ida had described: *the color of old copper, she said, like a penny left in the rain.*

There was something buried at the fourth level. Ida had been specific about this. Not a bead — the bead was higher, in the second chamber — but something older. Something that had been put in the ground before the petition, before Saya, before the names Nora knew.

Tayak did not know about the fourth level yet.

Nora set her needle in the pin cushion and looked at the phone.

She was eighty-two years old. She had been carrying this for sixty-two years. She had watched Tayak grow up and go to school and come back with degrees and start the excavation, and she had told herself: *when she is ready*. And then she had told herself: *when I am ready*. And the two readinesses had never quite aligned, the way two people can want the same thing at different times and keep missing each other in the doorway.

She picked up the phone.

Tayak's name was at the top of the recent calls list, the call time showing 9:14 PM, the duration 41 min.

Nora's thumb hovered over the green circle.

The refrigerator compressor kicked on in the kitchen, a low mechanical shudder traveling through the walls of the old house, and she felt it in the frame of her chair the way she always felt it — like a reminder that the house was still running, still holding, still keeping things in their places.

She put the phone face-down on the side table.

Not tonight.

But she left it closer to her hand than it had been before, and she picked up her needle, and she began to stitch the line that ran along the edge of the fourth chamber — the one she had never shown anyone, the one that held the oldest thing — and her stitches were, if she was honest with herself, a little uneven tonight. A little larger than a grain of rice.

Ida would have noticed.

The memory came the way it always came: not invited, not announced, just suddenly there, the way a smell can open a door you thought was sealed.

Ida's kitchen. October of 1962, the leaves already down, the yard muddy from three days of rain, and the air through the open window carrying the particular sweetness of tobacco drying in the barns two miles east. Nora had been twenty years old and had driven herself from Waldorf in a borrowed Ford pickup with a cracked passenger-side mirror, and she had not known why Ida had asked her to come alone. She had assumed it was about the boy from St. Mary's County. It was not about the boy.

Ida had been seventy-eight. Nora had not understood, at twenty, what seventy-eight cost a body. She understood now. Ida's hands had a tremor that she managed by keeping them occupied — always a cup, a cloth, a task — and her hair had gone the color of creek water in winter, that particular silver-gray that holds no warmth. She was a schoolteacher. Had been a schoolteacher for forty years, in the two-room school on Billingsley Road before the county consolidated, and she carried herself the way teachers carry themselves when they have spent four decades being the person in the room who knows the answer. Straight. Deliberate. She did not waste words.

She had cornbread in the oven. Nora could smell the butter crisping on the cast-iron skillet's sides, and the particular sweetness of the meal — not sweet like sugar, sweet like grain, like something that had been growing in the ground six weeks before. She remembered sitting at the table and thinking she was hungry, and then Ida came in from the back room carrying a cloth.

Not a quilt. A flour-sack cloth, the kind that had been printed with a faint blue pattern — flowers, or diamonds, something that had washed nearly to nothing — and Ida spread it on the table between them without speaking. She smoothed it with both palms the way you smooth a map before you read it. Her trembling hands stilled for that moment. Nora had noticed that: how the hands stopped shaking when they had something to do.

Then Ida drew on the cloth with her finger. No pen, no chalk, no mark. Just her fingertip moving on the worn cotton, tracing shapes that Nora watched the way you watch someone write in a language you are just beginning to learn.

A circle. Then inside it, another circle. Then another, smaller. Then smaller still. Her finger moved inward, always inward, toward a point at the center that she did not mark but only touched — one small press of her fingertip, as if she were ringing a bell.

She said: *I need to tell it before I lose the words.*

That was the first thing she said. Not hello, not sit down, not I'm glad you came — though Nora had already been sitting for ten minutes and Ida had already given her coffee. Just that. *Before I lose the words.* And Nora understood, at twenty, in the way you understand things at twenty without fully knowing you understand them, that this was not about forgetting. Ida's mind was sharp as a tack, would stay sharp for four more years before the stroke. This was about something else. This was about the words being the kind of words that had to be passed to a specific person at a specific moment, and the moment was now, and the person was Nora, and if Ida did not say it today she might lose the nerve, which was different from losing the words but felt the same from the inside.

She named the circles.

*At the top*, she said, *there is a bead. Glass, blue. It belonged to a woman named Saya, who got it from her mother, who came south along the Potomac with a trading party and stayed.*

Nora had not asked how Ida knew this. She had been twenty, and Ida was Ida, and you did not interrupt Ida.

*Below the bead*, Ida said, *there is a document. Paper, or what is left of paper. Saya put it there. She walked two days in winter to give it to the colonial government and they took it and did nothing, so she buried the copy in the ground where at least the ground would keep it.* Ida's finger traced the second ring on the flour-sack cloth. *The ground keeps things*, she said, as if this were a fact she was adding to a lesson plan. *That is the one thing you can say for it.*

The cornbread smelled done. Ida did not move.

*Below the document*, she said, *there is a pipe bowl. Older. A man made it — or a woman, she was not certain — who kept two kinds of records. One for the English to read and one for the people who came at night.* Her finger on the third ring. *Below that, something she called the old gorget — a shell, carved — from a woman who wore it at her throat and argued with her brother about what it means to survive.* The fourth ring. *And below that—*

She paused. The cornbread smell had shifted from sweet to the edge of char, and neither of them moved.

*Below that*, Ida said, *something copper. Old copper, old as the mound itself, nearly. Put there by a woman whose name I do not know, only that she was grieving, and that she put the copper in the ground because she needed the ground to hold what she could not.* Her finger touched the innermost ring, the one just outside the center point. *And below even that—*

She pressed the center of the cloth.

*The first thing. The woman's thing. The one that started the keeping.*

Nora had said: *What is it?*

And Ida had looked at her across the flour-sack cloth with the expression she used in the schoolroom when a student asked a question whose answer she was not going to give directly.

*I don't know*, she said. *My grandmother didn't know. She said the woman who told her didn't know either. She said: the one who put it there knew, and she went into the ground with it, and now only the ground knows, and maybe someday someone will ask the ground properly.*

The oven was smoking. Ida got up, unhurried, and pulled the cornbread out with a folded dish towel, and set it on the stovetop, and turned back to the table. The cloth was still spread between them. Nora had not touched it.

*Why me?* Nora asked. She had meant: why not her mother, why not one of the older women, why not someone who would know what to do with this. She had been twenty years old and she did not know what to do with it.

Ida sat back down. She folded her hands on the table, the tremor returning now that they had nothing to hold.

*Because your mother is gone*, she said. *And because you are the one who will know when it is time to tell it again.* She said it the way she said everything — not unkindly, not gently either, just as a fact that had been true before Nora arrived and would be true after she left. *You will know the person. When you see her, you will know.*

Nora had been twenty. She had driven home in the borrowed Ford with the cracked mirror and she had not written a single word of it down, because she had not needed to. It had gone in the way things go in when you are young and a woman you trust speaks to you across a flour-sack cloth in a kitchen that smells of cornbread and autumn grass. Tayak had been nine when she stopped expecting the phone to ring. She had been nine when the leaving became permanent, when the slow recession reached its end, when the ground went dry.

She picked up her reading glasses from the side table and held them without putting them on. The LED lamp made the lenses look flat, bluish, the prescription ground into glass by a machine that did not know whose eyes they were for.

Tayak did not know what she was excavating. That was the thing. She knew the land, she knew the strata, she knew the artifacts as artifacts — the bead, the document, whatever came next — but she did not know yet that the ground she was digging was her own ground in the oldest sense. Her own family's ground. The mound was not a site. It was a grave and a record and an act of love, all three, and the woman who had made it had made it so that a name would not be forgotten.

That was the part Nora had never told anyone.

Ida had said it sitting at the kitchen table in the house on Billingsley Road, the same house, the same kitchen, though the table was different now and the window over the sink had been replaced twice. Ida had been seventy-eight years old. Nora had been twenty. It was October of 1962, the leaves already down, the yard muddy from three days of rain, and Ida had said: *I need to tell you this before I lose the words. I am starting to lose the words in the mornings.*

She had told Nora the story of Saya. The petition. The bead. The burial. Nora had known some of it already — the shape of it, the outline — because Ida had told pieces of it over the years, the way you tell a child pieces of a thing before you tell them the whole. But that October she had told it whole, the first time, sitting at the kitchen table with her hands around a mug of chamomile tea gone cold.

And then she had said the other thing.

*It was not only about the land*, she had said. *The land was why she made the petition. But the burial — the bead, the oilskin, all of it — that was for her daughter. The daughter's name was not in the petition. The petition was for the living. The burial was for the girl who was already gone.*

Nora had not understood, at twenty, why that distinction mattered. She had understood it at thirty-one, the spring her daughter left. She had understood it more completely each year since, the understanding deepening the way a stain deepens in cloth — not spreading, just becoming more itself, more saturated, more permanent.

Saya had buried the bead so her daughter's name would not be forgotten. She had put it in the ground and covered it with earth and the earth had kept it for two hundred and fifty years, three hundred, longer — kept it through the colonial surveys and the land grants and the tobacco crops and the soybeans, through everything that had been done to that ground — and it was still there. Tayak had found it. The ground had kept it until the right hands came.

Nora set her glasses on the table.

She was eighty-two years old.

She thought about that number the way she rarely let herself think about it — not as an abstraction, not as a thing other people said about her, but as a fact with physical weight. Eighty-two winters. She had arthritis in both hands and a hip that ached in cold weather and a cardiologist she saw every six months who said things like *well-managed* and *for your age* in a tone that was meant to be reassuring. She slept less than she used to. She woke at three in the morning sometimes and lay in the dark listening to the house and could not always remember, for a moment, what decade it was.

She did not know how many more winters she had.

She had been telling herself she was waiting for the right moment. The right moment had been 2019, when Tayak had first brought her the permit application and sat at this same table and spread the survey maps across it, and Nora had looked at the coordinates and felt the floor shift under her feet, felt sixty years of waiting arrive all at once like a wave she had been watching build from a long way off. She had not spoken then. She had said *that's good, baby* and poured more coffee and let Tayak roll the maps back up and go.

She had told herself: not yet. She had told herself: when she finds the bead. When she finds the document. When she reaches the copper layer. Each threshold a reason to wait a little longer, to hold the story a little longer, to be sure.

But she knew, sitting in the quiet of the room with the folded quilt on the arm of the chair and the phone dark on the sewing table, that the waiting had not been entirely about Tayak's readiness.

It had been about her own.

Because telling the story meant opening the room with the closed door. The whole story — the bead, the burial, the daughter's name — could not be told without telling why Nora understood it the way she did. Could not be told without her daughter in it. And her daughter was the room she did not open.

Tayak was her daughter's child. That was the thing that sat at the center of everything, the blank circle, the space she had left without thread. Tayak was the daughter of the woman who had left, and Nora had raised her, and she had never once said to Tayak: *your mother buried something in me when she left, and I have been keeping it the way the ground keeps things, and I do not know yet if keeping it has been care or cowardice.*

She did not know which it had been.

She picked up the phone. The screen lit at her touch — no messages, no missed calls from Tayak, just the time in the upper corner and a news alert she had already dismissed. Her own name was in Tayak's contacts, she knew, listed as *Grandma Nora*, and when Nora called, Tayak's phone would show that name and Tayak would answer the way she always answered, the slight steadying in her voice, the attention that came up like a light.

She held the phone for a moment.

Then she set it face-down on the table.

Not tonight. She was not ready tonight. She had thought she was getting closer to ready, and perhaps she was, but tonight she had folded the panel over and touched the place where her daughter lived and found she was not there yet. She needed one more thing to happen first. She needed Tayak to find the copper herself, without being told, so that when Nora spoke it would be confirmation and not direction. So that the story would arrive the way stories were supposed to arrive — not pushed, not forced, but recognized. The way you recognize a face you have always known.

The heating vent clicked and the warm air came on again, the soft rush of it moving through the room, lifting the edge of a paper on the sewing table and settling it back.

Nora looked at the folded quilt on the arm of the chair.

Her daughter had seen this quilt once, in its early years, when it was only a few blocks wide and the central panel was barely begun. She had picked up the edge of it and looked at the stitching and said *Mama, this is going to take forever* in the voice she used when she meant something she couldn't say directly. Nora had said *I know* and taken it back and kept working. She had been working it for thirty years since. Her daughter had not seen it again.

She thought: *I am eighty-two years old, and I do not know how many winters I have, and the story has been in my mouth for sixty-two years, and if I die with it in my mouth it will be lost.*

That was the fear she had not let herself say aloud. Not that Tayak wasn't ready. Not that the moment wasn't right. The fear was simpler and older and heavier than either of those: that she would run out of time. That she would wait for the perfect moment and the perfect moment would not come before she ran out of winters, and the chain would break at her link, and Ida's voice would be the last voice to have carried this, and after Nora it would be silence.

She had been afraid of this for years. She had been managing the fear by not looking directly at it, the way you don't look directly at the sun — you look at what it illuminates instead, you look at the ground it lights.

But tonight she had looked.

She sat with it for a moment, the fear and the folded quilt and the dark phone and the name of her daughter that she did not say aloud.

Then she stood, slowly, her hip complaining in the way it always complained when she'd been sitting too long, and she turned off the lamp.

In the dark, the phone's screen stayed dark. Tayak had not called.

*You will know when it is time*, Ida had said. Not *you will feel ready*. Not *you will be sure*. Only: *you will know.*

Nora had known since 2019. She had been waiting to be willing to pay what knowing cost.

She thought she was almost there.

She went down the hall to bed, one hand on the wall in the dark, the house quiet around her, the quilt folded on the arm of the chair in the room she had left behind, its center hidden, its thread waiting, the blank circle at its heart still without color.

Tomorrow, she thought. She would find the thread tomorrow.

She found the thread that night.

She had told herself tomorrow, but she was not in bed five minutes before she was back in the doorway of the sewing room, the hall light making a yellow stripe across the floor, looking at the quilt on the arm of the chair.

The thread she needed was in the second drawer of the sewing table, in a small plastic bag she had sealed and put away two years ago. Aurifil, 50-weight, a spool she had ordered online after the permit was filed and then set aside because she had not been ready to use it. The color was called Old Gold on the label. She had chosen it because it was the closest she could find to the color Ida had described: the color of the pendant, the color of a thing that had been in the ground for a very long time and had come back up warm.

She sat down in the chair. She did not turn on the overhead light. She switched on the small lamp on the side table, the one with the LED bulb that cast its flat, even light across the quilt's surface, and she put on her reading glasses — the drugstore pair, +2.25, which she kept on a cord around her neck for exactly this — and she unfolded the central panel across her lap.

Ten pounds of cotton and batting and thirty years of her hands. It settled over her like something alive.

She threaded the needle without looking. She had done it ten thousand times. The betweens needle, size ten, short and fine, the eye just wide enough for the thread to pass through without fraying. She wet the thread end between her lips, flattened it, pushed it through. Her thimble was on the side table where she always left it — the silver one, her mother's mother's — and she put it on.

The central panel lay open across her knees. In the flat LED light, the pattern was almost invisible — the stitching done in thread so close to the fabric's color that you had to know where to look. She knew where to look. She had put every stitch there herself.

The map was not labeled. There were no words in it, no compass rose, no scale. It was a pattern of concentric shapes — not quite squares, not quite circles — that spiraled inward toward a center point, with three lines radiating out from that center at angles that corresponded, if you knew what you were looking at, to the three drainage channels that ran down from the high ground to the creek. The outermost ring was stitched in the same navy thread as the border's decorative line, invisible unless the light hit it sideways. The innermost point — the center of the center — was a small, dense knot of stitching, no bigger than a thumbnail, that she had made in 1994 and had not touched since.

She had been told what was there. Not shown. Told, in words, by Ida, in a kitchen in October of 1962, the two of them at a table with a pot of coffee going cold between them, Ida's hands flat on the oilcloth, her voice steady and deliberate, the way a person speaks when they are saying something for the last time and they need it to land correctly.

*At the bottom*, Ida had said. *At the very bottom, before the mound was a mound at all. There is something there that belonged to the woman who started all of it. The woman who first understood what the ground could hold.*

Nora had been twenty years old. She had not known what to do with it. She had carried it for sixty-two years, turning it over, adding to it what she learned, waiting.

She placed the needle against the border of the central panel, in the section she had last worked two years ago. The fabric was a deep ochre — not the Old Gold thread, which would go on top, but the base cloth beneath, a solid cotton she had chosen because it was the color of the earth at that particular site in a dry summer, the color of the ground before it gives anything up.

She pushed the needle through.

The thread pulled. The thimble pressed. The stitch was small — no bigger than a grain of rice, Ida would have said, no bigger than a grain of rice or it won't hold — and she drew it tight and felt the fabric gather slightly and then release.

She made a second stitch. Then a third.

Three stitches. That was all. That was what she had decided, in some part of herself that had made the decision without consulting the rest of her: that she would add to this quilt only when something happened at the site, and only as much as the thing warranted, and that she would finish the border of the central panel only when Tayak reached the bottom layer. When the bottom was open, she would finish the border. When the border was finished, she would tell Tayak everything she knew.

That was the agreement. She had made it with herself the day the permit was filed, standing in this room, holding the folded quilt, understanding that the excavation and the quilt were the same project now — that they had always been the same project, and she had just not been willing to say so.

She tied off the thread. She cut it close with the small scissors on the side table, the ones with the stork handles that had belonged to Ida.

She sat for a moment with her hands in her lap.

The phone was on the sewing table, face-down where she had left it. She did not pick it up. Tayak had not called again, and Nora had not called, and that was right. That was how it needed to be tonight.

She took off her reading glasses and let them hang on the cord against her chest. She folded the central panel back over, the map hidden again inside the quilt's bulk, and she set the whole weight of it back on the arm of the chair the way she always left it — not put away, not finished, just resting.

She turned off the lamp.

The room went dark. The refrigerator in the kitchen cycled on, its low hum traveling through the walls. Outside, a car passed on Billingsley Road, its headlights sweeping briefly through the window and across the ceiling and gone.

Nora stood in the dark.

She said a name.

Not Tayak's name. Not her daughter's name, the name she did not let herself say. A different name, older, the one Ida had given her at the end of that long afternoon in October of 1962, pressing it into her hands the way you press a seed into soil — not knowing if it will come up, only knowing it must be kept.

She had said this name aloud perhaps a dozen times in her life. Always alone. Always in the dark, or close to it. It was not a secret, exactly — it was something more careful than a secret. A secret is something you keep from others. This was something she kept for them. The distinction mattered.

She said it quietly, once, to the dark room and the folded quilt and the phone she had not used and the three new stitches in the ochre border.

Then she went down the hall to bed, one hand on the wall, the house holding its breath around her. She did not look back at the sewing room.

The phone lay on the table in the dark, its screen cold, Tayak's name somewhere in its memory, waiting.

The quilt waited too.

The name she had spoken spread out into the room's silence and was absorbed by it, the way sound is always absorbed — not destroyed, only changed, only carried forward in a different form, the way every voice that had ever said that name had carried it forward, one mouth to the next, across two thousand years of dark rooms and dying fires and the particular courage of people who understood that some things must be spoken aloud, even when no one is listening, so that the speaking itself becomes the act of keeping.

She had done her part tonight.

Tomorrow, Tayak would go back to the ground.
