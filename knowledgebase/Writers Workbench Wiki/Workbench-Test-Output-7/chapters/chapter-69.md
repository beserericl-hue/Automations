The mound had been Taya's for one winter.

Yanu had not gone back to it every day. She had meant to — had walked the path to the rise a dozen times in her mind before sleeping — but the world had not stopped for Taya's death, and the world's not stopping had been its own instruction. There was grain to grind and water to carry and the cold to get through, and Yanu had gotten through it by keeping her hands full. This was not courage. It was the oldest human method.

When she did go back, in the first thaw, the ground above the pendant had settled. The small depression she had pressed flat with both palms had filled with leaf litter and a crust of ice that was melting now into the dark soil. She knelt and pressed her hand to it and felt the cold come up through her palm, and she said Taya's name once, and then she stood and went back to the settlement before she could say it again.

She went back in the warm weeks, and again when the first green things pushed through the clay at the mound's base. Each time she said the name. Each time the ground held it without giving anything back, which was the right response, which was what the ground was for.

She was not watching the mound on the morning she first noticed the comb.

She had come to gather the long grass that grew on the mound's southern slope — it dried well, and she had a use for it — and she was moving around the base with her hands full when she saw it. A bone comb, small enough to sit in a closed fist, set at the mound's foot where the soil darkened from the seep of winter water. It had been placed with some care. The teeth pointed toward the rise, not away from it. Someone had pressed it into the soft earth just enough to hold it upright.

Yanu put down the grass.

She crouched and looked at the comb without touching it. The bone was old — not ancient, but not new either. Worked smooth on the spine, the teeth cut evenly with a small blade. Someone had owned this and used it and then set it here deliberately, and the deliberateness was in every detail: the angle, the pressure, the position at the mound's foot rather than on top of it, which would have been the wrong place for something that was not yet ready to go under.

She stood up. She looked around. The path from the settlement was empty. The oaks at the mound's north edge were loud with a pair of jays, but there was nothing else moving.

She picked up her grass and went home.

She did not ask who had left the comb. She told herself this was because she did not want to make the person feel they had done something wrong, but the truth was more complicated than that, and she knew it. The truth was that she was not certain the comb was wrong. The mound had Taya in it, and the pendant, and Yanu's grief, and the name spoken over the earth. These were real things in a real place. If someone else had a grief that needed a place — if someone else had a name that needed somewhere to go — she did not know by what right she would turn them away.

She had not built the mound. She had used it the way her mother had told her it could be used, and her mother had told her the way someone before had told her mother, and the mound had been there before any of them. It was a rise in the earth above the river terrace. It had been a rise in the earth when Yanu was a child, and it would be a rise in the earth when no one alive could remember Taya's name. The mound did not belong to Yanu. She had only used it first.

This was the thought she was thinking when she saw the woven fiber.

It was late in the same season, the dry weeks before the rains came back. She had gone to the mound in the early morning when the air still smelled of night — of damp bark and the cold that came off the river before the sun reached it — and there, on the western face, a strip of fiber had been laid against the mound's skin. Woven in a pattern she recognized as the kind of work the older men did when they had time and steady hands: a dark thread through a light one, tight and even. It was not long — the length of a forearm — and it had been pressed into the grass so that the grass held it down, which meant it had been placed with weight behind it, someone kneeling and pressing, the way you press something you want to stay.

Yanu stood at the mound's eastern side and watched it from a distance.

The strip of fiber lay still in the early light. The grass around it was already dry, the seed heads pale and bent. The soil at the mound's base was cracked from the dry weeks, the cracks running in short lines like the lines on an old hand. Where the comb had been — she could still see the small hollow — the earth had closed around it, and a thin stem of something was growing up through the disturbed ground, opportunistic and green.

A man had set that fiber down. She had not seen him, but she knew. She knew the pattern of the weave, and she knew the household it came from, and she knew that his wife had died in the cold months, quietly, in the way that people died when the cold found a weakness and the weakness gave way. She had not gone to his household after. She had not known what to say. She was not his family and she had no particular claim on his grief, and so she had stayed away, which was the wrong thing, which she had known was the wrong thing, and had done anyway.

He had come here instead.

He had come here and laid his fiber down and pressed it into the grass with his hands, and he had not asked her, and she had not been here to ask, and the mound had received it the way the mound received everything: without comment, without permission granted or withheld, simply by being there.

Yanu sat down on the ground at the mound's eastern base. The soil was dry and warm from the previous day's sun, and small pebbles pressed through the back of her tunic. She could smell the crushed grass where she had sat — dry and faintly sweet, the smell of late summer doing its last work before the rains. She sat there for a long time. The jays were back in the oaks. A dog barked once from the direction of the settlement and then was quiet.

She was trying to understand what had changed, and the difficulty was that she could not find the moment of change. The mound had been a rise in the earth. She had put Taya in it. The comb had appeared. The fiber had appeared. No one had asked her anything. No one had done anything wrong. The place had simply — she turned the word over and could not find a better one — the place had simply become what it was. As if it had always been moving toward this, and her act of burial had only made it visible.

She pressed her hand flat against the mound's face the way she had pressed it on the day she buried the pendant. The soil was warm. Beneath the dry surface the clay held its cold, and she could feel both at once — the warmth from above, the cold from below — and between them the ground that held everything she had put there.

She left her hand there until the warmth won.

Then she stood, brushed the dry soil from her tunic, and walked back to the settlement without looking back. She did not look back because she did not need to. She knew what was there. She knew what it was becoming. She had not made it and she could not stop it, and she was not sure she would have stopped it if she could.

The mound was behind her, holding its names in the dark.

The morning she found the three strangers, she had come early — before the settlement fires were lit, before the dogs had roused themselves from sleep — because she had not been sleeping well and the river air was better than the inside of her house, which smelled of old smoke and the particular staleness of a woman lying awake in the dark.

She smelled the mound before she saw it. Or rather she smelled something at the mound: a faint sweetness, resinous, the kind of smell that came from pine boughs freshly broken. She slowed without deciding to slow. Her feet found the path — the worn strip of bare earth that had not been there a year ago, that had appeared the way paths appear, through nothing more than going — and she followed it up the low rise.

They were on the western face. Three of them: a woman and two children, the children small enough that they knelt without effort, their knees finding the ground the way children's knees do, without complaint. The woman's hair was loose and unpinned, hanging forward so that Yanu could not see her face. Her tunic was dark, or darkened — damp at the hem from walking through the wet grass before the sun had dried it. She was pressing something into the soil with both hands, slowly, with the full weight of her upper body behind it. The children did not move. They knelt on either side of her and watched her hands.

Yanu stopped at the mound's eastern edge.

The woman did not look up. The children did not look up. They had not heard her, or they had heard her and had decided that what they were doing was more important than who was watching, which was the same thing.

Yanu stood and watched.

She recognized the household. The woman was from upriver — three bends north, where the bank flattened and two families had built together in the years after the flooding. Yanu knew her face but not her name, which was the kind of knowing you had with people close enough to nod to and too far to visit. She knew that the household had lost someone in the past season. She had heard it from Ranu, who heard everything, who collected grief the way he collected everything else and brought it home and set it down and turned it over in the telling. A child, Ranu had said. A boy. She had not asked more.

The woman pressed the object — Yanu could not see what it was, only the shape of the woman's hands around it — into the soil and held it there. Then she sat back on her heels and was still. The children were still beside her. The three of them made a shape against the mound's face that Yanu could not look at for long without her chest tightening.

She did not move. She did not announce herself. She stood at the eastern edge and let the morning go on around her: the first birds beginning in the oaks, a thin column of smoke rising from somewhere in the settlement below, the river making its flat sound in the distance. The grass at the mound's crown was pale with dew. The pine boughs — she could see them now, two of them, laid crosswise at the mound's base near where the woman knelt — were dark green against the dry late-summer grass, and the smell of them came to her in the light air, clean and slightly bitter.

She thought: I should go.

She thought: This is not mine to interrupt.

She thought, with a clarity that surprised her: it was never only mine.

The woman from upriver finally lifted her head. She looked at the mound's face, not at Yanu. Her expression was not readable from this distance — Yanu was too far, and the light was still coming in low and sideways, catching the dew on the grass but not fully illuminating faces. The woman said something. It was too quiet to carry across the mound's width, but Yanu could see the movement of her mouth. She said it once. Then she put her hand flat on the earth where she had pressed the object in, and held it there.

Yanu recognized the gesture. She had made it herself. She had not taught it to anyone.

One of the children — the smaller one, the one on the left — put her hand flat on the earth beside the woman's hand. A small hand, the fingers spread wide in unconscious imitation. The woman did not look at her. The child did not look at the woman. They both looked at the ground.

Yanu's throat closed.

She stood there until the woman stood, and gathered the children, and began to walk back down the western slope without looking in Yanu's direction. Whether she had not seen Yanu or had seen her and chosen not to acknowledge her, Yanu could not tell. It did not matter. The woman walked down the slope and the children walked with her, the smaller one still trailing her hand along the mound's face as she went, fingers dragging through the dry grass, and then they were past the base and on the path that led back north along the river, and the sound of them faded.

Yanu crossed to the western face.

The object was not visible. The woman had pressed it in well, and the soil had taken it. There was only a slight disturbance in the surface, the grass bent and the dry soil darker where hands had worked it. The pine boughs lay crossed at the base, already beginning to dry at the cut ends, the needles still supple. Yanu did not touch them. She stood and looked at the place where the woman's hands had been.

She did not know the object. She did not know the boy who had died. She did not know whether the woman had come here because she had heard about the pendant, or because she had heard about the comb, or because she had simply walked until she found a rise in the earth that felt like the right place to stop. Any of those reasons would have been enough. All of them were the same reason.

The mound's surface was more layered than it had been a year ago. She could see it now that she was looking: the grass grew differently in the places that had been disturbed and resettled, shorter and more sparse, the new growth coming up between the old stems in thin green lines. The soil at the base was pocked with small depressions where the dry weeks had cracked it and the cracks had been filled, intentionally or by rain, with fine loose dirt. The western face showed a faint path worn into the grass — not the path she used, which came from the east, but a separate one, coming up from the north side, the direction of the river households.

Someone had been coming here more than once.

She had not known. She had been coming from the east and they had been coming from the north and the mound had been receiving both of them without telling either one about the other, which was either indifferent or generous, and she was not sure there was a difference.

She sat down on the western face, in the place where the woman had knelt. The soil was warm from the woman's hands, or she imagined it was. The pine smell was stronger here, the broken resin sharp in the back of her throat. A small beetle moved through the grass near her knee, purposeful, going somewhere.

She pressed her own hand flat against the earth.

She had thought, when she buried the pendant, that she was doing something private. She had thought that grief was private, which it was, and that the place of grief was private, which it was not. These were not the same thing. She understood that now, sitting in the warm depression of another woman's grief, her hand on the same soil.

She did not know whether what she felt was loss.

The mound had been hers in the way that a word is yours when you are the first to say it — for a moment, only for a moment, and then it belongs to everyone who needs it. She had said it first. She had buried Taya and spoken the name and pressed her hand to the earth, and the mound had held that, and it held this too, and it would hold whatever came next, and none of that required her permission or her presence or her grief to be the only grief the earth had ever taken in.

She did not know whether that was a loss.

She thought of Taya's face. She could still do that — summon it with precision, the particular way Taya's eyes went when she was deciding something, the small serious line between her brows. She could still do that. The mound did not change that. The woman from upriver did not change that. The comb, the fiber strip, the pine boughs pressed into the soil — none of it touched what Yanu held inside her own chest, which was Taya, which was only Taya, which no amount of shared earth could dilute.

She lifted her hand from the soil. There was dry dirt in the creases of her palm and under two of her nails. She did not brush it off.

She thought: the mound is not smaller for being shared.

She thought: I am not smaller for being one among others.

She sat there while the sun came fully up and the dew burned off the grass and the settlement below woke fully into its noise — dogs, children, the flat crack of wood being split, someone calling a name she could not make out. The pine boughs beside her began to lose their smell as the morning heat took the moisture from them. The beetle had gone.

She was not sure, even sitting there with the sun full on her back, whether what she felt was loss or gift. She turned it over the way you turn a stone to see what lives under it, and what she found was that it was neither, or it was both, which was the same as saying the question was wrong. The mound was not hers to lose. The mound was not hers to give. It was a place that grief had found useful, the way water finds low ground — not because anyone directed it, but because that is what grief does, and that is what low ground is for.

She stood. The soil fell from her tunic in a small dry shower.

She looked at the mound's western face: the pine boughs, the faint path worn from the north, the disturbed soil where the woman's hands had been, and below all of it, beneath the grass and the dry clay and the dark earth, the pendant, which was Taya, which was the name she had spoken, which was the first thing this place had ever held.

She was still the first.

That was not nothing.

She walked back east along the path she knew, and behind her the mound held its morning shadow, and the pine boughs dried in the sun, and the small hollow where the woman's object lay settled further into the earth, and the grass bent in the river wind and straightened and bent again, and the place went on being what it had become.

The girl came to her at midmorning, three days after the pine boughs.

Yanu was scraping a deer hide stretched between two posts at the edge of the settlement, working the bone tool in long strokes from the center outward, the way her mother had shown her. The hide was stiff and the morning was warm and she had been at it long enough that her shoulder ached. She heard the girl before she saw her — not footsteps but the particular quality of someone waiting, the held breath of someone who has rehearsed a question and is deciding whether to ask it.

She turned.

The girl was perhaps seventeen. Small-framed, with dark hair pulled back and fastened with a piece of cord, though several strands had come loose and hung against her jaw. She wore a plain wool tunic that had been washed many times, the color of it faded from whatever it had been to something between gray and brown. She was holding something in both hands, pressed against her stomach — a small clay cup, the kind made for drinking, ordinary, the rim uneven where the potter had not taken care. Her eyes were dry. That was the worst of it. The eyes of someone who has been crying for so long that crying has used itself up.

Yanu set down the bone tool.

The girl did not speak immediately. She looked at the ground to one side of Yanu's feet, then up, then at the hide, as if she needed something to look at that was not Yanu's face.

"I went to the rise," she said. "The one above the river path."

Yanu waited.

"I saw where someone had been." The girl shifted the cup in her hands. "I saw the pine boughs. And the path coming from the north." She paused. "I knew it was yours. People said."

Yanu had not known people said. She felt something move in her chest — not quite discomfort, not quite satisfaction. She let it pass.

"What do you say," the girl said, "when you put something in the earth?"

There it was. The question she had not known anyone would ask.

Yanu looked at the cup in the girl's hands. The clay was local, the color of the riverbank clay, which fired to a dull ochre. Someone had made it quickly or carelessly, or both. It was not a beautiful cup. It was a cup that had been used and would not be used again, and the girl was holding it the way you hold something that has already become something other than what it was.

She thought about what to say. She thought about her mother, who had said something at her father's burial, something Yanu could not now remember exactly, only the sound of it, the particular falling rhythm of the words. She thought about her grandmother, who had brought her to this same rise when Yanu was young and had said almost nothing — only stood beside her and let the place speak. She thought about the women of the settlement who sang over the newly dead, the old songs whose words had worn smooth from use, the ones nobody remembered learning because they had always known them.

She discarded all of it.

"You say the name," she said.

The girl looked at her. Her hands tightened slightly on the cup.

"Just the name?"

"The name is everything."

The girl was quiet. She was thinking — Yanu could see it, the small movement behind her eyes, the same movement Taya used to make when she was working something out, and Yanu felt the familiar sharp thing in her sternum that was not pain exactly but lived where pain lived.

"My son," the girl said. She did not say anything else. She did not need to.

Yanu nodded once.

"You say the name," she said again. "You say it to the earth. You press your hands flat, so it can hear you." She paused. "That is all."

"That is all there is?"

"That is all the ceremony there has ever been."

The girl looked down at the cup. She turned it once in her hands, the uneven rim catching the light, and then she looked back up at Yanu with the dry eyes and the question already answered in them, and she said, "Thank you," and she went.

Yanu watched her go — across the open ground, past the edge of the settlement, up the slope through the dry grass toward the rise. The girl did not look back. She walked the way people walk when they have decided something: not quickly, not slowly, but with the particular steadiness of someone who knows where they are going and has stopped arguing with themselves about it.

Yanu picked up the bone tool. She turned back to the hide. She worked the tool in long strokes, the way her mother had shown her, center outward, and she listened without meaning to — for what, she could not have said. The sound of the girl's footsteps had already been swallowed by the settlement noise, the dogs, the children, the flat crack of wood being split somewhere behind the nearest house.

She did not follow. She did not need to.

She knew what was happening on the rise. The girl was kneeling in the dry grass. She was pressing her hands flat to the earth, feeling the warmth the sun had put into the clay. She was holding the cup — the ordinary cup, the used cup, the cup that had already become something else — and she was opening her mouth, and she was saying the name.

One name. Just the one. The name that was everything.

Yanu worked the hide. The bone tool scraped and pulled and the hide gave a little more with each stroke, loosening toward softness. The sun was high enough now that her shadow fell directly beneath her, small and dark, and the heat pressed on the back of her neck and on her shoulders, and somewhere above the settlement, on the rise above the river path, the earth received another name, and held it, and was not changed by it, and was entirely changed by it, and both of those things were true at once, and the grass bent in the river wind and straightened, and the cup went into the earth, and the name went with it, and the mound — which was not yet called anything, which had no name of its own — became, without anyone deciding it, the place where names were kept.

That evening Ranu came and sat beside her at the fire.

He had heard. Of course he had heard. Ranu heard everything — not because he listened harder than other people but because he was always nearby, always at the edge of things, the way a good dog is always at the edge of things, and the settlement was small, and the girl with the cup had not been quiet about where she was going or why.

He sat for a while without speaking, which was unusual for him, and Yanu let him have his silence because she knew it would not last.

It did not last.

"I have been thinking," he said, "about what you told her."

"I know."

"The name is everything. That is good. That is exactly right." He paused, and she could hear him arranging something in his mind, the way you can hear a man pick up a tool and turn it over before he uses it. "But I think there should be words of approach."

Yanu said nothing.

"Something to say when you come to the rise," he said. "Before you kneel. A few words — not long, nothing like that. Something simple. So the place knows you are coming."

She looked at the fire.

"And then perhaps a gesture," he said. "Something for the hands. The way you told her to press them flat — that is good, that should stay. But before that. A way of holding the hands when you walk up, so the body is already in the right — the right —" He moved his own hands, demonstrating something she could not quite see in the firelight, his fingers arranged in a shape that seemed to mean something to him. "So the body arrives ready," he said.

She watched the fire.

"And then when you leave," he said, "there should be words of departure. Not long. Just something to close it. The way you close a door."

Yanu looked at him then.

He was watching the fire too, not her, his face open and absorbed in the way it got when he was building something in his mind — a story, a frame, a ceremony. She knew that face. She had known it for twenty years, the particular quality of his attention when he was adding rooms to a thing that did not need rooms.

She said, "You are adding walls to a door that already opens."

He was quiet.

The fire shifted. A piece of wood settled and sent up a brief column of sparks that rose and went dark before they reached the height of the nearest roof.

"Yes," he said. "I probably am."

She looked back at the fire. She felt the tightness in her chest ease by one degree — not because he had agreed, exactly, but because he had stopped. With Ranu the stopping was the thing. He could build endlessly if you let him, story on story, frame on frame, until the original thing was buried under its own elaboration. But he could also stop, if you caught him at the right moment, and when he stopped he stopped cleanly, without resentment, without the sulking she had seen in other men when their ideas were turned back.

She loved him for that. She had loved him for it for twenty years and she still did not have a better word for what it was — not patience, not humility, something between the two, something that had no name she knew.

"The girl did not need words of approach," she said, not to argue but because it was true. "She walked up the slope and she knew where she was going."

"Yes."

"She did not need words of departure. She came back down and she was different. The departure took care of itself."

"Yes."

"The ceremony is the name," she said. "Everything else is just — how you get there and how you leave. People know how to do those things. They have always known."

Ranu looked at the fire for a long moment. Then he said, "If it is too simple, people will add to it themselves. They will invent the approach words and the departure words and none of them will match."

Yanu considered this. It was not a foolish point. She knew what happened to simple things when many people handled them — she had watched it with the harvest prayers, which had been three words when her grandmother taught them and were now a full morning's recitation with variations by household.

"Let them," she said.

He looked at her.

"Let them add what they need," she said. "The name is the center. The center holds. What they put around it is theirs."

He was quiet again. She could see him testing it, the way he tested the weight of a story before he carried it — feeling for where it would bear and where it would give.

"You say the name," he said slowly. "You press your hands flat. That is all."

"That is all."

He nodded once. He had the look of a man who had set something down and found his hands lighter than he expected.

The fire had burned low while they talked. The settlement around them had gone quiet in the way it went quiet in the hour before full dark — dogs settling, children called in, the last sounds of work giving way to the sounds of people arranging themselves for the night. Somewhere to the north a woman was singing something low and formless, more breath than melody, the kind of sound you make when you are not singing for anyone.

Yanu watched the coals. They were the deep orange-red of the last heat, the color that looks solid but is almost gone.

Ranu reached over and put more wood on the fire without being asked. It was a small thing. He did it the way he did most things — without ceremony, without announcement, simply because it needed doing and he was there. The wood caught and the light came back up and her hands, which had been disappearing into the dark, were visible again, the knuckles dry and cracked from the bone tool, the dirt still worked into the lines of her palms from the afternoon.

She did not wash her hands before the fire.

She let the warmth reach them, and she thought about the girl on the rise, and the cup going into the earth, and the name going with it. She thought about Taya. She thought about the word she had said over the mound on the morning she buried the pendant, the single syllable of it, the way it had left her mouth and entered the air and then entered the earth, and how afterward the earth had been the same earth and also not the same earth.

She had not needed words of approach. She had not needed words of departure.

She had needed the name. She had said the name. The earth had received it.

That was all the ceremony there had ever been.

She would not always be here to say so. She understood that, sitting at the fire with Ranu beside her and the night coming on and the coals breathing their last orange heat beneath the new wood. She was not young. The mound was not young either, though it was younger than she was, younger than Taya would have been. Someday someone would stand on the rise and not know her name, and not know Taya's name, and not know who had first pressed their hands flat to the earth there and spoken into it.

But the girl knew. The girl with the ordinary cup and the dry eyes and the son she had not named aloud until she was alone on the rise in the dry grass with her hands pressed flat to the warm clay.

That girl would tell someone. Or she would not. Either way, the ceremony was what it was: the name, the hands, the earth. Simple enough to survive anything. Simple enough that you could not lose it by accident, could not bury it under elaboration, could not mistake it for something it was not.

You could only forget it entirely, and that was a different kind of loss, and she could not prevent it, and she had stopped trying to prevent the things she could not prevent.

Ranu said, "I will not add the approach words."

"Good."

"I may still think about them."

"You will think about them for the rest of your life," she said. "That is different from adding them."

He made a sound that was not quite a laugh and not quite agreement but contained both. She felt the familiar warmth of it, the warmth that was not the fire's warmth but lived in the same part of the body, somewhere below the ribs, somewhere that had been cold for a long time after Taya and had slowly, over years, come back to something that was not quite what it had been but was enough.

The new wood had caught fully now. The fire was bright again. The night pressed in around it, close and warm and smelling of wood ash and the last of the day's heat rising from the ground.

She leaned slightly against his shoulder.

He did not move, which was what she wanted.

The fire burned. The settlement breathed around them in its nighttime way. On the rise above the river path, in the dark, the earth held what it had been given, and held it without effort, the way the earth holds everything — without preference, without memory, without forgetting.

The summer that followed was dry enough that the river ran low and the grasses on the rise went pale by midsummer, the seed heads rattling in the afternoon wind off the water. Yanu walked up in the early morning when the air was still cool and the dew had not yet burned off, and she stood at the mound's southern face and listened to the river and thought about nothing in particular, the way you think about nothing when you are old enough to have learned that nothing is its own kind of fullness.

Taya's name was smooth in her mouth now. Worn the way a river stone is worn — not diminished, only finished. In the first years she had said it carefully, the way you carry something fragile. Now she said it the way you breathe, without deciding to, without effort, and it meant the same thing it had always meant, which was everything, and also it meant something quieter now, something closer to simply: *she was here*.

She was not counting the years. She had stopped counting the years the way she had stopped trying to prevent the things she could not prevent. The girl — the young woman now, the one who had come to the rise with her ordinary cup and her son's unspoken name — had a daughter of her own. The daughter was perhaps seven years old, perhaps eight, with the look of a child who has recently discovered that adults do not know everything and is still deciding what to do with the information.

Yanu saw them from the path.

She had not meant to stop. She was walking back from the river, a clay jar of water balanced against her hip, the strap of it cutting into her palm the way it always did, the familiar ache of it. She stopped because the girl — the woman — was kneeling on the rise with her daughter beside her, and the daughter was asking something, and the woman was answering, and Yanu heard it.

She stood on the path in the dry grass, the jar on her hip, the morning light coming low and flat across the rise and turning the pale grass gold, and she did not move.

The daughter had brought something. Yanu could not see what it was from the path — something small, held in both hands the way children hold things they have been told matter. The woman was showing her where to put it, pressing her own hands flat to the earth first, demonstrating the gesture, and the daughter watched and then imitated it, her small palms flat on the warm clay, her face very serious.

The woman said something. Her voice did not carry fully. Then the daughter said something — a question, the rising pitch of it clear even without the words.

Then the woman's voice, carrying now, distinct in the morning quiet:

"You say the name."

The daughter's voice, smaller, the question still in it:

"Just the name?"

And the woman, without hesitation, without looking up from the earth:

"The name is everything."

Yanu stood on the path.

The jar was heavy on her hip. The strap had cut a red line across her palm that she would feel for the rest of the day. The dry grass smelled of dust and the faint green smell of the river below, and somewhere behind her a dog was barking at something it had decided was worth barking at, and the morning was an ordinary morning, and she was an old woman on a path with a jar of water, and she had just heard her own words returned to her by someone who did not know where they had come from.

She had not told the woman where the words came from. She had said them once, on a morning years ago, when the woman had come to the rise with her son's name and her ordinary cup and her careful, dry-eyed grief. She had said them the way you say a thing that is simply true — not as instruction, not as ceremony, not as the beginning of anything. She had said them because they were what she knew.

And the woman had kept them. Had held them the way you hold a thing that fits your hand. Had passed them now to her daughter, kneeling in the dry grass on the rise above the river, with her daughter's small palms flat on the warm clay and her daughter's face very serious, learning the shape of a ceremony that had no other shape.

The daughter said the name. Yanu could not hear it. She did not need to hear it. The daughter's lips moved, and the name went into the air, and the woman beside her was still, and the earth received it, and that was all.

Yanu turned and walked back down the path toward the settlement.

The jar was heavy. She shifted it to her other hip. The path was worn smooth here, the grass pressed flat by years of feet, and she walked it without watching her feet, the way she had walked it for years, the way her body knew the path better than her mind did. The river was visible below through the scrub, low and slow in the summer heat, the surface flat and brown and unreflective in the morning light.

She thought: it has already outlived me.

Not as grief. Not as satisfaction either, exactly. As a fact, the way the river was a fact, the way the dry summer was a fact. The ceremony was what it was, and it was already moving through time without her, carried in the woman's mouth, now in the daughter's hands, and it would move further than she could see, through people she would never know, through mornings she would not be alive to walk through, and it would carry the name — not her name, not Taya's name specifically, but the idea of the name, the shape of the thing, the understanding that the name was everything and that everything else was approach words, and approach words were what you added when you were afraid the name was not enough, and the name was always enough.

She had not told anyone. She would not tell anyone. There was nothing to tell. The woman had not asked where the words came from, and Yanu had not offered it, and that was right. The words did not belong to Yanu. They had never belonged to Yanu. She had found them the morning she stood on the rise with the pendant in her hands and Taya's name in her throat and the earth waiting, and she had said what was true, and what was true did not belong to anyone.

The path curved down toward the settlement. She could smell the morning fires, the woodsmoke thick and familiar, and beneath it the smell of the river mud warming in the sun, and beneath that the smell of the earth itself, dry and mineral and old, the smell that was always there when you paid attention, the smell that the settlement was built on and the rise was built on and everything was built on.

She would be home before the fire needed feeding.

She walked the last of the path with the jar on her hip and the red line of the strap across her palm and Taya's name smooth and finished in her mouth, worn to its true shape, and the light came up over the rise behind her and the day began.
