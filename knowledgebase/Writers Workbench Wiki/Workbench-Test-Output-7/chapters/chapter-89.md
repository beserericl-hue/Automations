The office smelled of wet soil and acetone, and Wren had stopped noticing either.

She sat at the long worktable at 11:47 p.m. with the completed report stacked before her in two bound volumes, each one thick enough to stop a door. Volume I: Text and Analysis. Volume II: Plates and Artifact Catalog. Together they ran to 847 pages, and every one of those pages had passed through her hands at least three times. She knew their weight exactly. She had carried them from the printer room in two trips, holding each volume against her chest, and the cardboard corners had left red marks on her forearms that had not yet faded.

The title page was on top.

*Site 18CH-447: Archaeological Investigation of a Multi-Component Mound Site, Charles County, Maryland.*

*Principal Investigator: Dr. Tayak Moyaone*

*Field Assistant: W. Calvert*

She had typed Tayak's name first. That was the convention, and it was also the truth, and she had not needed to think about it for more than a moment before her fingers found the keys.

Her own name sat below, smaller, in the same font. *W. Calvert.* Not Wren, not Wren Calvert in full — just the initial and the surname, the way she had signed every field drawing for four months. The signature was a habit she had started in the second week, before she understood what the surname meant on this particular site, and now it felt like the only honest way to write it. The initial took up less space. The name itself was unavoidable.

The office was quiet except for the HVAC and the distant sound of the scanner in the next room, still running through the last of the artifact plates. She had set it going at nine-thirty and it had not finished. The machine made a low whirring sound and then a click, whirring and then a click, like a clock that had learned only two notes.

Wren's phone lay face-up beside the stack. Three texts from her mother, the most recent at 10:14 p.m.

She had not read them. She knew what they said because she knew her mother, and she did not need to read them tonight. Tonight was for the report.

She pulled Volume I toward her and opened it to the acknowledgments page. This was the part she had written last, at ten o'clock, after everything else was done — after the Harris matrix was cross-referenced against the context sheets for the fourth time, after the 3D stratigraphic profiles exported from Metashape were embedded and the scale bars checked against the originals, after the radiocarbon table was formatted and the artifact catalog proofed entry by entry, 51 entries, from the glass bead in stratum one to the copper pendant in stratum five, each one with its field number and provenience coordinates and Munsell color and Wren's own ink drawings reproduced at 1:1 scale.

The acknowledgments were seven lines.

She read them now, in the way you read something you wrote yourself — looking for the place where the words failed to carry what you meant.

*The author wishes to thank the Piscataway tribal council and the Moyaone family for their consultation and cooperation throughout this project. The field team — Dr. Tayak Moyaone, Delia Marsh, and Osei Asante — conducted this work with exactness and care. The geomorphological analysis by Dr. Priya Suresh (informal consultation) provided essential context for the base-layer interpretation.*

*The author also notes, for the permanent record, that the site designated 18CH-447 falls within the boundary of a 1687 land patent granted to Thomas Calvert by the Lords Baltimore. The author is a descendant of that family. This connection was disclosed to the principal investigator prior to the author's joining the field team and is recorded here in accordance with professional standards of conflict-of-interest disclosure.*

*The patent boundary description identifies the high ground adjacent to Mattawoman Creek as a Piscataway burial ground. The colonial government knew what this land was when it granted it.*

She had written the last sentence at 9:52 p.m. and had sat with it for eight minutes before she decided to keep it. It was not conventional acknowledgments language. It was not the careful, qualified prose she had used for the preceding 840 pages. It was a plain statement of fact, and it was the most important sentence in the document, and she had put it in the acknowledgments because that was the only place in a formal report where she was allowed to speak in her own voice.

She closed the volume.

Her hands were steady. She had checked twice, the way she checked things — once to know, once to be sure.

The scanner clicked and whirred. On the worktable beside the volumes, the artifact drawings were stacked in their protective sleeves: 51 sheets of bristol board, each one scanned at 600 dpi, each one signed in the lower right corner with a fine-point archival pen. *W. Calvert. 18CH-447.* She had drawn them all herself, working from the photographs and her own field sketches, using the conventions she had learned from Tayak's field notes — heavy line for the exterior edge, fine line for interior detail, stippling for texture, cross-section inset at lower left. The copper pendant had taken four hours. She had drawn it three times before the proportions were right, and she had thrown away the first two attempts and started again on fresh bristol board, because the pendant deserved to be drawn correctly.

She thought about the pendant now, sitting in its foam-lined tray in the conservation suite in College Park, in Adaeze's care. Artifact 18CH-447-051. Recovered from the base of stratum five at a depth of 1.42 meters below datum, face-up, oriented north-south. The copper had gone green with age, a specific green, the color of shallow water over dark stone. She had photographed it in situ with the metric scale and the context tag, and then she had photographed it again from three angles after Tayak lifted it, and then she had drawn it four times — three failures and one final version — and still she felt she had not quite gotten the weight of it right. You could not draw weight. You could only suggest it.

Her phone lit up. A fourth text from her mother.

She turned the phone face-down.

The thing about the acknowledgments — the thing her mother would not understand, or would understand perfectly and hate — was that it was not a betrayal. It was a record. The 1687 patent already existed. Thomas Calvert had already received it. The boundary description had already named the Piscataway burial ground as a survey marker, which meant the colonial government had already known what the land was and granted it anyway. All of that had happened. Wren had not done any of it. She had only written it down in a document that would go to the state historic preservation office and sit in the permanent site files alongside the permit application and the field logs and the 847 pages that documented, as precisely as she knew how, what the ground had held for two thousand years.

She was not taking anything from her family. She was not giving anything to Tayak. She was putting a fact in the record where it belonged, because that was what the record was for.

She had told herself this for three weeks. She believed it. She also knew her mother would not speak to her for a month, and she had decided this was a reasonable price, and she had decided it alone, at this desk, at some point between finishing the artifact catalog and starting the acknowledgments, and she had not called anyone to confirm the decision because there was no one to call.

The scanner finished. The whirring stopped, and the click did not come after it, and the silence was sudden enough that Wren looked up.

She sat in it for a moment.

Then she pulled the submission form from the folder on her left and uncapped her pen — the same archival pen she used for artifact labels, because she did not own a different kind — and she signed her name at the bottom of the form. *W. Calvert.* She wrote the date. She wrote the site number. She checked the box marked *Principal Investigator listed on title page* and wrote Tayak's name in the field beside it, and she checked the box marked *Conflict of interest disclosure included* and wrote *See acknowledgments, p. vii.*

She set the pen down.

The report sat before her in its two volumes, 847 pages, every stratum mapped in three dimensions, every artifact drawn by hand, every name where it belonged. Outside the window the parking lot was empty except for a maintenance truck parked under a sodium light, its orange glow making the pavement look like old clay. Somewhere in the building a door opened and closed. The HVAC ran.

Wren looked at the title page one more time.

*Principal Investigator: Dr. Tayak Moyaone.*

She thought about the trench at 2 a.m. on the night the pump failed, and Tayak's hands on the intake hose in the dark water, and the way Tayak had not looked at her when she said *I know what you did and I know why*, and the text message Wren had sent three weeks ago that Tayak had not answered. She thought about the pendant coming up out of the earth face-up, which meant nothing scientifically and which she had noted in the field log as *orientation: face-up, significance unknown* and which she had thought about every day since.

She closed Volume I.

She would submit the report in the morning. She would carry both volumes to the department office and hand them to whoever was at the desk, and she would keep a copy of the submission form, and then it would be in the record and the record would be permanent and nothing that happened afterward — not her mother's silence, not Pruitt's attorney, not the text message Tayak had not answered — would be able to change what was written on page vii.

She picked up her phone and turned it face-up and looked at her mother's four texts without opening them.

Then she put the phone in her bag, turned off the desk lamp, and sat for a moment in the dark with the report in front of her, the two volumes just visible in the orange light from the parking lot, before she stood and put on her coat.

The acknowledgments section opened with a cursor blinking in white space.

Wren had written acknowledgments before — two thesis chapters, a co-authored site report for a highway survey in Anne Arundel County where she had thanked the landowner and the county GIS office and left it at that. This was different, and she had known for three weeks that it would be different, and she had saved it for last the way you save the thing you are least sure you can do.

She typed the section heading. *Acknowledgments.* She hit return twice.

The lab was quiet. The HVAC had cycled down to its lowest register, the kind of sound you only noticed when everything else stopped. On the drying rack to her left, the last of the ceramic sherds from context 47 sat in their foam-lined tray, each one tagged in B-72 and archival ink, the acetone smell mostly gone now, replaced by the dry-cardboard smell of the archival boxes stacked along the wall. The copper pendant was in the conservation suite with Adaeze. The report was finished except for this page.

She put her hands on the keys and did not type.

The thing she had been telling herself was true: the 1687 patent already existed. Thomas Calvert had already received it. The boundary description had already used the Piscataway burial ground as a survey marker, which meant the colonial government had already known what the site was when they granted it. She had not done any of that. She was thirty-one years old. Thomas Calvert had been dead for three hundred years.

What she had done was know. She had known for two years — since she found the photocopy in her grandmother's estate papers, the metes-and-bounds language in faded colonial script naming *the Piscataway settlement at the high ground adjacent to Mattawoman Creek* — and she had said nothing. She had applied for the field assistant position anyway. She had spent four months on the site, writing context numbers in waterproof ink, fitting pump intake hoses in dark water, drawing the pendant four times. She had disclosed the conflict in the trench in the rain, to Tayak, after the damage was already done.

This was the part she could not make smaller than it was.

She typed.

*The author discloses a personal connection to the land on which Site 18CH-447 is situated. The site falls within the boundary of the 1687 Calvert land patent granted to Thomas Calvert by the Lords Baltimore, a patent in which the author's family has held an interest through successive conveyances to the present. The 1687 patent boundary description references the Piscataway burial ground at the high ground adjacent to Mattawoman Creek as a survey marker, indicating that the colonial government was aware of the site's status as a Piscataway burial ground at the time the patent was issued. This conflict of interest was disclosed in writing to the principal investigator and to the tribal council in September 2024. The author has received no compensation from any party with a title interest in the patent and has no financial stake in the outcome of any legal proceeding arising from this report.*

She stopped.

She read it back.

The language was flat, which was correct. Acknowledgments were not confessions. They were not apologies. They were statements of fact placed in the record so that anyone reading the report in twenty years — or two hundred — would know what the author had known and when. She had modeled the phrasing on the conflict-of-interest disclosures in the SAA ethics guidelines and on the boilerplate in two federal Section 106 consultation reports she had pulled from the SHPO database the previous week, reading them in this same chair at two in the morning with the scanner running beside her. She had wanted the language to be dry enough that no one could call it performance. She had wanted it to be precise enough that no one could call it evasion.

She read it a second time.

*The 1687 patent boundary description references the Piscataway burial ground at the high ground adjacent to Mattawoman Creek as a survey marker, indicating that the colonial government was aware of the site's status as a Piscataway burial ground at the time the patent was issued.*

That sentence was the one her mother would not forgive. Not the disclosure itself — her mother had known the disclosure was coming since Wren told her on the phone three weeks ago, had called her ungrateful, had said *the land has been in this family since your great-grandmother's time* and then hung up. What her mother would not forgive was that sentence in print, in a document that would go to the state historic preservation office and sit in the permanent site files alongside the permit application and the field logs, because that sentence said plainly that the Calvert family had known. Not just that the land was contested. That the colonial government had known it was a burial ground and granted it anyway, and that the Calverts had held the grant for three hundred years without saying so.

Wren had not put that interpretation in the sentence. The sentence said only what the document said. But her mother would read the interpretation into it, and her mother would not be wrong.

She changed nothing.

She hit *Save.*

The file saved with a sound that was not a sound — just the cursor blinking again in the same place, the paragraph sitting there on the screen in twelve-point Times New Roman, the same font as the rest of the report, nothing about it to indicate that it had taken her three weeks to write four sentences. She looked at it for a moment longer. Then she scrolled up to the top of the acknowledgments section and read the full page from the beginning.

She had thanked Tayak first, by name and title, for the opportunity to work on the site and for her patience with the documentation process. She had thanked Priya Suresh for the geomorphological modeling. She had thanked the tribal council for their consultation and for access to the oral history materials that had informed the interpretive sections. She had thanked Adaeze in the conservation suite. She had thanked Delia and Osei by name, listing their contributions to the east and west transects. She had thanked Elspeth for the total station work and the 3D stratigraphic profiles, which had taken six weeks to process in Metashape and which were, Wren thought, the best thing in the report. She had thanked her undergraduate methods professor at Maryland for teaching her to draw artifacts by hand before touching a camera, a habit that had produced the pendant illustration on plate 23, which she also thought was the best thing in the report, depending on the day.

She had not thanked her mother.

She had not thanked Pruitt's attorney, who had called her twice in October.

She had not thanked her grandmother, who had read the patent boundary description in 1987 and set it aside without telling anyone, and who had died without telling anyone, and whose silence had passed to Wren's mother with the estate papers, and from Wren's mother to Wren three weeks before Wren applied for the field position, and from Wren to this paragraph, which was where it stopped.

The cursor blinked.

Wren saved the file again, which was not necessary and which she did anyway, and then she closed the document and sat for a moment looking at the desktop. The scanner sat dark on the table beside her. The archival boxes were stacked in their rows. The foam-lined tray held its sherds. Everything in the room was labeled and numbered and in its place, and the report was 847 pages plus this page, and the page was done, and she had changed nothing.

She thought about page vii sitting inside Volume I, in the dark, between the table of contents and the project background section, and she thought about it sitting there in twenty years in the SHPO site files in a gray archival box, and she thought about someone pulling the box in 2044 and opening the report and reading the acknowledgments the way you read acknowledgments — quickly, looking for names you recognize — and stopping at that paragraph, and reading it again.

She hoped they would read it again.

Her phone showed four unread texts from her mother and one from a number she did not recognize, which was probably Pruitt's attorney's personal cell, and she left all of them unread and put the phone in her bag.

She picked up the archival pen — the same pen she used for artifact labels, because she did not own a different kind — and uncapped it, and on the submission form she wrote *See acknowledgments, p. vii* in the conflict-of-interest field, and she capped the pen, and she set it down.

Outside the window the parking lot was empty. The sodium light over the maintenance truck made the pavement look like old clay, the same reddish-brown as the Bt horizon at the base of the trench, the layer below which the ground had not been disturbed.

She would carry both volumes to the department office in the morning.

She turned off the monitor. The room went dark except for the orange light from outside, and in that light the two volumes of the report sat on the desk in front of her, and she could just make out the title on the spine of Volume I, and her name below it, and below her name, in the same font and the same size, the words *Field Assistant.*

She had written Tayak's name on the title page as principal investigator. She had written it there because it was true, and because the permit was Tayak's, and because Tayak had been on the site for eleven months before Wren arrived, and because none of what the report documented would exist without Tayak's eleven months and Carver's training and Cecile Marant's small brush roll and the forty-three pages of field assessment Tayak had written before Wren signed anything. She had written it there also because she had owed Tayak a debt she could not repay in any other currency, and this was the currency available to her, and she had used it, and she had not told Tayak she was doing it, because Tayak had not answered the text.

The text had said: *I listed you as PI. I'm sorry it took me this long to do the right thing. I don't expect anything back.*

Tayak had not answered.

Wren sat in the dark with the report in front of her and thought about the pendant coming up out of the earth face-up, and about the way the copper had looked in the tray under the field light — not green, not brown, but the color of shallow water over dark stone — and about the four drawings she had made of it, three failures and one final version, and about how even the final version had not gotten the weight right.

You could not draw weight.

She stood, and put on her coat, and left the light off.

The upload window opened at 11:51 p.m.

Wren had the file path memorized by now — she had navigated to the university's field documentation repository four times in the past three weeks to upload preliminary context sheets and photo logs, and the folder structure had printed itself into her hands the way site grids did, so that she moved through it without reading the labels. She dragged the PDF of Volume I into the upload field, then Volume II, then the supplementary appendices folder, which contained the Harris matrix exports, the radiocarbon table, the Metashape orthophotos rendered as high-resolution TIFFs, and appendix D.

The upload bar appeared. It was a thin blue line, and it moved the way those bars always moved — quickly through the first third, then slower, then slower still, as if the file were reconsidering.

She watched it.

The lab smelled of acetone and old cardboard and the particular staleness of a room that had been occupied for months by one person eating at her desk. The HVAC had cycled down to its nighttime register, a low hum that she had stopped hearing weeks ago and could only locate now by listening for it deliberately. Outside the window the parking lot sat empty under its sodium light, the pavement that same reddish-brown it always was at night, and she looked at it for a moment and then looked back at the screen.

She wrote the number on the submission form in the conflict-of-interest field below *See acknowledgments, p. vii*, because there was room and because the form asked for it and because she had learned, on this project more than any other, that you wrote everything down twice.

She opened her email.

The draft had been sitting there since ten o'clock. Three recipients: Tayak's university address, which Wren had used for the past four months and which Tayak answered within the hour when she answered at all; the tribal council's legal address, which Marcus had given Wren in September after the gorget find, in a text that said *for anything that needs to go on record*; and the state commission's public record inbox, which was listed on the commission's website under a heading that said SUBMISSIONS AND CORRESPONDENCE and which Wren had confirmed was active by sending a test message three weeks ago and receiving an automated acknowledgment within forty seconds.

She had written the body of the email in September and revised it six times. It was four sentences. It said that the attached files constituted the complete field documentation for Site 18CH-447, that the report listed Dr. Tayak Moyaone as principal investigator and Wren Calvert as field assistant, that the conflict-of-interest disclosure on the submission form referenced the acknowledgments section of Volume I, and that questions could be directed to the university's archaeology department or to Dr. Moyaone directly.

Her phone was in her bag. She did not take it out.

She closed the laptop.

The room was quiet. The scanner sat dark. The archival boxes stood in their rows along the east wall, their labels facing out: context numbers in her own handwriting, the same hand she'd used for the artifact labels, for the field drawings, for the acknowledgments on page vii. She would take them to the department office at eight, before anyone arrived, and leave them with the administrative assistant and sign the delivery log and walk out. That was the last thing. After that the report would belong to the site files and the SHPO archive and the commission's public record and the tribal council's legal team and to Tayak, who was the principal investigator and whose name was on the title page and who had not answered the text.

Wren stood at the door with both volumes in her arms and looked back at the room. The monitor was dark. The laptop was closed. The archival pen sat capped on the desk beside the empty tray where the submission form had been. Wren read it. She read it again. She read it a third time the way you read something when you are trying to decide if it means what it appears to mean, or something more, or something less. It appeared to mean what it meant.

She put the phone in her coat pocket and went down the stairs.

The car was cold. She put both volumes on the passenger seat and turned the heat on and sat while the engine idled and the vents blew cold air that would eventually become warm air. The phone was in her pocket. She did not take it out.

On the passenger seat, Volume I sat with its spine toward her. She had printed the title on a label and applied it with archival adhesive, pressing out the air bubbles with the flat of her thumbnail: *Site 18CH-447 Excavation Report, Volume I: Text and Analysis.* Below that: *Principal Investigator: Dr. T. Moyaone. Field Assistant: W. Calvert.* Below that: the site number, the permit number, the date.

Her field notebook was in her bag. She had not meant to bring it — it was supposed to go into the curation box in the morning with the other primary records — but she had picked it up without thinking when she left the lab, the way you pick up something you have carried for months without registering the weight.

She pulled it out now. Notebook 1 of 2 for the site. Green cover, water-stained at the lower right corner from the September rain that had come in sideways under the tarp and soaked the east edge of the trench and her notebook and her left boot simultaneously. She had kept writing. The ink had held because she used waterproof ink, which Tayak had told her to use on the first day and which she had used every day since.

She turned to the inside front cover.

She had written things there that were not data — not formally. A measurement she needed to remember. A reference to a context sheet number. The date she had arrived on site. And, six weeks ago, in pencil so she could erase it if she decided it did not belong: *Sioſ—*

She had circled it twice. The circles were slightly uneven, drawn fast, the way you circle something when the thought is moving faster than the pencil.

She looked at it for a while. The heat was coming through the vents now, real heat, and the windshield was beginning to clear at the edges.

*Sioſ—.* The rest of the name gone to water damage in a 1691 Charles County folio, the paper soft and brown at the margin where the damp had worked inward over three hundred years and taken the ink with it. A petition filed in the county court arguing that the high ground near Mattawoman Creek was unavailable for patent because it already held the dead. The petition had not been answered. The court had recorded it — Thomas Ware, clerk, winter session 1691 — and then nothing. No ruling. No dismissal. No record of the petitioner appearing again.

Wren had found the folio in October, in the Charles County records room, on the same afternoon she found the 1741 deed transferring the Friendship tract to James Harrington. She had been working through the colonial patent books looking for the chain of title, and the folio had been misfiled, or filed deliberately, between two instrument books from the wrong decade. The archivist — a woman in her forties with reading glasses pushed into her hair — had not known it was there.

Wren had photographed it and written the partial name in her notebook and circled it twice and then continued working through the deed volumes because the afternoon was short and she had a list and the name was not on the list.

She had thought about it since. Not constantly. The way you think about something that does not resolve.

A woman — Wren had written *her* in the margin, a guess, a reading of the handwriting's formal register, which did not prove anything — who had stood in a county court in 1691 and filed a piece of paper arguing that the ground was already spoken for. Who had known the legal form well enough to use it, or had found someone who did. Who had named the mound in a colonial document and signed it with a name that was now half a name, the rest of it dissolved.

The petition had not been answered. The patent had been issued anyway, to Thomas Calvert, in 1687 — four years before the petition, which meant the petition was a challenge after the fact, filed against something already done. Which meant she had known it was already done and filed it anyway.

Wren sat with that for a moment.

The windshield was clear now. The parking lot was empty except for a facilities truck at the far end, its amber light turning slowly.

She opened her bag and found the archival pen — the one she kept in the outer pocket, not the one she had left capped on the desk in the lab. She opened Volume I to the inside front cover. The paper there was the same acid-free stock as the rest of the report, cream-colored, heavier than standard. She had left it blank because inside covers are not for data.

She wrote the partial name in the lower left corner. Not small, not large. The same size she used for context numbers in the field.

*Sioſ—*

She looked at it. Then she wrote, below it, in the same hand:

*Charles County Court, 1691. Petition against the Calvert patent. No ruling recorded.*

She capped the pen. She looked at what she had written. It was not a citation. It was not a footnote. It was not part of the report's evidentiary record, which was complete and submitted and no longer hers to alter.

It was the only thing she could still give to a woman whose name had been half-destroyed by water and who had filed a petition that no one answered and who had known, when she filed it, that it would probably not be answered, and who had filed it anyway.

Witness, Wren thought. The word her grandmother had used for what a document did when it named a thing. Not proof. Not argument. Witness.

She closed the report.

She took the phone out of her coat pocket. Tayak's message was still on the screen. *Thank you.* Wren looked at it for the last time and then put the phone away without replying, because there was nothing she could say that would be more precise than what Tayak had already said, and because some exchanges are complete the moment they arrive, and adding to them only dilutes what they were.

She put the car in gear.

The two volumes rode on the passenger seat, Volume I on top, the archival label facing up in the sodium light from the parking lot lamps. On the inside front cover, in the lower left corner, in waterproof ink that would hold as long as the paper held: a partial name, a date, a court, a silence of three hundred and thirty-three years.

She drove.

The phone lit up at 12:40 a.m., her mother's name filling the screen with the particular brightness of a call that has been placed more than once in the same hour. Wren watched it. The road south on Route 6 was empty in both directions, and she had pulled into the right lane out of habit, the way you do when you are tired and want the shoulder close. She watched the phone ring four times and then go dark.

She drove.

The volumes were on the passenger seat where she had put them, Volume I face-up, the archival label white in the dark. She had not moved them to the back seat. She was not sure why. Company, maybe. Or the need to feel the weight of the thing she had done still present beside her, not yet set down.

Her mother had called twice before she left the parking lot. Wren had not answered either time. She had driven out of College Park on autopilot, south on Route 1 to the Beltway and then east and south again, the way she had driven it forty or fifty times in the past four months, the route so familiar her hands knew the exits before her eyes found the signs. Charles County in the small hours. The kind of dark that has no ambient glow, just the road ahead and the tree line and the occasional amber flash of a reflector post.

At the Mattawoman Creek bridge she pulled over.

Not because she had planned to. The car simply slowed, the way a body slows when it recognizes a place before the mind catches up, and she turned onto the gravel apron at the bridge's south end and stopped. She cut the engine. The dark came in around the car, and the sound of the creek came with it — water moving over the shallow gravel run below the bridge, the sound low and continuous, the way it had been every time she had crossed here in the past four months, though she had never stopped before.

She got out.

The air was cold and smelled of wet clay and something green underneath it, the spicebush coming into its late-season last effort along the bank. She could see the water from the bridge railing — a faint gleam of moving black below, the gravel bar pale on the near side. The sky was overcast and the dark was the deep, even dark of a clouded-over country night, no moon, no stars, just the road and the creek and the tree line and the sound.

She did not go to the mound.

She was aware of it, the way you are aware of something behind you in a room, a presence located and acknowledged and not turned toward. It was two miles north through the field road, past the site fence and the locked equipment shed, past the marker flags Elspeth had reset in September, past the datum stake Tayak had driven in the first week of the season. Wren knew its position the way she knew the site grid — in her feet, in her hands, in the particular quality of attention the ground there required. But it was not hers to go to. It had never been hers to go to in the way she had sometimes, in the first weeks, half-imagined it might be. She had understood that more clearly as the months went on. The mound was Tayak's work and Nora's knowledge and Kimi's voice and the council's decision and the commission's record, and it was the ground's own long patience, and it was the names spoken into it by people who had stood there for two thousand years. Wren's part had been the documentation. She had done that. The documentation was on the passenger seat of her car, 847 pages, and it was no longer hers either.

She stood at the railing for four minutes. She counted them, not deliberately, the way you count when your mind is quiet and has nothing else to do. The creek ran below her. A car passed on the road behind her, slowing briefly as the driver registered the parked car on the gravel apron, then continuing south. The spicebush smell came up from the bank in a cold wave.

She thought about the acknowledgments paragraph. It was on page six of Volume I, below the standard list of institutional affiliations and tribal consultation credits and laboratory staff. She had written it last, at 11:15 p.m., after the rest of the report was compiled and paginated and the PDF had exported clean. It had taken her twelve minutes to write and she had not revised it. The paragraph named the Calvert land grant of 1687. It named the boundary description and the burial ground it referenced. It named Wren's connection to the Calvert family and stated that she had held this information for two years before disclosing it. It named the disclosure as the right thing and the late disclosure as a failure she did not intend to repeat. It was not an apology to Tayak, because an apology would have made it about Wren, and it was not about Wren. It was a record. The acknowledgments section of a formal report is a record of what the project owed and to whom, and Wren had written it that way.

Her mother would not speak to her for a month. Possibly longer. Wren had known this since October, since the morning she had called her mother to say she was going to disclose the patent connection to the tribal legal team, and her mother had said *the land has been in this family since your great-grandmother's time* and then *don't call me* and then, the following morning, a series of texts that Wren had read and not answered. The texts had continued at intervals. The most recent one had arrived three days ago: *Pruitt says you've put us in an impossible position. Do you understand what you've done?*

Wren understood what she had done. She had documented a burial site to the standard the site required. She had named the conflict of interest she had carried too long. She had listed Tayak as principal investigator because Tayak was the principal investigator — had been from the first permit, had been through the suspension, had been through the hearing and the thirty-day clock and the commission ruling — and listing herself as anything else would have been a lie told in the language of academic convention. She had written a partial name in the lower left corner of the inside front cover because a woman had filed a petition in 1691 that no one had answered and the least Wren could do was witness it, which was not the same as answering it but was what she had.

She thought about Tayak reading the acknowledgments paragraph.

Tayak would read the report the way she read everything — twice, from the beginning, with the kind of attention that did not skip and did not skim. She would read the abstract and the background and the methods and the results by context, and she would read the artifact catalog and the radiocarbon tables and the Harris matrix appendix, and she would read the acknowledgments. She would read the paragraph about the Calvert grant. She would read Wren's name beside it.

Wren did not know what that would cost Tayak. She knew it would cost something. The commission would read it too — the acknowledgments section was not a footnote, it was part of the formal submission, and the commission's legal staff would read the report cover to cover because that was their job, and they would find the paragraph and they would understand what it said about the title chain and about the 1687 boundary description and about what the colonial government had known when it issued the patent. That was why Wren had written it. But Tayak would read it first, and Tayak would understand what it meant that Wren had held the information for two years, and what it meant that she was naming it now, in the acknowledgments of the final report, which was the most permanent record the excavation would produce. Whether Tayak would call her after reading it, or not call her, or call her and say something Wren had not yet imagined — that was a question Wren could not answer from a bridge over Mattawoman Creek at 12:44 in the morning.

She got back in the car.

The cold had settled into her shoulders and the back of her neck, the particular cold of standing still outside in late October when you are tired and have been inside under fluorescent light for six hours. She started the engine and turned the heat on and sat for a moment while the car warmed. The volumes were on the passenger seat. Volume I, the archival label facing up. Inside the front cover, in the lower left corner: *Sioſ—. Charles County Court, 1691. Petition against the Calvert patent. No ruling recorded.*

Her mother would not speak to her for a month.

Wren considered this. She turned it over the way you turn a thing over when you are trying to find the weight of it, to see if it is heavier than you thought or lighter. A month of silence from her mother. The texts that would come anyway, because her mother's silence was never complete — it was a silence with a running commentary underneath it, texts at odd hours, forwarded emails from Pruitt's firm, the occasional call that rang once and stopped. A month of that. And then, eventually, a conversation that would be difficult and would not resolve everything and would have to be had regardless.

She considered it again.

The creek ran below the bridge. The spicebush smell was gone now, cut off by the car's windows and the heater's dry warmth. The road south was empty.

A reasonable price, Wren thought. For what she had done and what she had not done sooner and what the report now said in permanent ink on acid-free paper that would hold as long as the paper held. A month of her mother's silence was a reasonable price for that. She was aware that she might be wrong about this — that the price might turn out to be higher, or differently shaped, or to arrive in ways she had not anticipated. She was aware that reasonable was a word she was using because it was the word available, and that the thing she had done had costs she could not yet fully count.

But she had done it. The report was submitted. Tayak's name was on the title page as principal investigator, and Wren's name was below it as field assistant, and the acknowledgments paragraph was on page six, and the commission would read it, and Tayak would read it first.

Whatever Tayak would say when she did — that was the thing that waited on the other side of the drive home. Not tonight. Probably not tomorrow. But it was there, patient as the creek below the bridge, running in the dark, going somewhere Wren could not yet see.

She pulled back onto Route 6 and drove south.
