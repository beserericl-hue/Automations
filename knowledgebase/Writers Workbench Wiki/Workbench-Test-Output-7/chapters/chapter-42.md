The coffee had been cold for two hours. Kimi knew this because she had picked it up three times without drinking it, each time setting it back on the stack of printed pages that now covered half her desk.

The desk was a door on sawhorses. She'd built it herself when she moved into this apartment four years ago, proud of the practicality. Tonight it felt like a crime scene.

She had seventeen browser tabs open. The Maryland Business Express portal occupied the center screen, its government-gray interface washed pale by her monitor's blue light. Around it: a PDF of the state's highway expansion environmental impact summary, two county planning newsletters she'd subscribed to under a fake name, and a satellite image of Route 228 that she kept zooming in on, as if the answer were visible from orbit.

Three weeks since the Post story. Three weeks of Tayak's silence on anything personal, only the terse professional texts. *Got your message, busy at the site.* Those texts told Kimi exactly how things stood.

She had started the highway research to make amends. That was the honest version. The less honest version was that she couldn't sit still, couldn't post anything to the account without feeling like a fraud, couldn't look at the 80,000 follower count without thinking about what she'd done with that number. So she'd done what she always did when she felt useless: she'd gone looking for something to be useful with.

The highway expansion had seemed like a side thread. The Route 228 widening project, announced quietly in a county planning bulletin she'd caught only because she'd set up alerts for anything mentioning Charles County. The environmental impact summary listed six parcels under review. One of them, Parcel 14-C, sat less than a quarter mile from the mound.

She'd started pulling the development entities named in the county's public comment filings.

Chesapeake Heritage Development LLC had appeared in a letter objecting to a conservation easement on a neighboring parcel. The letter was polite, technical, and very specifically worded to sound like it was about environmental stewardship while actually arguing against any restriction on land use. Kimi had read it three times before she understood what it was doing.

She'd pulled the LLC's business entity filing two days ago. Standard stuff: registered in Maryland, principal office in Annapolis, registered agent listed as Hargrove Capital Partners, 1800 K Street NW, Washington DC.

She'd written the name down and moved on.

Tonight she'd been working the highway expansion angle from the other direction. Who had lobbied for the Route 228 widening, who had submitted public comments in favor, who stood to benefit from the access corridor. The state DOT's public comment archive was not designed to be searched. It was designed, as far as Kimi could tell, to technically exist while being practically useless. She'd been copying names by hand into a spreadsheet for ninety minutes.

And there it was.

*Hargrove Capital Partners, on behalf of Chesapeake Regional Infrastructure Coalition.*

She read it again. Then she pulled up the Chesapeake Heritage Development filing in the other tab and read that name again too.

Same registered agent. Same 1800 K Street address.

She sat back. The chair creaked. From the street below came the sound of a car with a failing muffler, dragging itself up the hill toward Columbia Heights.

It could be a coincidence. Registered agents serviced dozens of companies. That was the whole business model. One firm, many clients.

But.

She opened a new tab and searched Hargrove Capital Partners directly. Their website was the kind of website that said almost nothing in very expensive fonts. *Strategic investment, regional development, infrastructure-aligned real estate.* A team page with four headshots, all men, all in the same shade of navy blazer. No client list. No portfolio.

She searched the name in the Maryland business database. Hargrove Capital Partners appeared as the registered agent for thirty-one entities. She started going through them.

Most were shells, holding companies, LLCs with geographic names that told you nothing. She recognized the pattern from a media law course she'd taken in college, the one semester she'd spent thinking she might want to be a journalist before she'd decided she'd rather make things than report on them.

Then she hit filing number nineteen.

*Chesapeake Corridor Holdings LLC.* Registered 2019. Principal office: same Annapolis address as Chesapeake Heritage Development.

She searched Chesapeake Corridor Holdings in the county planning database.

It came up in a 2021 land acquisition record. A parcel on the eastern side of Route 228. Purchased for $340,000.

She pulled the satellite image back up and found the parcel. It sat between the highway and the conservation buffer. If the road widened, if the access corridor went through, that parcel's value would triple. Maybe more.

Kimi stood up. She walked to the window and looked out at the street without seeing it.

The company threatening the mound's protection, Chesapeake Heritage Development, shared a registered agent with a company that had bought land along the highway expansion route two years before the expansion was publicly announced.

She didn't know what that meant yet. She knew what it looked like.

She went back to the desk and pulled up the original Chesapeake Heritage Development filing again, reading it more carefully this time. The LLC had been formed in 2020. Its stated purpose was, and she read this slowly: *heritage-aligned real estate development and interpretive site management.*

*Interpretive site management.*

She thought about that phrase for a long moment. It was the kind of phrase that sounded like preservation and meant the opposite. The language of a company that wanted to control what a site meant, who told its story, what got built around it and why.

She thought about the mound. She thought about Tayak in the trench, careful as a surgeon, brushing dirt from something two thousand years old.

She sat down and opened a new document. She started writing it out, not for the account, not for anyone yet, just for herself, just to see if it held together on the page. The registered agent. The two companies. The land acquisition. The highway expansion timeline. The conservation easement objection.

It held together.

*Chesapeake Heritage Development LLC*, she wrote inside it. She drew a line down.

There. Under *organizer of record*: not Hargrove Capital Partners this time, but a different name. *Tidewater Corridor Group, LLC. Wilmington, Delaware.*

She wrote that in a second box and drew a line connecting it to the first.

Delaware. Of course. You incorporated in Delaware when you wanted to keep things quiet. The state's disclosure requirements were thin enough to read through. She'd learned that in the same media law course, from a professor who'd spent a semester explaining all the ways information could be technically public and practically invisible.

Active. Registered 2018. Registered agent: Corporation Service Company, which told her nothing. CSC was the registered agent for half the shell companies in the country. But the filing listed a *principal place of business*: 1800 K Street NW, Washington DC.

Same address. Same building where Hargrove Capital Partners kept its office, its four men in navy blazers, its expensive fonts.

Then the road had been aligned exactly as recommended. The mound was not incidental to this. The mound was the destination.

The county planning office had them. The state DOT had them. The Maryland business entity database was public. Any of this could have been found by anyone willing to spend the hours. She thought it was the second one. The whole architecture of it, the Delaware incorporation, the CSC registered agent, the Hargrove layer between Tidewater and its subsidiaries, the geographic names that sounded like they meant something but didn't, it was built to be navigable from the inside and opaque from the outside. You'd have to start from a specific thread and pull it in a specific direction to end up where she'd ended up tonight.

She'd started from Chesapeake Heritage Development because Tayak had mentioned the name. She'd pulled the thread because she owed Tayak something after the Post disaster, and because she was better at this than she'd admitted to anyone, and because she'd been angry for weeks in a way that needed somewhere to go.

She looked at the diagram again.

The company threatening the mound's protection was a subsidiary of the same holding company that held the highway corridor contract and the heritage consulting agreement with the state. The same entity that stood to profit from the road stood to profit from controlling the story of what the road uncovered. If the mound was designated a heritage site under their management, if Chesapeake Heritage Development became the interpretive authority, they would control access, framing, and development rights in the surrounding corridor.

They would own the land. They would own the story of the land. And the road they'd helped route would bring the visitors who'd make both profitable.

Kimi thought about the phrase *interpretive site management* and felt something cold move through her that was not quite fear and not quite rage but lived in the same neighborhood as both.

She picked up her phone and found Tayak's number. It was after one in the morning. She typed a text instead: *Are you awake? I need to show you something.*

The reply came back in under a minute. *Still at the site. What is it.*

Not a question. Tayak typed statements when she was tired and focused. The question marks disappeared.

Kimi looked at her notebook, at the five boxes and their connecting lines, at the photograph she'd taken of it. She thought about how to say it in a text and decided she couldn't.

The LLC, the registered agent, the Delaware parent, the highway comment, the land acquisition, the consulting agreement. She kept it factual. She didn't editorialize. "Tayak."

"I'm here."

"Tidewater Corridor Group. Delaware LLC, registered 2018, principal office at 1800 K Street."

Silence.

The generator hum in the background dropped a register, then steadied.

Her voice was flat in the way it got when she was thinking hard enough that the thinking had crowded out everything else: tone, affect, the normal human signals that told you how a person was receiving what you'd just said. Kimi had heard that voice before. She hadn't heard it sound quite like this.

"Tayak. Are you, "

"Send it," Tayak said. "I'll call you in the morning."

She thought: *Tayak knows something she didn't tell me.*

She had no evidence for it. But the silence on the phone, not surprise, not anger, not the particular sharp intake of breath Tayak made when something landed wrong, had been the silence of a person fitting a piece into a place they'd already made room for.

Kimi closed the notebook. She would ask in the morning.

She turned off the desk lamp and sat in the dark for a moment, and thought about the word *interpretive*, and what it meant to control the interpretation of a thing, and whether the people who'd built this structure had ever once considered that someone might trace it back.

The streetlamp had gone off. The room was the grey-blue of early morning in a city that hadn't quite started yet.

She'd drawn it fast the night before, in the logic-first urgency of someone following a thread before it snapped. Now she read it slowly, the way she'd learned to read legal language. Not for what it said but for what it was arranged to not say.

Chesapeake Heritage Development LLC. Registered in Maryland, 2021. Principal activity: *heritage site consulting and interpretive management services*. The phrase had felt bureaucratic the first time she'd read it. Now it felt like a door with a lock on the inside.

She'd written in the margin: *what is interpretive management*. She'd circled it twice.

She knew what it meant. She'd worked in UX long enough to understand that the person who controlled the interface controlled what the user experienced as real. Interpretive management was the same thing applied to land: you decided what the site meant, what the signs said, who gave the tours, which story got told in the visitor center and which one got filed in a binder in a back room. You managed the interpretation. You owned the narrative.

She drew a line from *Chesapeake Heritage Development* to the box she'd labeled *Tidewater Corridor Group*, the Delaware parent, and then from that box down to the two spokes she'd confirmed the night before: the highway corridor contract, and the heritage consulting agreement with the county.

This was the part she hadn't written out yet. She'd seen it the night before, in the way you sometimes see a shape in a pattern before you can name it. The cold thing that had moved through her when she read *interpretive site management* for the second time. But she hadn't put it in words. She wrote: *Chesapeake Heritage Development is a subsidiary of Tidewater Corridor Group.*

It was, in fact, very simple, which was the thing that made her stomach turn, because simple structures were the ones that worked.

Protection was the product. That was what she hadn't been able to say the night before, the thing that had been sitting just outside the words she'd used with Tayak. The designation existed not to shield the mound from development pressure but to absorb it. To take the site off the contested list by giving it a status that looked like preservation and functioned like acquisition. The heritage partner would manage access, set interpretive priorities, control the visitor experience, and hold a contractual relationship with the county that would make any future challenge to site use a matter of contract law rather than tribal rights.

And the road, the highway expansion that would bring traffic through the corridor, that would make the land around the mound commercially viable in a way it currently wasn't, would bring the visitors who'd make the heritage site profitable enough to justify the management contract. The mound would pay for its own absorption.

She was back at her desk by one-fifteen. She made a fresh cup of tea, this time drinking it while it was still hot, standing at the kitchen counter, before she sat down.

She searched Raymond Gault in the Maryland business entity database.

He appeared as a principal on three entities. Chesapeake Heritage Development LLC. Tidewater Corridor Group LLC. And a third entity she hadn't seen before: Chesapeake Crossroads Heritage Area Foundation, Inc., a nonprofit, registered in Maryland in 2017.

She sat very still.

The Chesapeake Crossroads Heritage Area Foundation. She'd seen that name. She'd seen it in the county planning newsletter she'd been using as background, the one she'd subscribed to under a fake name three weeks ago. She pulled up the newsletter archive and searched.

There it was. A brief item from six months ago: *The Chesapeake Crossroads Heritage Area Foundation has released its five-year interpretive plan, available on the foundation's website. The plan outlines heritage corridor priorities for Charles, Calvert, and St. Mary's counties, with emphasis on Indigenous heritage sites, colonial-era settlements, and waterfront recreational access.*

She opened the foundation's website. It was a cleaner, more professional site than Hargrove Capital Partners' website, with photographs of rivers and old buildings and, on the *Partners* page, a list of organizational partners that included the county planning office, the Maryland Historical Trust, and, listed under *Site Management Partners*: Chesapeake Heritage Development LLC.

She clicked on the five-year interpretive plan. It was a forty-three-page PDF. She opened it and searched for *Piscataway*.

The word appeared eleven times. Eight of those appearances were in a section titled *Indigenous Heritage Corridor*, which described a proposed interpretive trail linking documented Indigenous sites along the Potomac watershed. The trail was described as an opportunity for *authentic cultural engagement* and *heritage tourism development*. One of the anchor sites listed for the trail was described as *a significant pre-contact burial mound in Charles County, currently under archaeological investigation.*

She read that sentence twice.

Then she searched the document for *memorandum of understanding*.

It appeared once. In a footnote on page thirty-one, attached to a reference to the Indigenous Heritage Corridor: *Development of the corridor is contingent on execution of memoranda of understanding with relevant tribal governments and site management partners. Preliminary agreements are in progress.*

*Preliminary agreements are in progress.*

She looked at the date on the document. Published eight months ago.

She looked at the planning commission summary she'd photographed that morning. *Commission voted to authorize staff to proceed with stakeholder consultation. No objection.* Also eight months ago.

She picked up her pen and added a sixth box to the diagram. *Chesapeake Crossroads Heritage Area Foundation.* She drew a line from it to Tidewater Corridor Group and another line to Chesapeake Heritage Development and wrote Raymond Gault's name along both lines.

Then she sat back and looked at what she had.

The foundation was the public face. The heritage area plan was the document that would go to county commissioners, to the Maryland Historical Trust, to anyone who asked what the development group's intentions were for the corridor. It was the thing that made the whole structure look like stewardship. Forty-three pages of interpretive trail descriptions and heritage tourism projections and language about *authentic cultural engagement*, with a footnote about preliminary agreements and a partner list that included the county planning office.

And somewhere in the space between the foundation's five-year plan and the county planning commission's authorization of stakeholder consultation, a proposal had reached the tribal council.

She still didn't have the MOU. She had the proposal letter, the planning commission vote, the foundation document, and Raymond Gault's name on three entity filings. She had a footnote about preliminary agreements. She had a date range that told her the consultation had happened and that the commission had proceeded without objection.

She needed the MOU.

She opened the county clerk's online portal and started filling out the FOIA request form. The form asked for the requester's name, contact information, the agency whose records were being requested, a description of the records sought, and the preferred format for delivery. She filled in all of it. Under *description of records*, she wrote: *Any executed memoranda of understanding between Charles County, Chesapeake Heritage Development LLC, or the Chesapeake Crossroads Heritage Area Foundation and any tribal government or tribal council, related to heritage site management along the Route 228 corridor or the Indigenous Heritage Corridor described in the Chesapeake Crossroads Heritage Area Foundation five-year interpretive plan, dated approximately eight months ago.*

She submitted the form. The confirmation page said standard turnaround was fifteen business days, which meant she would have it in three weeks if the office processed it promptly, and in six weeks if it didn't. She wrote the submission date in her notebook.

Then she sat back and thought about what else she could do in the meantime.

She could search Raymond Gault's name in the DOT public comment archive, the same one she'd spent ninety minutes searching by hand the night before. She could search the Maryland Historical Trust's Section 106 consultation records, which were partially public. She could look at the foundation's 990 filings, which nonprofits were required to make available and which would show board members, revenue sources, and major expenditures. She could look at whether Tidewater Corridor Group or any of its subsidiaries had received state or county grants tied to heritage area programming.

She started with the 990.

The foundation's most recent 990 was filed for the prior tax year. She found it through the IRS's public database. The board of directors listed seven names. Raymond Gault was listed as treasurer. The other six names she didn't recognize, but she wrote them all down.

Total revenue for the year: $847,000. Major contributors: a private foundation she didn't recognize, two individual donors listed only by initials per IRS convention, and a line item described as *government grants and contracts* totaling $312,000.

She searched the Maryland Department of Planning's grant database for the foundation's name.

It appeared twice. A heritage corridor planning grant of $75,000 from two years ago. And a site interpretation and development grant of $237,000 from eighteen months ago, awarded under the state's heritage area program.

She looked at the grant description for the $237,000 award: *Support for development of interpretive infrastructure along the Chesapeake Crossroads Heritage Corridor, including signage, trail development, and site management planning.*

She wrote the grant numbers in her notebook.

The state had funded the foundation. The foundation was controlled by the same man who ran the LLC that was positioned to manage the mound. The state grant had paid for the planning work that had produced the forty-three-page document with the footnote about preliminary agreements.

She thought about Tayak navigating this terrain. An assistant professor at the University of Maryland, Piscataway, working on a site that a state-funded foundation with a corporate parent had already written into its five-year interpretive plan. The Section 106 consultation process that was supposed to give tribes a seat at the table, the process that listed the Piscataway Indian Nation and the Piscataway Conoy Tribe as consulting parties, running alongside a parallel track in which a private foundation with state funding was already planning the visitor center.

She thought about what it cost to be brought in late, after the frame had already been built.

She had been at her desk for four hours. The tea tin was empty. She made coffee instead, the real kind, and drank it while she went through the last items on her list: the DOT public comment search for Raymond Gault's name, which came up twice, both times as a commenter on highway alignment alternatives; and a search of the Maryland Historical Trust's Section 106 records, which were online but incomplete, the database ending fourteen months ago with the initiation letter she'd already photographed at the county clerk's office.

She had what she had.

She opened a new document and started organizing it, not the narrative version she'd written at twelve forty the night before, but a structured summary, the kind she would hand to someone who needed to act on it. Entity names, filing dates, document references, grant numbers, the Raymond Gault connection across three entities, the timeline from the Route 228 environmental impact assessment through the Section 106 initiation through the foundation's five-year plan through the county planning commission vote. She organized it chronologically. She added the documents she'd photographed at the county clerk's office as attachments.

She named the document *CHD summary for TAM* and saved it to the cloud folder.

Then she looked at the clock. It was four-forty-seven.

Tayak had said she'd call tonight.

Kimi closed the summary document and opened her social media management dashboard, the one she used for the tribal heritage account. She had not posted in four days, which was the longest gap since she'd started the account three years ago. The draft queue had six posts in it, things she'd scheduled before the Post disaster and then paused. She looked at them. They were fine posts. Good photographs, accurate captions, the kind of content the 80,000 followers had come to expect.

She did not publish them.

She sat with the dashboard open and thought about what it meant to have 80,000 people following a story she was telling, and whether the story she was telling them was the whole story, and whether the whole story was hers to tell. She thought about the foundation's forty-three-page plan with its language about *authentic cultural engagement* and its photographs of rivers and old buildings and its footnote about preliminary agreements. She thought about the difference between a platform and a frame.

She closed the dashboard.

She went to the window. The street outside was doing the thing it did at five in the afternoon when the light was low and orange and the people on the sidewalk were moving with the particular purposefulness of people who had somewhere to be before dark. She watched them for a while. She thought about the mound. She thought about the pendant Wren had photographed on white cloth, the copper one, green at the edges, that Tayak had said was older than anything they'd expected to find at that depth.

She thought about what it meant for an object to survive two thousand years in the ground. The specific conditions required. The particular luck of it. The fact that the ground had held it and the ground was still there and Tayak was in it.

She thought about a visitor center with panels on the wall and a binder in a filing cabinet and a man named Raymond Gault whose name appeared on three entity filings and two DOT public comments and a state grant application.

Her phone lit up at seven-twelve. Tayak's name on the screen.

She picked up before the second ring finished.

"I'm here," she said.

"I know." Tayak's voice had the quality of someone who had been talking all day and was now talking to the one person they didn't have to manage. In the background: the close acoustics of the trench tent, probably, the low hum of the generator. "Tell me what you found today."

Kimi told her. She went through it in the same order she'd organized the summary document: the county clerk visit, the Raymond Gault connection, the Chesapeake Crossroads Heritage Area Foundation, the 990, the state grants, the five-year interpretive plan with its footnote about preliminary agreements. She kept it factual. She gave Tayak the document references so she could pull them herself.

When she finished, Tayak was quiet for a moment.

"The foundation's five-year plan," she said. "The footnote about preliminary agreements."

"Published eight months ago."

"And the planning commission authorized stakeholder consultation eight months ago."

"Same month."

Another pause. "The MOU request," Tayak said. "You submitted the FOIA today?"

"This afternoon. Fifteen business days standard turnaround, so three weeks minimum."

"Okay." A breath. "Okay. That's the right move." She paused again, and Kimi heard something shift in the background, a chair or a cot, the sound of someone settling. "Kimi. The Raymond Gault connection. Have you searched his name in the Section 106 consultation record?"

"The MHT database cuts off fourteen months ago. He's not in the portion that's online."

"I'll ask Wren to pull the full consultation file from MHT directly. She has a contact there." A pause. "Don't search his name publicly yet. Not on the account."

"I wasn't going to."

"I know you weren't." The flat voice again, but not the thinking-hard version. Something else. Something that sounded, underneath the flatness, like she was being careful with a person she cared about. "You've built something real here. I need you to hold it a little longer."

Kimi looked at the diagram on the desk. Five boxes from the night before, the sixth she'd added that afternoon. Raymond Gault's name along two lines. The wheel, more complete now than it had been at midnight.

"How long?" she said.

"Until I've talked to the people I need to talk to." Tayak paused. "There are things in the institutional record that I'm still working through. I don't want you building on top of a foundation I haven't confirmed yet."

Kimi thought about the silence on the phone the night before. The piece fitting into a place already made room for. She thought about the careful way Tayak had said *there may be* that morning when Kimi had asked about the county record.

"Is there something in the institutional record that connects to the council?" she said.

The pause that followed was the weighted one, the one with something in it.

"There may be," Tayak said. "I don't have the full picture yet. When I do, I'll tell you."

It was the same answer as that morning, and it was not an evasion. It was a person who had learned, the hard way, not to name things before she was sure of them. Kimi recognized the discipline. She was trying to practice it herself.

"Okay," she said. "I'll hold it."

"Good." A breath. "Send me the summary document."

"It's already in the cloud folder."

"I'll read it tonight." Another pause, shorter. "You did good work today."

"I found Raymond Gault," Kimi said.

"Yes." And something in the way Tayak said it told Kimi that Raymond Gault was a name Tayak had already encountered somewhere, in the institutional record she was working through, and that finding him in the corporate map was the kind of confirmation that made a structure real in a way that suspicion alone couldn't.

"Tonight," Tayak said. "Get some sleep."

"You too."

The line ended.

Kimi set the phone down on the desk next to the diagram. Six boxes. Six lines. Raymond Gault's name in two places. The wheel, and at its center, a Delaware holding company, and at the end of one spoke, a burial mound in Charles County, and somewhere in the institutional record Tayak was working through, something that connected the wheel to the people who were supposed to be protecting the mound from it.

She didn't know what that something was. She had a shape and she didn't have a name for it yet, and Tayak had asked her to hold it, and she was going to hold it.

She closed the notebook.

She went to the kitchen and made dinner, the first real meal she'd eaten since the half-piece of toast at nine in the morning. She stood at the stove and thought about Nora's handwriting on the tea tin label. She thought about the account and the 80,000 followers and the six draft posts sitting unpublished in the queue. She thought about the difference between a platform and a frame, and whether she had been building one when she thought she was building the other.

She ate standing at the counter, the way she'd eaten the toast that morning, and then she washed the dish and put it away and went back to the desk.

She opened the cloud folder one more time and checked that everything was there. The seventeen files from the night before, the eight photographs from the county clerk's office, the summary document, the 990, the state grant records, the foundation's five-year interpretive plan. The diagram in three photographs because the first two had shadows across the center box.

She scrolled through the folder the way you check a door you've already locked.

Then she set the phone face-down on the desk and sat in the chair she'd been sitting in since nine in the morning, and she thought about what she'd handed over.

Not the folder. The folder was just files. What she'd handed over was the shape of it. The architecture. The thing she'd seen at midnight two nights ago when the Delaware filing finally opened and she'd read the name of the managing member and understood that the wheel had a center.

Tayak would see it the same way. Tayak saw structures. It was what she did, read a stratum and understand what it meant about the ones below it, the ones above it, the pressure that had formed it. She would open the folder and she would read the Delaware filing and she would look at the diagram and she would see the wheel.

And then she would see the line Kimi had drawn last, the thin one, the one she'd added that afternoon, the Raymond Gault line connecting the foundation to the LLC to the holding company, the line that made the whole structure visible as a single thing rather than a collection of separate entities.

Kimi thought about what it cost to sit in a trench tent with a phone and a cloud folder and a set of institutional records and understand, piece by piece, the shape of what had been built around the thing you were trying to protect.

She turned off the desk lamp. The room went orange-gray, the particular color of a city night that has absorbed decades of streetlight and never quite given it back. She sat at the dark desk for a moment and looked at the window.

Somewhere south of here, on the other side of the Bay Bridge and down through the tidal counties where the roads ran on slight rises above wet ground, Tayak was in a tent with field notes and a phone that now had a cloud folder in it and a summary document organized by date, the way Tayak organized her own field records, because Kimi had looked at the shared drive structure from the excavation and had tried to match it, had tried to build something Tayak could navigate without friction.

She had built what she could build. She was holding what Tayak had asked her to hold. In the morning she would go back to the Maryland business entity database and search the names she hadn't searched yet, the six board members from the foundation's 990 whose names she didn't recognize, the individual donors listed by initials, the private foundation that had contributed the largest single gift.

She would keep pulling the thread.

Tonight she was going to sleep.

She went to bed. She fell asleep faster than she expected, and she did not dream about the diagram, and when she woke at six-fifteen the notebook was still open on the desk, the six boxes exactly where she'd left them, Raymond Gault's name along two lines in her smallest handwriting, the wheel waiting for morning.
