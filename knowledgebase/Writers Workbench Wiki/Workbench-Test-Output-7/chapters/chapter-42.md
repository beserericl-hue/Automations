The browser tab had been open for six days.

Kimi had named the folder on her desktop *CHD — DO NOT CLOSE* and then, because she lived alone and there was no one to judge her, added a second folder inside it called *please be right*. Forty-three PDFs. Eleven browser tabs. A legal pad with three columns of names, addresses, and registered agent firms, the handwriting getting smaller and more cramped as she moved down the page.

The apartment smelled of cold coffee and the Thai takeout container she hadn't thrown away yet. Outside the window, Silver Spring was doing what Silver Spring did at eleven on a Tuesday — the 16 bus grinding past on Colesville, a group of students loud on the corner for thirty seconds and then gone, the bodega across the street throwing a yellow stripe of light onto the wet pavement.

She had not posted anything in four days. For Kimi, who ran *Piscataway Rising* like a second heartbeat, four days of silence was a kind of penance.

The *Post* piece had run on a Thursday. By Friday, Dana Osei's paragraph five had been screenshotted and shared by every institutional account that wanted to make the tribe look like it was exploiting its own history. *Founding families with a financial stake in the excavation's outcome.* Kimi had read it seventeen times, trying to find where her words had gone in and what had come out. She knew where. She'd been excited and careless and she'd said *founding families* like it was a good phrase, like it proved something, and Osei had heard it differently or wanted to hear it differently or had already been pointed toward hearing it that way by whoever had given him Marcus's email address.

She hadn't been able to look at Tayak's face on the video call the next morning. Tayak hadn't yelled. That was worse.

So: forty-three PDFs. Eleven tabs. The legal pad.

The Maryland SDAT business entity search was open in tab three. She had typed *Chesapeake Heritage Development LLC* the first time three weeks ago, when she'd been looking for something — anything — to do that wasn't posting. The filing had come back clean and boring: registered in 2019, principal office on Riva Road in Annapolis, registered agent listed as Holloway & Breck LLP, also Annapolis.

She'd written down *Holloway & Breck* and then gone to make tea and then come back and stared at it for a while and then gone to bed.

The Route 301 environmental impact statement was in tab seven. She had downloaded it three weeks ago when someone in the tribal council thread had linked it, and she'd skimmed the cultural resources section and bookmarked it and not gone back until tonight, because tonight she'd had a specific reason.

The EIS was 340 pages. The cultural resources section began on page 187. She had read it carefully this time, not skimming, and on page 203, in a table listing consulting parties for the Section 106 process, she had found a law firm listed as legal representative for the project sponsor.

*Holloway & Breck LLP, Annapolis, MD.*

She sat back in her chair.

The takeout container was on the corner of the desk. She looked at it for a moment, then looked back at the screen. Then she pulled up the SDAT filing again and put the two windows side by side.

Same firm. Same Annapolis address — she checked the street number — same street number.

Her heart was doing something she didn't want to name yet. She had been wrong before. She had been confidently, publicly, humiliatingly wrong before, and the cost of that was still sitting in Tayak's silence on a video call. So she made herself slow down. She opened a new tab and searched for Holloway & Breck directly.

The firm's website was the kind that cost money — dark gray background, serif font, a photograph of the Bay at dusk that managed to look both tasteful and aggressive. Practice areas: real estate, land use, transportation infrastructure, public-private partnerships. Attorneys listed in alphabetical order. She scrolled until she found the partner section.

Clifton Breck. Twenty-two years in practice. Former counsel to the Maryland State Highway Administration.

She wrote that down.

Then she went back to the EIS. The project sponsor for the Route 301 connector expansion was listed on the cover page as a joint venture: the State Highway Administration and *Chesapeake Gateway Partners LLC*. She had read that three weeks ago and thought nothing of it. Now she typed *Chesapeake Gateway Partners LLC* into the SDAT search.

Registered 2017. Principal office: Riva Road, Annapolis.

Same address as Chesapeake Heritage Development LLC.

She stood up. She didn't mean to — her body just decided. She walked to the kitchen, filled a glass of water, drank half of it standing at the sink, and walked back.

Same address. She checked it three times. Same Riva Road address, same suite number.

She opened the Chesapeake Gateway Partners filing and scrolled to the members section. The managing member was listed as *Tidewater Capital Group Inc.* She typed that into a new tab.

Tidewater Capital Group was a Delaware corporation — of course it was — with a Maryland registered agent. The registered agent was Holloway & Breck.

She sat very still for a moment, the way she sat when she was trying not to break something by moving too fast.

Chesapeake Heritage Development LLC — the company that had inserted itself into the heritage-area planning documents around the mound site, the company whose name appeared in the five-year action plan under *priority heritage investment zones*, the company that had, as far as she could tell, positioned itself to manage any visitor infrastructure if the mound was formally protected — was registered at the same address, with the same law firm, as Chesapeake Gateway Partners LLC, the private partner in the Route 301 expansion.

Both of them traced back to Tidewater Capital Group.

She picked up her phone. It was 11:14 p.m. She put it back down.

She needed to be sure. *Sure* sure, not *pretty sure* sure. She opened the EIS again and went to the appendix, which she had never read, because appendices were where documents went to become unreadable. Appendix D was the Section 106 consultation record. She scrolled through it — letters, agency responses, boilerplate — and then stopped.

On page D-47, in a letter from Holloway & Breck to the Maryland Historical Trust dated eight months ago, there was a paragraph she had to read twice.

*Chesapeake Gateway Partners LLC, in its capacity as private development partner, has engaged its affiliate Chesapeake Heritage Development LLC to conduct heritage-area planning and interpretive development in the project corridor, consistent with the goals of the Stories of the Chesapeake Heritage Area Action Plan and the Section 106 Programmatic Agreement.*

*Affiliate.*

They had written it themselves. They had put it in a letter to the Historical Trust and no one had — she stopped. Someone had received that letter. Someone at the Trust had read the word *affiliate* and filed it in an appendix.

Slade's office.

She picked up her phone again. 11:21 p.m. She thought about Tayak at the site, which was two hours south, and about what Tayak was doing right now, which was probably not sleeping. She thought about the four days of silence on *Piscataway Rising* and about paragraph five and about the fact that she had eighty thousand followers who were waiting for her to tell them something true.

She put the phone down and opened her legal pad to a clean page.

She wrote: *Chesapeake Heritage Development LLC → Chesapeake Gateway Partners LLC → Tidewater Capital Group Inc. (Delaware). All: Holloway & Breck, registered agent. Same Riva Road address. EIS Appendix D, p. 47: CHD described as "affiliate" of CGP. CGP is private partner in Route 301 connector expansion.*

She underlined *affiliate* twice.

Then she wrote: *The company threatening the mound is the same company positioned to profit from its protection.*

She looked at that sentence for a long time. Then she picked up her phone and called Tayak.

It rang four times. She was composing a voicemail in her head — *I think I found something, call me when you can, I'm sorry to call late* — when Tayak picked up.

"Kimi." Her voice was flat with tiredness, not anger. Maybe not anger.

"I found something." Kimi kept her voice level. "I need you to tell me if it matters."

The line was quiet for a moment. Kimi heard wind — Tayak was outside.

"Go ahead," Tayak said.

"Chesapeake Heritage Development LLC. The company in the five-year action plan, the one listed under priority heritage investment zones near the site."

"I know the name."

"It's a subsidiary." Kimi looked at the legal pad. "Same Riva Road address, same registered agent — a firm called Holloway & Breck — as Chesapeake Gateway Partners LLC. Gateway is the private partner in the Route 301 connector expansion. Both of them trace back to a Delaware holding company called Tidewater Capital Group."

Silence.

"Tayak."

"I'm here."

"There's a letter in the EIS appendix. Section 106 consultation record, page forty-seven. Holloway & Breck wrote it to the Historical Trust eight months ago. They used the word *affiliate*. They described Heritage Development as an affiliate of Gateway Partners and said Heritage Development would handle, quote, heritage-area planning and interpretive development in the project corridor." Kimi set the phone on the desk and put it on speaker so she could look at the legal pad while she talked. "The company that's been inserting itself into the preservation conversation around the mound is the same company that profits from the highway going through. If the mound gets formally protected, the land around it becomes heritage-adjacent. They build a visitor center, a parking lot, a trail. They market the residential lots in the corridor as open-space adjacency. The protection *is* the profit."

She stopped. She had not meant to say all of it that fast.

The wind on Tayak's end shifted. Something creaked — a gate, maybe, or a truck door.

"How solid is this?" Tayak said.

"SDAT business filings. The EIS is a public federal document. The letter is on Holloway & Breck letterhead with a date stamp from the Trust's incoming mail log — that's in the same appendix, two pages earlier." Kimi turned the legal pad toward herself and read from it. "Registered 2017, both of them. Same suite number on Riva Road. I checked it three times."

"The letter went to the Trust."

"Yes."

"Eight months ago."

"Yes."

Another silence. This one was different — not processing, but something closer to recognition. Kimi had heard Tayak go quiet like this once before, at a council meeting two years ago when something had landed wrong. It was the quiet of a person who has just seen the shape of a thing they had already half-known was there.

"Tayak. If someone at the Trust received that letter and didn't flag it — "

"I know."

" — and if that letter has been sitting in a public appendix for eight months while we've been arguing about the site — "

"Kimi."

"I'm not accusing anyone. I'm saying the chain exists and I can document it and someone needs to know what it means before the University Hearing."

The wind again. A boat horn somewhere distant — she could hear it through the phone, low and long, carrying across whatever water was near the site at this hour.

"How long ago did you find this?" Tayak said.

"Tonight. An hour ago. I've been checking it since."

"Did you tell anyone else?"

"No. I called you."

Kimi waited. She was aware of her own heartbeat, which was doing something unnecessary. She pressed her palm flat on the legal pad the way she did when she needed her hands to stop moving.

"Screenshot everything," Tayak said. "Every filing, every page of the appendix, the letter, the incoming mail log. Timestamp the screenshots. Save them somewhere that isn't just your phone."

"Already started."

"The SDAT filings — can you get PDFs?"

"Yes. I've got four open right now."

"Get them all. The Delaware incorporation record for Tidewater too, if it's accessible."

"Delaware's public search is limited but I can get the registered agent and the filing date. That's enough to show the chain."

"Then get it." A pause. "Kimi. You said the letter was in the EIS appendix."

"Appendix D. Section 106 consultation."

"That appendix — who would have reviewed it on the Trust side?"

She had thought about this. "Cultural Resources Program Manager signs off on Section 106 compliance. That's Slade."

The silence this time was very long. Long enough that Kimi looked at her phone screen to check the call was still connected.

Then: "Send me everything you have."

Tayak's voice was still flat. Still tired. But under it now was something Kimi recognized from their childhood — from the specific way Tayak's voice went when she had stopped being surprised and started being certain.

The call ended.

Kimi sat for a moment with her hand still on the phone. Outside the window, Silver Spring was doing what it always did at this hour — a bus hissing to a stop on Colesville, a car alarm three blocks over starting and stopping, the particular urban quiet that was not quiet at all but just noise at a lower register. She had lived in this apartment for two years and she still could not sleep through it.

She picked up the legal pad and read the chain again from the top. *Chesapeake Heritage Development LLC → Chesapeake Gateway Partners LLC → Tidewater Capital Group Inc.* Then the sentence at the bottom. She had underlined *affiliate* twice and the ink had gone through to the next page.

She opened Adobe Acrobat and pulled up the EIS. Page D-47. She took a screenshot, then used the timestamp function she had set up three weeks ago when she first started building the file — a small habit from the Post disaster, making sure everything she touched had a date and time attached to it. 11:38 p.m. She saved it to the shared drive under a folder she labeled *Tidewater — documentation* and then immediately renamed it *CHD corporate chain — verified*.

She worked through the SDAT filings in order: Chesapeake Heritage Development, then Chesapeake Gateway Partners, then the Tidewater Capital Group Maryland registration, then the Holloway & Breck firm page with Clifton Breck's biography still open in a tab. She captured each one. She went back to the EIS cover page and screenshotted the project sponsor line — *Chesapeake Gateway Partners LLC* — and the date. Then she went back to Appendix D and captured the incoming mail log page, which showed the Holloway & Breck letter received and date-stamped by the Trust's Cultural Resources office.

It took forty minutes. When she was done she had twenty-two files in the folder, each one named by document type and date, each one timestamped.

She opened a new email to Tayak and attached all twenty-two files. Then she stopped and looked at the attachment list. Twenty-two files was not a story. It was evidence. Evidence needed a shape.

She opened a new document and wrote a single page: what she had found, in what order, with the document name and page number for each claim. No interpretation beyond the facts. She read it back twice, took out two sentences that said more than the documents proved, and put the document in the folder. Then she attached that too, at the top of the list.

She sent the email at 12:19 a.m.

She sat back. The bus on Colesville hissed again — a different bus, or the same one making its loop. On her screen the folder sat open, twenty-three files now, and the email showed *Sent* in the corner.

She looked at the sentence on the legal pad. *The company threatening the mound is the same company positioned to profit from its protection.* She had written it to test whether it was real. It was still real.

She thought about what Tayak would do with it. Tayak would read everything tonight — she was certain of that — and in the morning she would call someone, or she would go back to the site, or she would sit very still for a long time and then do something that looked like a small move and turned out to be the right one. That was how Tayak worked. Kimi had spent twenty-six years watching her cousin move through the world like someone who had already mapped the exits.

She turned off the desk lamp. The folder sat open on her screen in the dark. Twenty-three files. She had done what she could do.

She should have felt better.

She picked up her phone and checked the time: 12:24 a.m. The email had been sent five minutes ago. She set the phone face-down on the desk.

Outside, the 16 bus made its loop again. A delivery truck, lower and slower, braked at the corner, idled for a moment, and moved on. The bodega's yellow light was still there on the wet pavement, steady and indifferent.

She had told Tayak she did not know if this helped or made it worse.

She was starting to think it was neither. She was starting to think it was a door, and she had opened it, and she did not yet know what was on the other side. But the door was open now and there was no way to close it — not for her, not for Tayak, not for anyone who had read the email she had sent at 12:19 a.m.

She saved the legal pad notes as a photo with her phone. Sent it to herself. Then she closed every browser tab except the SDAT page for Chesapeake Heritage Development LLC, which she left open because she had a feeling she was going to need it again, and soon.

She closed the laptop.

In the dark, the screen glow from the SDAT page had lit the ceiling in a pale rectangle. Now it was gone. The apartment was the color of the street outside — amber and gray, the bodega's light diffused through the window glass into something softer than it deserved to be.

Twenty-three files. She had sent everything she had.

Tayak would know what to do with it. That was the thing Kimi had learned to trust, even when it cost her something to trust it — even when trusting it meant sitting in a dark apartment at half past midnight with a legal pad full of corporate registration numbers and the particular exhaustion of having done the right thing and not yet knowing if it would matter.

She pulled her knees up onto the chair the way she had since she was a child, her back against the armrest, her face toward the window. The bus on Colesville. The amber light. The city doing what cities did.

She did not think she was going to sleep. But she stayed still, and after a while the not-sleeping became something close enough to rest that it didn't matter what she called it.
