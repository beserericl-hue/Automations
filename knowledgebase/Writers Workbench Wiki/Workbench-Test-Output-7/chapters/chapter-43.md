She had been listening for breath all night.

Not with her ears — ears could be fooled by wind in the smoke hole, by the creak of the house settling, by her own pulse loud in the dark. She listened with her palm, flat against Suki's chest, feeling for the small rise and fall that had been there at dusk, there at moonrise, there when Waya woke the first time and pressed her hand down and felt it and let herself sleep again.

The second time she woke, the chest was still.

She did not move her hand. The packed earth beneath the sleeping mat was cold through the woven reeds, and the fire had burned to orange coals that gave almost no light, and Suki lay on her back with her face turned slightly toward Waya, the way she always slept, as though even in sleep she was checking that her mother was still there.

The fever had come two days ago, sudden and total, the way fever always came — one morning the child was pulling at the dried corn bundles hanging from the roof beam and laughing at the sound they made, and by midday she was hot to the touch and would not eat, and by evening her breathing had gone fast and shallow, each breath a small labor. Waya had made the willow-bark tea and gotten most of it into her. She had laid wet moss on the child's forehead and changed it when it dried. She had asked Ahanu to heat the stones and held Suki in the steam until the child stopped fussing and went limp with exhaustion, and then she had wrapped her and brought her back to the mat and put her own body alongside her for warmth and listened.

She had done everything she knew.

She kept her hand on the still chest and waited for the light.

It came slowly, gray first through the smoke hole, then pale along the edge of the door covering, then enough to see by. Suki's face was smooth. The fever had left it — that was the first thing Waya noticed, and for one moment the thought moved through her like water moving under ice: *the fever broke*. Then her hand on the chest told her the rest.

She looked at her daughter's face for a long time.

Suki had her father's brow and Waya's own mouth, and her hair was still damp at the temples from the last fever sweat, lying in dark strands against her skin. She was very small. Waya had forgotten, in the ordinary days of carrying her and feeding her and pulling her back from the fire and chasing her through the garden rows, how small she was. She looked at her now and understood that she had been forgetting this every day, the way you forget the weight of water until the vessel is empty.

Outside, a bird began. One call, then another, then the whole morning chorus building in the trees beyond the garden. Waya heard it as though from a great distance.

She did not call for Ahanu. He was sleeping on the far side of the house, and their two older children beside him, and she could hear the slow breath of all three of them, and she did not want to end this yet. She was not ready for what would happen when she called out — the movement, the voices, the hands of neighbors, the things that had to be done to a body, the words people would say that were true but would not help. She was not ready for any of it.

So she stayed.

She moved her hand from Suki's chest to her face, touching the small forehead, the cheek, the jaw. The skin was cooling. It still held the shape of her daughter perfectly — every feature she had watched change month by month from the first wet, furious morning of her life — and Waya pressed her memory against each one now as though she could keep them by pressing hard enough.

She had known three children. She knew what it was to want a thing so completely that the wanting became a kind of work, steady and daily, and she knew what it was to have that work taken away without warning or reason or sufficient cause. The first two were alive and breathing thirty feet from her. She had been lucky, she had always known she had been lucky, and she had tried not to think too directly about that luck because thinking about it too directly seemed like an invitation.

She thought about it now.

The coals in the fire pit shifted and settled, sending up a brief glow that lit the underside of the roof beams and the hanging bundles of dried herbs — goldenrod, boneset, the last of the summer's mint — and then faded. The smell of woodsmoke was in everything: the mat, the sleeping skins, her own hair. She would smell it for the rest of her life and it would bring her back to this morning, she knew that already, the way certain smells were doors you could not close.

Suki's hand lay open beside her face, fingers slightly curled, the way a child's hand lies in sleep.

Waya covered it with her own.

She stayed until the light was full and the sounds of the waking village began to come through the walls — the low voices of women at the fire outside, the sound of a dog, a child laughing somewhere near the creek. Then she lifted the door covering and looked out into the morning, which was ordinary and bright and entirely indifferent, and she called Ahanu's name in a voice she did not recognize as her own.

She had wrapped Suki herself, in the sleeping mat — the good one, the one she had woven the winter before the child was born, tight-twilled cattail with a border of dark-dyed rush that she had been pleased with at the time and had never said so to anyone. She folded the mat around the small body with the care she always used for careful work, tucking the edges under, smoothing the surface flat. Her hands did not shake. She noted this about her hands the way she might note the weather — as a fact, without judgment.

Ahanu came to her when she called his name. He crossed the house in three steps and crouched beside the mat and looked at his daughter's face, and then he looked at Waya. His eyes were asking something she did not have words for and did not intend to find.

"She is gone," Waya said.

That was all she said.

Ahanu reached for her — both hands, palms up, the gesture he used when he wanted her to come close. She had come to that gesture a hundred times. She looked at it now and took one step back. Not far. Just enough.

He held the gesture a moment longer, then let his hands fall. He understood, or he thought he understood, and she did not correct him either way. He turned to the mat and put his hand on the outside of it, over where the child's shoulder would be, and stayed there with his head bowed, and Waya watched him from the step she had taken.

The two older children woke to the sound of their father's breathing. Waya moved them outside before they could see the mat. She told them to go to the fire and eat and stay there. Her son looked at her face and went without speaking. Her daughter — nine years old, old enough to understand the shape of what was happening though not all of what it meant — looked back once at the door covering and then followed her brother. Waya watched them go and then went back inside.

The women came before the sun had cleared the tree line. She heard them before she saw them — the low, particular murmur of women arriving at a house where something has happened, a sound she had heard before and had made herself, and which she now received with a stillness that she could feel the women noticing. Mara came first, which was right, because Mara always came first. She was a broad woman with thick, capable hands, and she set those hands on Waya's arms in the doorway and looked at her with the full attention of someone who has sat with many losses and intends to sit with this one.

Waya stepped back from her hands.

Mara let her. She was too wise to press. She went to the mat and knelt and began the work that needed doing, and the other women came in around her, and the house filled with the low sounds of that work — careful, unhurried, the sounds of women who knew what they were doing and were doing it with respect. Waya stood near the wall and let them. She was grateful for their hands on her daughter's body. She could not have said why she could not let those same hands touch her.

Sokan appeared in the doorway. She stood there in the early light with her arms at her sides, and she did not come in, and she did not speak. She was looking at Waya with an expression Waya recognized from the months after the drought, when Sokan had learned that some griefs do not want company. She had learned it then and she held it now, and Waya was grateful to her for standing in the doorway instead of crossing it. She met Sokan's eyes once. Sokan dipped her chin — a small, deliberate acknowledgment, the kind that asks nothing — and then moved back from the door, and Waya heard her settle outside, near enough to be present, far enough to give air.

Waya did not weep. She was aware of not weeping the way you are aware of a stone in your moccasin — a constant, specific pressure that you do not stop to address because stopping is not something you are doing right now. The weeping was there. It was not absent. It was waiting in a place she had put it and she intended to leave it there until she was somewhere the weeping would not require anything of her, would not cause anyone to come toward her with their hands open.

She took her bow from the wall peg. The wood was smooth under her fingers, worn to the shape of her grip over many seasons. She took the quiver from beside it and slung it over her shoulder.

Mara looked up from her work.

"Waya," she said. Just the name. A question and a caution in one syllable.

"The burial will be done without me," Waya said.

It was not a request. Mara looked at her for a long moment with those capable eyes that had seen everything, and then she nodded once and looked back down at her work. She would tell the others what to do. She would tell Ahanu. She would manage it all with the competence she always brought to things that needed managing, and the burial would be right, and Suki would go into the earth correctly, and Waya would not be there to watch it happen.

She stepped through the door.

Sokan was sitting on the ground outside, her back against the house wall, her knees drawn up. She looked at the bow in Waya's hand and the quiver on her shoulder and said nothing. Waya walked past her. She heard Sokan begin to rise — a small sound, the shift of her weight — and then stop. She had learned it. Waya was glad she had learned it.

The village was awake and moving. A woman at the central fire looked up and then looked away. A man mending a net near the creek path went still as she passed. She felt their awareness of her like a change in air pressure, the way the forest goes quiet when something large moves through it. She walked through the middle of the village and out the north side and into the trees without stopping, without turning, without altering her pace.

The forest took her in.

The light came down through the oak canopy in long, moving columns where the wind shifted the upper branches, and the ground under her moccasins was soft with the season's fallen leaves, wet underneath from recent rain and smelling of rot and green growth together — the smell of things ending and beginning at the same time, which she noticed and did not find comforting and did not need to find comforting. She was not here for comfort. She was here because the village was full of people who loved her and she could not bear the weight of being loved right now, could not bear the open hands and the careful faces and the words that were true but would not help.

She moved north along the ridge without a destination. The forest was loud with late-season birds — a woodpecker somewhere above her, working a dead snag with a sound like a small, persistent argument; crows crossing the canopy in ones and twos, calling to each other in their flat, carrying voices. She tracked them without thinking about it, the way her body tracked everything in the forest out of long habit, reading the sounds for what they meant. The crows were moving west, unhurried. Nothing was alarmed. She was alone.

She walked until the village sounds were gone and then she walked further, until she could hear the creek below the ridge — the low, continuous sound of moving water over stone — and she stopped at a place where a fallen hickory had come down across the slope, its root ball torn up and leaving a raw wound of orange earth, and she sat on the trunk and set her bow across her knees and looked at the creek through the trees.

She had done everything she knew. She had made the tea and laid the moss and held the child in the steam and put her own body alongside her for warmth. She had stayed awake through two nights and into the third morning and she had done everything she knew and it had not been enough, and she had no name for what it was that had been required of her in addition to everything she knew, no name for the gap between what she had done and what would have been sufficient.

She did not speak the child's name aloud. She was not ready for that. She was not ready for what would happen to her when she said it into the open air of the forest with no one to hear it — what would come up through her from wherever she had put it, what she would have to do with that.

She sat on the fallen hickory with her bow across her knees and the creek moving below her and the crows crossing the canopy overhead, and she was very quiet, and the forest was very loud, and she stayed.

She moved south on the second day, following the creek down to where the bottomland opened out and the ground went soft and dark, the soil changing underfoot from the dry leaf-duff of the ridge to something that gave slightly with each step, the moccasin leather darkening with moisture. The bottomland smelled of mud and standing water and the sharp green of crushed sedge where she pushed through the margins of a backwater slough. Cattails stood in the still water, their brown heads going soft and ragged at the tips, trailing white fiber in the windless air. She moved through them without sound.

She had killed a rabbit on the first morning — taken it cleanly at thirty paces with a light arrow, field-dressed it with her chert knife on a flat stone at the water's edge, the blade familiar in her hand, the work automatic. She had eaten half of it that evening over a small fire and left the rest for whatever came in the night. She had not been hungry. She had eaten because the body required it, because she was not here to damage herself, only to be away from the village, and there was a difference.

The cut bank was where the creek bent sharply east, undercutting a shelf of orange clay and tree roots that formed a low overhang, barely high enough to sit under, but dry. She had slept there two nights with her back against the clay and her bow across her thighs. The clay smelled of iron and deep water. Roots hung down over the opening like a fringe, and through them she could see the creek surface in the dark, moving, catching what light there was.

On the third morning she woke before full light and sat watching the creek. A great blue heron was working the shallows twenty paces upstream — standing absolutely still, then striking, then still again. It caught something, tilted its head back, swallowed. Then it stood still again. She watched it for a long time. She was not thinking about it. She was watching it the way you watch fire, the way you watch moving water — not for information, only because the eyes need somewhere to go.

She heard him when he was still well back in the trees.

She had known he would come. Not because he had said anything — he had said nothing, only looked at the bow in her hand and let her go — but because she knew him. He would give her two days. He would not be able to give her three.

She did not turn around. She watched the heron. The heron had not moved.

Ahanu came out of the trees and down the bank and sat beside her at the water's edge. Not close enough to touch. He put his forearms on his knees and looked at the creek. He did not speak.

The heron struck again. Missed this time — the water flashed and the bird reset itself, standing again with the same absolute stillness as before, as if the miss had not happened, as if patience was simply the condition it lived in.

She could feel Ahanu's presence the way you feel a fire at your back — warmth and fact without needing to look at it. He smelled of woodsmoke and the particular sweat of a man who had been moving fast through heavy timber and had stopped. He had tracked her. He had done it alone, which meant he had left Sokan to hold the village, which meant he had trusted Sokan with that, which was not a small thing and she knew it.

He waited.

The creek moved. The heron stood. Somewhere back in the bottomland a frog called once and stopped.

She said, "Suki."

The name came out of her the way a stone comes out of a sling — not thrown, exactly, but released. She felt the release of it move through her chest, felt the shape of it in her mouth, one syllable, two sounds, the name she had given the child in the first days when she was still learning what the child was. The name she had not said aloud since the morning Mara came and the work of the burial began.

She did not say anything else.

Ahanu did not move. He did not put his hand on her. He did not make a sound. He sat with his forearms on his knees and looked at the creek and let the name be in the air between them, let it exist, let it be the thing it was.

The heron struck a third time and this time it held something — she could see the small thrashing at the end of the long bill — and tilted its head back and swallowed and then, without warning, opened its great wings and lifted off the water with a sound like a sail filling, and was gone over the treeline.

The creek moved.

She felt something shift in her — not breaking, not releasing in any clean or manageable way, but a small internal movement, like a stone settling deeper into mud. The name was out now. It was in the air. It would be in the air from now on, the way all the names of the dead were in the air, which was where they belonged, which was why the living said them.

She had not been ready. She was still not ready. She had said it anyway.

Ahanu was quiet beside her. His breathing was slow and even, a man who had run hard and was now still, a man who had learned — and she thought of this without bitterness, only with a flat, clear recognition — that the most useful thing he could do for her was to take up no more space than he needed to. He had learned it badly at first and then better and now, sitting here on the clay bank with the creek moving in front of them and the child's name still hanging in the cold morning air, he was doing it well.

She sat for a while longer. The light was coming up properly now, filtering down through the bare upper branches of the sycamores along the creek, white bark catching it and throwing it back. The bottomland smelled of mud and cold water and somewhere upstream the iron smell of the clay bank behind her.

She stood.

She did not look at Ahanu. She picked up her bow from the ground beside her and settled the quiver on her shoulder and stepped up from the water's edge onto the bank. Her moccasins were dark with moisture, soft with it, and she felt the cold of the wet leather against her feet and did not mind it.

She began walking north.

She heard Ahanu rise behind her — the shift of his weight, the adjustment of his body — and then his footsteps on the bank, a few paces back. Not beside her. Behind her, where she had not asked him to be and where he understood, without being told, that she needed him to be.

The bottomland moved past her. The cattails, the slough, the dark soft ground. She walked back up through the sedge and into the trees where the ground firmed under her feet, the leaf-duff dry again on the ridge, the smell shifting from mud and water to the drier smell of bark and old leaves and woodsmoke carried faintly on the wind from the direction of the village.

She did not know what she would do when she got there. She did not know what she would say to Sokan, or to the woman at the central fire who had looked up and looked away, or to any of them. She walked north through the trees and did not try to know.

The village was awake.

She heard it before she saw it — the knock of a stone maul against a post, a child's voice rising and then cut off, the low sound of a woman singing something tuneless while she worked. The smell of woodsmoke thickened as they came through the last of the trees and the ground opened into the cleared space around the houses, the earth packed hard and pale between the structures, the central fire sending a thin column of smoke straight up into the still morning air.

Waya stopped at the edge of the trees. Ahanu came up beside her. Not behind her now — beside her, which was right. They were back. The three days were over.

He did not say anything. He looked at the village the way she did, taking it in.

Sokan was at the far side of the central fire, crouched over something, her back to them. Two children ran across the open ground between the houses and vanished around a corner. A dog lifted its head from where it lay near the woodpile, looked at them, and put its head back down.

No one had seen them yet.

Ahanu reached up and lifted the shell gorget from around his own neck.

She heard the cord go taut and then the small sound as it cleared his head, and she looked at him. He was holding it out to her. The gorget lay across his palm — the carved shell, the concentric circles, the raptor worked into the face of it with whatever tool the maker had used, the single suspension hole with its twisted cord. He had worn it since Sokan gave it to him. It had been against his skin through the drought and the rain and the reconciliation and the argument and all the rest of it. It was warm from his body.

He said: "Wear it until you are ready to give it to someone else."

She looked at it for a long moment.

She reached out and took it from his palm.

She did not put it on. She closed her fist around it. The carved shell was cool and smooth against her fingers, the cord trailing from her fist. She felt the slight weight of it, less than she expected, the way carved shell always was — lighter than stone, lighter than copper, lighter than the thing it carried.

Ahanu nodded once. He picked up his bow from where he had slung it over his shoulder and walked into the village. She watched him go. He crossed the open ground toward the central fire and Sokan looked up and saw him, and Waya watched the small shift in Sokan's body — not relief exactly, not surprise, something more practical than either — and then Sokan went back to her work.

Waya stood at the edge of the trees a moment longer.

Then she walked in.

She went to her own house without stopping at the fire. The door-flap was tied back — someone had been in, or the morning air was being let through, or Sokan had done it without thinking. The interior was dim, the hearth-coals still faintly orange under their ash, the smell of woodsmoke and dried maize and the particular smell of her own house, which she had never been able to name exactly but which she recognized the way you recognized a voice. The sleeping mats were rolled against the wall. The bundles of dried herbs hung from the roof beam where she had tied them before she left — the yarrow, the dried sumac berries, the bundle of cattail she had meant to work into a mat and had not gotten to. Everything exactly where she had left it.

She sat down on the sleeping platform and opened her fist.

The gorget lay in her palm. In the dimmer light of the house it looked different — the carved lines deeper, the raptor's eye a shadow rather than a mark. She turned it over. The back was smooth, unworked, the natural curve of the shell still visible in the way the surface caught the light from the smoke hole.

She sat with it for a while.

Outside, the village sounds continued: the maul, the child, the low singing. The dog barked once at something and stopped. She heard the fire snap as someone added wood. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a morning that did not know what it was the morning of.

She found the cord she wanted in the bag at the foot of the sleeping platform — twisted sinew, stronger than plant fiber, less likely to stretch wet. She had used it for other things. She cut a length with the chert knife she kept at her belt, the same knife she had carried into the forest three days ago, the blade still clean because she had taken only the one rabbit. She threaded the cord through the suspension hole. Her fingers were steadier than she expected. She tied it at the back of her neck with a knot she trusted.

The gorget settled against her chest. She pressed her palm flat against it and felt the slight hardness of the shell under her hand, the slight give of her own skin beneath.

She did not know what *ready* felt like. She had told herself, walking back through the ridge trees, that she would know it when it came. She was not sure she believed that. She was not sure *ready* was a thing that arrived from the inside rather than a thing that came from the outside — a circumstance rather than a condition, someone who needed what she had, a moment that called for it, a hand held out the way Ahanu had held his out to her this morning at the edge of the trees.

She would wear it until then. That was what he had said. That was what she would do.

She pressed her palm against the gorget a moment longer. Then she let her hand drop.

She stood. She retied her belt and straightened the quiver on her shoulder and pushed the door-flap aside and walked back out into the morning. The smoke from the central fire went straight up in the still air. Sokan was still crouched at the far side of it, working something with her hands — Waya could see now that it was a mat, the reeds spread across her knees. She did not look up.

Waya went to the woodpile and began stacking wood for her own hearth. The logs were split hickory, still fragrant, the cut ends pale and sharp-smelling. She worked steadily, carrying an armload at a time, and the gorget moved against her chest with each trip, the cord settling into its new position, the shell warming slowly with the work and the morning sun that was coming now at a low angle over the eastern treeline and laying long shadows across the packed earth of the village ground.

No one spoke to her. No one looked at her in a way that required anything from her. The village moved around her the way a creek moved around a stone — not ignoring it, not making much of it, simply continuing.

She was glad of it. She was not ready to be spoken to. She was not ready to speak. She was ready only to carry wood and feel the gorget against her chest and breathe the woodsmoke morning air of the place she lived, the place she had come back to, the place that was still, despite everything, the place where she belonged.

The hickory was heavy and fragrant and real in her arms. She carried it inside. She knelt at the hearth and blew the coals back to life and fed the fire with small pieces first and then larger ones, the way her mother had taught her, and watched the flame take hold. The smoke rose and found the smoke hole and went out into the morning sky above the village.

She sat back on her heels and watched the fire.

Suki's name was in the air. It would stay in the air. It was where it needed to be. The gorget was at her throat. That was where it needed to be, for now, for as long as now lasted — and she did not know how long that would be, could not have said, sitting here in the firelight with the shell warm against her chest and the morning coming in through the smoke hole, whether it would be a season or a year or twenty years before she understood what *ready* meant and held the gorget out to someone else the way Ahanu had held it out to her.

Twenty years. The thought arrived without warning and sat there, neither frightening nor comforting, simply present: it might be twenty years.

She could carry that. She thought she could carry that.

The fire settled and breathed. Outside, a child's voice rose, higher this time, and then laughter — another child, and then a third — and the sound of running feet on the packed earth, moving away toward the far end of the village. The dog barked once, joyfully, and was silent.

Waya put her hands on her knees and felt the warmth of the fire on her face and sat very still, the gorget resting against her breastbone, lighter than the thing she was carrying — which was not the gorget, which was only a name, and which she would carry until she couldn't, and then a little longer after that.

Twenty years.

She had thought, sitting at that fire with her hands on her knees, that she could carry it. She had been right. What she had not known was that carrying it would become so ordinary — that the gorget would stop being a thing she was aware of and become simply part of the weight of herself, the way a scar stops being a wound and becomes skin.

Seasons passed. The planting moons and the ripe-corn moons and the cold moons when the creek froze at its edges and the ice groaned in the dark. She wore the gorget through all of them. She wore it when she knelt in the garden turning soil with a digging stick, the shell swinging forward and tapping the earth. She wore it the summer the deer were scarce and the men came back from three days out with nothing and the village ate cattail root and dried sumac berries for a week, and she went out alone with her bow and came back with two rabbits and a grouse and did not say where she had gone. She wore it when Ahanu's hair went fully white — not gradually, the way most people's did, but in a single winter, so that by the time the planting moon came again he was entirely silver and looked like a different man standing at the edge of the same fire. She wore it when Sokan died, quietly, in her sleep, in the cold season of the seventh year, and Ahanu sat outside Sokan's house for an entire day without moving, and Waya brought him food he did not eat and sat beside him without speaking, and the gorget rested between them like a third presence.

She wore it when Ahanu died.

That was the twelfth year. A fever, like Suki's, but slower — three weeks of it, the kind that took a person down in stages, each day a little less of him present behind his eyes. She sat with him near the end and held his hand, which had become very light, the bones close to the surface, and he looked at the gorget on her chest and said nothing about it. He did not need to. His face said what it said. She pressed his hand once and he pressed back, weakly, and that was the last conversation they had that meant anything, though he lived two more days after it and said other things — her name, Sokan's name, a word she didn't recognize, perhaps something from his childhood, something that belonged to a time before she knew him.

They buried him properly. She spoke his name at the mound. She did not weep at the mound — she wept later, alone, in the dark of her house, her face pressed into a rolled sleeping mat so no one would hear, the gorget pressing into her cheek, the shell hard and cool against her skin.

After that she was the oldest one. It surprised her, each time she thought of it, though it should not have. She had watched the older ones go one by one, had done the sitting-with and the speaking-of-names and the carrying of food to houses that had lost someone, and somewhere in all that sitting and carrying she had become the one others brought food to, the one they sat beside. She did not feel old. She felt like herself, which was perhaps what old felt like from the inside.

Her children grew. Her son Ritu, the youngest living, grew broad and serious and married a woman from the eastern band who laughed at everything and made him laugh too, which Waya had not been sure he knew how to do. Her middle daughter married and moved two days' walk south and came back for the planting ceremonies with her own children in tow, small ones who pulled at Waya's skirt and looked up at her with their mother's eyes. And her oldest — the one who had been nine years old the winter Suki died, old enough to have understood the shape of what was happening though not all of what it meant, old enough to have watched Waya walk back in from three days in the forest and said nothing, only taken the rabbits from her hands and begun to dress them — her oldest stayed.

She built her house on the south side of the village and kept her garden and learned the names of the dead the way Waya had learned them, sitting at fires, listening, asking questions that were not quite questions. She had her father's way of being still. She had something of Waya too — a directness, a willingness to sit with difficult things without flinching from them. Waya watched her and thought: she is ready. And then thought: ready for what? And then understood, slowly, over the course of a season, that she was thinking about the gorget.

She did not act on it immediately. She waited until she was certain — not that her daughter was ready, because she had decided that ready was not a condition that arrived cleanly, but that the moment was right, which was a different thing. The moment was right on an evening in the late summer of the twentieth year, when the air had begun to carry the first edge of coming cold beneath its warmth, and the fireflies had stopped but the crickets had not, and her daughter came and sat beside her at the fire outside Waya's house without being asked, the way she sometimes did, and they sat together in the cricket-loud dark and said nothing for a while, and Waya felt the gorget on her chest and knew.

She reached up and lifted the cord from her neck.

Her daughter watched but did not speak.

Waya held the gorget in her palm. The shell was warm from her skin — twenty years of her skin — and in the firelight the carved lines of it caught the orange light and made small shadows, the concentric circles and the bird at the center looking, as it always had, like something that had just landed and might leave again at any moment.

"I need to tell you something," Waya said.

Her daughter turned to face her fully. She had her father's eyes, dark and patient. She waited.

"This belonged to Ahanu," Waya said. "Before that it belonged to Sokan. Before that I don't know — Sokan knew, but she didn't say and I didn't ask, and that is a thing I regret." She paused. "Don't do that. When someone tells you where a thing came from, hold the whole story, not just the part that starts with you."

Her daughter nodded once. She was watching the gorget.

"Ahanu gave it to me the morning I came back from the forest. You remember when I went into the forest."

It was not a question. Her daughter said, "I remember."

"I went because your sister died." Waya said it plainly, the way you say the name of a thing you have been carrying so long it has lost its power to surprise you but none of its weight. "Suki. She was two years old. She had a fever for four days and then she was gone. You were nine. I don't know what you understood."

"I understood she was gone," her daughter said. "I didn't understand where you went."

"Into the forest. I needed three days without anyone speaking to me." Waya turned the gorget over in her palm. "When I came back Ahanu was waiting at the edge of the trees. He gave me this. He said: wear it until you are ready to give it to someone else." She looked at her daughter. "I have been wearing it for twenty years."

Her daughter was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "Why now?"

"Because I am old," Waya said, "and because you are here, and because I know you will hold the whole story and not just the part that starts with you."

She held the gorget out.

Her daughter took it. She held it in both hands the way you hold something that belongs to someone else — carefully, the weight of it acknowledged. She looked at the carved bird at the center.

"Suki," Waya said.

Her daughter looked up.

"That is the name you carry with this. Suki. She was two years old. She died of a fever in the planting season, the year after the hard winter. She held your father's hand at the end and she did not cry." Waya stopped. The crickets were very loud. She said it again: "Suki." And then a third time, slowly, so the shape of it would sit in her daughter's memory the way a stone sits in a streambed, worn smooth but unmoved: "Suki."

Her daughter said it back to her, quietly. "Suki."

"Good." Waya let out a breath. "Now. The gorget goes to you. You wear it or you keep it, that is your choice. But when the time comes — when you know what I mean by *the time comes* — it goes to the mound. Not buried deep. Placed at the edge of the second layer, where Ahanu is, where Sokan is. You'll know the place."

"I know the place," her daughter said.

"And when you give it to the mound, you say the names. All of them, back to the beginning. Sokan, Ahanu, Suki." She paused. "And mine."

Her daughter looked at her.

"I won't be here," Waya said, without bitterness, simply stating the arithmetic of it. "Say my name too. So the mound knows who carried it."

Her daughter's hands tightened slightly around the gorget. She did not speak for a moment. Then she said, "I will."

Waya nodded. She felt lighter — not empty, not diminished, but the specific lightness of a thing set down after a long carry. The cord was gone from her neck. She could feel its absence the way you feel the absence of a sound that has been present so long you stopped hearing it: a new quiet, not comfortable yet, but real.

She looked at the fire. The wood was hickory — it always seemed to be hickory, in her memory, the whole of her life smelling faintly of hickory smoke — and it had burned down to a deep orange bed of coals that gave off more heat than light. The crickets went on. The dark pressed in from the treeline, the shapes of the oaks at the edge of the village just visible against the sky.

"One more thing," Waya said.

Her daughter waited.

"The story doesn't start with you. It doesn't start with me. It doesn't start with Ahanu or Sokan." She gestured at the gorget in her daughter's hands. "Someone made that. Someone carried it before Sokan. I don't know who. Sokan knew and didn't say." She looked at her daughter steadily. "So you say what you know. You say Sokan, Ahanu, Suki, Waya. You say: before that I don't know, but there was a before. You don't pretend the story starts where your memory starts. You leave the door open for the part you can't see."

Her daughter looked down at the gorget for a long moment. Then she lifted the cord and settled it around her own neck, and the shell came to rest against her chest, and she pressed her palm flat against it the way Waya had pressed her palm against it ten thousand times over twenty years.

"I'll remember," her daughter said.

Waya believed her. She believed her the way she had believed Ahanu when he pressed the gorget into her hands — not because she had proof of it, but because the believing was itself a kind of act, a choice made in the direction of the future rather than against it. She had no way to know what her daughter would do with the story when Waya was no longer here to ask. She had no way to know if her daughter's daughter would hold it, or her daughter's daughter's daughter, or whether the chain would thin somewhere down the years the way all chains thinned — a name mispronounced, a detail lost, a season when no one had time to sit at a fire and speak the old words. She could not know. She could only do what Ahanu had done: hold the thing out, say the name, trust the hand that took it.

The coals breathed orange in the dark. Her daughter sat beside her, the gorget at her throat, her face turned toward the fire. She was thirty years old and she looked, in this light, the way Waya had looked at thirty — before Suki, before the forest, before the weight of carrying a name for twenty years had settled into her the way smoke settles into wool: permanent, invisible, impossible to wash out.

Waya put her hands on her knees. She was tired. It was a good tiredness, the kind that came from a thing finished rather than a thing abandoned.

She did not know if her daughter understood what the mound was — not fully, not the way Ahanu had understood it, not the way the ones before Ahanu had understood it. That was the question she could not answer tonight. She had given her daughter the gorget and the names and the instruction. Whether her daughter held all of it, or only part, whether the story she carried forward would be the whole story or a thinned version of it, a shape without all its detail — that was not Waya's to know. It never had been. Every person who had ever held this thing out to another person had faced the same not-knowing, and had held it out anyway, and that was the only way the chain had ever worked: an act of faith made in the dark, with fire for light, and the names said three times so they would hold.

Suki.

Waya said it one last time, silently, in the place behind her teeth where words lived before they became sound. The gorget was gone from her chest. The name was still there. It would be there until she wasn't.

The fire breathed. Her daughter sat beside her and did not speak, and the dark held them both, and somewhere at the far edge of the village a child called out once and was answered, and the night went on.
