The field notes were spread across Wren's desk in the order she'd made them — unit B, unit C, the east transect — each page covered in her small, crowded handwriting and flagged with color-coded tabs she'd bought at a dollar store in La Plata. The copper pendant measurement was on the third page. She'd written the number twice, once in pencil and once in ink, the way Tayak had taught her, because pencil fades and ink bleeds but you need both to survive the field.

Her phone lit up with a 301 number she didn't recognize. Southern Maryland. She almost let it go.

"Wren." Her mother's voice came through flat and careful, the way it got when she was choosing words. "I need to tell you something."

Wren set down her pen. "What happened?"

"A woman called this morning. An attorney. From Pruitt's firm in Prince Frederick — Lester Pruitt. She left a voicemail, then called back when I didn't answer. She was asking about the Calvert land grant."

The room didn't change. The notes were still spread across the desk. The window still showed the same grey strip of campus sky. Wren heard herself say, "What did she want to know?"

"Whether we still held any interest in the 1687 patent. Her words. She said she was running the chain — something about a boundary description that references a Piscataway settlement." A pause. "She was very polite. I gave her nothing."

"Okay." Wren picked up the pen and put it down again. "Did she say who she was working for?"

"She said she couldn't disclose her client. She left a number." Her mother read it out. Wren wrote it in the margin of the nearest field note page without thinking, then looked at what she'd done and felt something cold move through her.

"Mom." She kept her voice even. "Did you know about the boundary description before I took this job?"

The pause was two seconds too long.

"I found it in Gran's papers," her mother said. "After she died. There was a copy of the patent — old paper, photocopy of something older. The boundary line said something about the Piscataway settlement at the high ground." Another pause. "I read it. I set it aside."

"And when I took the field position with Tayak."

"Yes."

"You knew."

"Wren—"

"I'm not angry." She was, a little. Or not angry — something more specific than anger, a feeling like standing on ground that had shifted without making a sound. "I just need to understand what I'm working with."

"I didn't think it was relevant." Her mother's voice had gone tight. "It's an old boundary description. It doesn't mean we own anything. Gran never did anything with it."

"It means our family's name is in the title chain of the land I've been excavating for two months."

Silence.

"That's a conflict of interest," Wren said. "That's what it means."

Her mother didn't answer that directly. She said, "What are you going to do?"

Wren looked at the field notes. The pendant measurement, written twice. The careful tabs. Everything she'd logged, countersigned, handed forward. Two months of work on ground that her ancestor had been granted in 1687, bounded by the settlement of the people whose burial mound she'd been uncovering with a trowel and a brush.

"I don't know yet," she said. "I'll call you back."

She set the phone on the desk face-down.

The office was quiet. Down the hall someone was running water in the break room sink. Through the window the sky had gone the color of old pewter, the kind of flat November light that made everything look like it was waiting for something to happen.

Wren turned the nearest field note page over and looked at the blank back. She thought about Kimi. Kimi, who had eighty thousand followers and a researcher's instinct for pulling threads, who had been working the title chain on the Calvert connection for weeks — Wren had seen the posts, the screenshots of deed images with red highlights around phrases like *bounded by the Piscataway settlement at the high ground.* Kimi had gotten there. And whatever she'd found had moved into legal channels, because Lester Pruitt's firm didn't run title chains to chat. They called because someone had hired them to clear a cloud on a title, or to locate heirs, or to find out whether anyone still had a claim.

The Calvert connection was no longer just an archival footnote. It was a phone call. It was an attorney's voicemail on her mother's machine.

Wren picked up the pen and wrote Pruitt's number again at the top of a clean page, properly this time, with the date beside it. Then she wrote: *Kimi's research has surfaced the chain. Moving through legal channels.* She underlined *legal channels* once, the way she underlined things in the field when she wasn't sure what they meant yet but knew they meant something.

She thought about Tayak. About what Tayak had asked her to keep, and what Tayak didn't yet know was already in motion.

She turned the page over. Turned it back.

Outside, the first drops of rain hit the window glass — not a storm, just the slow, deliberate kind of Maryland rain that settles in and doesn't leave.

The field log was still open on her laptop from the morning.

Wren hadn't closed it. She'd meant to finish the entry for the copper pendant — provenance note, chain of custody, Adaeze's conservation suite timestamp — but her mother's call had come in before she'd gotten past the header, and now the cursor sat blinking in the middle of an unfinished sentence: *Artifact 18CH-447-051, copper alloy pendant, recovered from Stratum Five at depth—*

She pulled the laptop closer and scrolled up.

Forty-seven entries. Two months of work, running from the glass bead in early September to the pendant this week, each one logged in the same format: date, stratum, depth in centimeters, artifact description, field condition, disposition. And at the bottom of each entry, two signature lines. Tayak's in the left column. Wren's in the right.

She'd signed every one.

She sat with that for a moment. The rain was still coming down outside, slow and even, the kind that didn't announce itself. The office smelled of coffee gone cold and the faint chemical tang of the fixative she'd used on the gorget fiber samples three weeks ago.

She scrolled to the first entry. September 9. The glass bead. *Wren Calvert,* in her own handwriting, scanned and uploaded to the university's digital repository. She scrolled to the next. September 14. A cord-marked rim sherd from the eastern face — Delia's find, but Wren had countersigned the bag. September 19. A fire-cracked hearthstone, probably not diagnostic, but logged anyway because Tayak logged everything. *Wren Calvert.* September 23. October 4. October 11.

She stopped scrolling.

The problem wasn't the signatures. The signatures were correct — she'd been present, she'd verified the chain of custody, the documentation was clean. The problem was what the signatures meant if someone decided to look at them from the outside. If someone decided to argue that the field team's documentation was compromised because one of the two people who had countersigned every artifact bag had a family name in the title chain of the land being excavated.

The attorney at Pruitt's firm would find that out. If she was running the chain, she'd run it all the way to the present, and the present included a field assistant named Calvert working an active excavation at Site 18CH-447.

She opened a new browser tab and typed *Maryland land records* into the search bar, then stopped. She knew what she'd find. She'd been through it already, weeks ago, when she'd first discovered the connection — the MDLANDREC portal, the colonial patent images, the metes and bounds description with its reference to *the Piscataway settlement at the high ground adjacent to Mattawoman Creek.* She'd pulled the liber and folio numbers, cross-referenced with the county deed books, traced the chain from Thomas Calvert's 1687 patent through the Harrington partition and back into the Calvert name through Mary Harrington's marriage in 1793. She knew the chain. She'd built it herself.

What she hadn't done was tell Tayak that her mother had known before Wren took the position.

She'd told Tayak about the ancestry. That conversation was in the record — Wren tells Tayak, Tayak receives it, they work out what to do. That was weeks ago. What Wren had not said, in that conversation or any conversation since, was that her mother had found the patent copy in her grandmother's estate papers and read the boundary description and said nothing, not when Wren applied for the position, not when Wren started in the field, not until Wren had been on site for six weeks and called home to mention what they'd found in Stratum One.

Even then her mother hadn't said it directly. She'd said *that's interesting* in a careful voice, and Wren had filed the carefulness away and not examined it.

She closed the browser tab.

The thing about a conflict of interest was that it didn't have to be malicious to be a conflict. It just had to exist. The question a hearing would ask — the question Slade's office would ask, if it came to that — was not *did you intend harm* but *what did you know and when did you know it.* And the answer, for Wren, was: she had known about the ancestry for two months. Her mother had known for longer. And neither of them had disclosed the full picture when disclosure would have been cleanest.

She pulled up the evidence bag log — the physical log, not the digital one, scanned and uploaded as a PDF. The bags were numbered sequentially. Each one had a handwritten label: artifact number, date, stratum, initials of the recovering excavator, initials of the witness. Tayak's initials were T.M. Wren's were W.C.

*W.C.* Forty-seven times. On bags that a property attorney was now, somewhere in Prince Frederick, working backward through a title chain to reach.

She thought about what Kimi had been doing.

Kimi had been pulling the deed images for weeks — Wren had seen the posts, the screenshots with their red digital highlights. Kimi was good at research in the way that people were good at things when they cared about them more than they cared about being careful. She'd found the Calvert connection in the colonial records, found the boundary language, found the Piscataway settlement reference. And then she'd apparently taken it somewhere, or someone had taken it from her, because the next step after archival research was legal action, and legal action was someone from Pruitt's firm calling Wren's mother on a Tuesday morning.

The question was who Pruitt was working for.

If the firm was working for someone trying to strengthen the recognition case — a tribal attorney, an advocacy group — then the Calvert title chain was about to become evidence of Piscataway presence at the high ground, which was exactly what Tayak needed. The 1687 boundary language was direct: *bounded by the Piscataway settlement at the high ground.* A colonial grantee's own patent, describing the Indigenous community's presence on the land as a fixed geographic marker. That was the kind of primary source that made historians stop and read it twice.

But if Pruitt was working for someone else — for a development interest, for a title insurance company, for anyone who needed the site's documentation to be clean and found instead that it wasn't — then the Calvert connection became a different kind of evidence. It became a chain-of-custody problem. It became a question about whether the excavation's field documentation could be trusted when one of the two people who had countersigned every artifact bag had a family interest in the land.

Wren picked up her pen. She wrote at the top of the clean page: *Best case / Worst case.*

Under *Best case* she wrote: *Pruitt firm working for tribal counsel or allied attorney. Calvert boundary language enters the record as evidence of Piscataway presence. Recognition case strengthened. Conflict of interest flagged, managed, documented. Field team's work stands.*

She looked at that for a moment, then wrote under *Worst case*: *Pruitt firm working for Slade's office, or for development interests, or for anyone who needs the site documentation to fail. Calvert name in the title chain becomes the story. Every bag I countersigned gets questioned. Tayak's field record — forty-seven entries, two months of careful work — gets challenged on the grounds that her field assistant had an undisclosed interest in the land.*

She underlined *undisclosed.*

Because that was the word. She had disclosed the ancestry. She had not disclosed that her mother had known before she took the position. That gap — two weeks, maybe three, between when her mother had read the patent copy and when Wren had started in the field — was small enough that it could be explained. It was also large enough that it could be used.

She set the pen down.

The rain had picked up slightly, tapping now against the glass with more intention. Down the hall, whoever had been running water in the break room had stopped. The building had gone quiet in the way that buildings went quiet in the late afternoon, when most people had left and the ones who remained were working too hard to make noise.

She looked at the laptop screen. The field log entry was still unfinished. *Recovered from Stratum Five at depth—*

She typed: *112 centimeters below datum. Artifact in situ, undisturbed. Chain of custody: T. Moyaone (excavating), W. Calvert (witness and transport). Evidence bag 18CH-447-051 sealed and signed at 14:23, transported to conservation suite, received by A. Okonkwo at 15:05.*

She saved the entry.

Then she sat back and looked at what she'd written. It was accurate. It was complete. It was exactly what the log should say. And it would be the last entry she countersigned before she told Tayak the full story — not the partial story she'd told weeks ago, but the whole thing, including her mother, including the timing, including the fact that she'd known about the boundary description and had not thought to ask her mother directly until today.

She owed Tayak that. She'd owed it to her for weeks, and she'd been paying the debt in silence, which was its own kind of interest.

She picked up her phone. She put it back down.

Not tonight. Tonight she needed to know more about Pruitt — which client, what the firm was actually after. She needed to know whether the Calvert connection was about to become a weapon or a tool, because the answer changed what she said to Tayak and how she said it. Telling Tayak that the title chain was moving through legal channels without knowing whose hands it was in was the same as handing her a live wire and saying *I think this might be important.*

She opened a new tab and typed *Lester Pruitt attorney Prince Frederick Maryland.*

The first result was a firm in Calvert County. Property and estate law, the website said, established 1987. Three attorneys. Pruitt was the senior partner, the photograph showing a heavyset man in his sixties with a careful haircut and a tie that cost more than it needed to. The firm's practice areas were listed in a clean column: *residential and commercial real estate, estate administration, title examination, quiet title actions.*

Quiet title.

She read that twice.

A quiet title action was what you filed when the ownership of a piece of land was uncertain — when there was a cloud on the title, a competing claim, a gap in the chain. You filed it to settle the question once and for all, to make the court declare who held the land and clear everyone else's claim away.

Someone had hired a quiet title firm to run the chain on the Calvert land grant.

Which meant someone believed there was still a cloud on it.

She sat with that for a long moment, listening to the rain. The evidence bags. The countersignatures. The forty-seven entries. The 1687 patent with its boundary description that named the Piscataway settlement as a fixed point on the landscape, as permanent and real as a creek or a bounded oak.

Someone thought that language was still live.

And they were right.

She didn't call Tayak that night.

She told herself she would know more in the morning. She told herself she needed to understand whose hands the title chain was in before she handed Tayak anything. She told herself a lot of things while she washed her coffee cup and locked up and drove home through rain that had settled into a steady, windless fall, the kind that didn't announce itself and didn't stop.

She could call Tayak. She had the number memorized. She'd dialed it probably forty times in the last two months — artifact questions, log discrepancies, a broken camera mount, the afternoon the site truck had a flat and she'd needed to know whether to call the department or the field account. Tayak always answered, or called back within the hour. The conversation would take maybe fifteen minutes. *There's a firm in Prince Frederick running the chain on the Calvert grant. Quiet title. I don't know whose client they're working for. I think Kimi's research got close enough to the boundary language that someone decided to move.* Fifteen minutes, maybe twenty.

The field road was dry enough that she could take it all the way to the first gate without dropping into four-wheel drive, which surprised her. The recent rain had packed the gravel rather than washing it out. She parked just past the gate where the road widened into a shallow turnaround, the truck angled so she could see the site from the driver's seat without getting out.

The mound was visible from here, or what the mound had become — the long, careful cut of the excavation trench running east to west across its southern face, the profile wall held back by timber bracing, the blue tarp over the open section weighted at the corners with sandbags she'd helped fill herself. The morning light came in flat and pale from the east, and the mound's shadow fell long and specific across the cut grass of the perimeter, pointing toward the tree line the way a finger points at something it wants you to look at.

She'd stood in that trench for two months. She knew the smell of it — wet clay and iron and something older underneath, a dry mineral smell that came up from below the second stratum when the air was right, like the inside of a stone building that had been closed for a long time. She knew the feel of the profile wall under her gloved hand, the way the stratigraphy changed texture as it changed color, the dense dark band of the second fill giving way to the looser, paler matrix below. She knew which corner of the tarp leaked when the wind came from the northwest and which sandbag had a slow split along the seam that she'd been meaning to replace for three weeks.

She knew the site the way she knew the field log — entry by entry, bag by bag, depth by depth. She'd built that knowledge carefully, the way Tayak had taught her to build it, without shortcuts and without assumptions.

And her name was on every bag.

She got out of the truck.

The grass along the perimeter was still damp, and her boots darkened at the toe within the first twenty steps. She walked the fence line without going through the gate, following it east along the marked boundary until she reached the corner post where the orange survey tape had faded from the summer to a pale salmon, barely distinguishable from the dry seed-heads of the grasses around it. From here she could see the full length of the trench. The timber bracing. The careful slope of the backdirt pile, separated by stratum the way Tayak insisted, each layer kept distinct so the sequence could be read. Nothing was mixed. Nothing was lost. The record was clean.

She stood there.

Across the field, maybe a hundred and fifty meters, Dawes's truck was parked beside the equipment shed. She could see him moving near the shed's open door, doing his morning rounds — checking the padlocks, walking the perimeter the way he did every day, unhurried and methodical. He moved the way people moved when they'd been doing the same job long enough that the job had become a kind of thinking. He came around the corner of the shed and saw her.

He raised a hand.

She raised hers.

He didn't change his pace or his direction. He finished his circuit of the shed and went back inside, which was either incuriosity or tact. She thought probably tact. Dawes had been on this land longer than anyone except the mound itself, and he'd learned to read what the people here needed, which was usually to be left alone with whatever they were working through.

She turned back to the trench.

*Every bag I countersigned gets questioned.*

She'd written that last night and underlined it, and standing here now with the actual trench in front of her she understood for the first time what it would mean in practice. Not as an abstraction — not as a worst-case line in a list — but as a physical fact. Forty-seven evidence bags, each one sealed with a label in two hands, hers and Tayak's, each one a link in the chain of custody that made the artifacts legally recoverable as evidence. The copper pendant. The shell gorget. The ceramic pipe bowl. The colonial document. The glass bead. Each one pulled from the ground with documented care, each one photographed in situ, each one logged with depth and orientation and associated matrix before it was moved. The record was meticulous. Tayak had made it meticulous on purpose, because she'd known from the beginning that this site would be contested.

And Wren had made it meticulous too, faithfully, for two months, and her family's name was written in the 1687 patent as a fixed point on the landscape, as real and permanent as the mound itself. And her mother had known that when Wren took the position.

The question she hadn't let herself ask directly until now was whether that made the record contaminated, or whether it made her a witness whose testimony was complicated. Those were different things. They would be treated as the same thing by anyone who needed them to be.

She looked at the mound for a long time. The tarp. The bracing. The pale winter light coming in low and flat across the cut face of the profile wall, which from here showed its stratigraphy the way a book shows its pages when you hold it spine-up — all the layers visible at once, each one a different color, each one a different century.

The attorney would keep running the chain. She would find what Kimi had found, or more than Kimi had found, because the firm had access to the full deed record and the patience of people who billed by the hour. They would find the boundary description. They would find the language that named the Piscataway settlement as a fixed point — *bounded east by the Indian high ground at Mattawoman, being the ancient place of their dead* — and whoever had hired them would decide what to do with it. If that person was allied with tribal counsel, the language would enter the record as evidence of Piscataway presence and the recognition case would be strengthened. If that person was Slade's office, or development interests, or anyone who needed the site documentation to fail, the language would become a question about the chain of custody, and the question would have Wren's name in it.

She had no way to know which it was without knowing the client. She'd tried to find out this morning and been politely refused. She could try other approaches — call the Charles County courthouse, check the land records portal for any recently filed instruments on the parcel, search the SDAT database for the tax account and see if there'd been any ownership changes or pending actions. She could do all of that. It would take the rest of the day and she might still not know whose hands the chain was in.

Or she could call Tayak and tell her what she knew, which was that someone was running the chain, and that the chain led to a boundary description that named the Piscataway settlement, and that Wren's family had held that land, and that Wren's mother had known this before Wren started in the field.

She could give Tayak the live wire and say *I think this is important* and trust Tayak to know what to do with it.

She looked at the trench one more time. The profile wall. The careful slope of the backdirt. The faded survey tape at the corner post, salmon-pink in the flat light.

She walked back to the truck.

She spent the afternoon at her kitchen table with the laptop and a legal pad, running the chain herself through the Maryland land records portal, pulling deed images by liber and folio, working backward from the most recent instrument she could find on the parcel. The scanned pages came up grey and slightly skewed, the colonial-era entries in a cramped secretary hand that required zooming in until the individual letters resolved from ambiguity into legibility. She'd done this before, in the weeks after she'd found the 1687 patent, but she'd stopped when she'd found what she was looking for. Now she kept going.

The 1741 transfer from Anne Calvert to James Harrington. The 1798 estate partition. Mary Harrington's inheritance of the southern portion. The 1793 marriage that brought the Calvert name back to the land. She followed it forward from there, deed by deed, the tract subdividing and recombining as families died and married and sold, the acreage shrinking with each generation, the archaic patent name — *Friendship* — persisting in the legal descriptions long after the land it described had been cut into parcels that bore no resemblance to the original 200-acre grant.

By three in the afternoon she had traced the chain to a 1962 deed, a transfer between two names she didn't recognize, and then the portal's digitized record ended. The instruments after that would be in the county deed books, physically, in La Plata, and she wasn't going to drive there today.

She sat back and looked at what she had.

The chain was intact. Every transfer documented, every instrument recorded, every acreage accounted for. It was a clean chain, which meant it was also a chain that ended cleanly at a current owner, and a current owner meant a current parcel, and a current parcel adjacent to the mound site meant someone had a legal interest in the land that might or might not be compatible with the excavation permit and the NAGPRA filing and the recognition case and everything Tayak had spent two months building.

She looked at her phone. The light outside had gone the particular flat gold of late afternoon in November, the shadows long and specific, the air through the cracked window carrying the smell of woodsmoke from somewhere down the block and the faint brackish edge of drainage ditches that ran through this part of the county toward the tidal creeks.

The attorney would file what she was going to file. The chain would surface when it surfaced. Nothing she did tonight would change that.

Except that wasn't true. If the chain surfaced in a courtroom before Tayak knew about it, Tayak would be the last person to learn that her field assistant's family had held the adjacent land and that a quiet title action was moving through the county courts. That was not a manageable surprise. That was the kind of surprise that ended careers and broke field records and handed everyone who wanted the site to fail exactly the story they needed.

She picked up her phone.

She put it down.

She picked it up again and held it in both hands, looking at Tayak's name in her contacts list. The contact photo was from the site — Tayak in the trench, looking up at the camera with a trowel in one hand and an expression of mild impatience, the profile wall visible behind her in perfect stratigraphy, every layer distinct.

Wren had taken that photograph.

She set the phone face-down on the table and looked at the legal pad, at the chain of title she'd traced in her own handwriting, deed by deed, generation by generation, from 1687 to 1962. Her family's name running through it like a thread through fabric, holding it together, holding it in place.

She would call in the morning. She would call first thing, before she drove to the site, before she did anything else. She would tell Tayak everything — the attorney, the quiet title action, her mother's knowledge, the timing, all of it. She would hand Tayak the live wire and trust her to know what to do with it.

She told herself that.

Outside, the woodsmoke smell had thickened slightly, and the last of the flat gold light had gone from the windows, and the street was dark and quiet in the way that streets went quiet when the day had finished asking anything of anyone.

She didn't believe herself. But she would call in the morning anyway.

The field notebook was still in her bag.

She hadn't meant to take it home — it belonged on site, or at minimum in the lockbox in her truck — but she'd been distracted when she left, turning the phone call over in her mind, and now it sat on the kitchen table beside her coffee cup and the legal pad with the title chain written out in her own careful hand. She pulled it out and set it flat on the table.

The cover was red, same as Tayak's. Tayak had given her the same brand when she started, a gesture so ordinary it had felt like an initiation. She opened it to the page she already knew was there.

October 14. The entry was in her own handwriting, which she knew well enough to read quickly, the letters small and consistent, the way Tayak had trained her — date, site designation, unit number, depth, context note, object description. Below the last field entry for that day, separated by a ruled line she'd drawn with the edge of her thumbnail, was the note she'd written to herself. Not for the record. For herself, in the margin, in pencil rather than ink, so small she'd had to press hard to make it legible.

*mother knew.*

Two words. No period. She'd written it the way you write something you don't want to think about directly but can't afford to forget.

She sat with it.

The coffee had gone cold while she was running the title chain, and she didn't get up to reheat it. The kitchen was quiet. Her upstairs neighbor had stopped moving around an hour ago. The refrigerator cycled on and made its low, domestic sound, and then cycled off, and the quiet came back.

She had told Tayak — that conversation was in the record, weeks ago — she had told Tayak about the 1687 patent, about Thomas Calvert, about the boundary description that referenced the Piscataway settlement at the high ground. She had told her cleanly and completely, or she had believed she was telling her cleanly and completely, which was not the same thing.

She had not told her that her mother had known before Wren took the position.

She had not told her because at the time it had seemed like a separate fact — her mother's knowledge, her mother's silence, her mother's decision to set the patent copy aside in the estate papers after Wren's grandmother died and not say anything when Wren came home that summer and talked about the field position with Tayak. Those were her mother's choices, not hers. She had found the connection herself, in the archive, in the ordinary course of doing her job. That was what she'd told herself.

The margin note said otherwise.

She had written *mother knew* on October 14, the same day she found the patent, before she'd said a word to Tayak. She had written it because she already understood what it meant. She had understood it and then she had chosen what to include when she texted Tayak that evening, and she had not included that.

Not a lie. Not exactly. But the kind of omission that becomes a lie the longer it sits.

She put her finger on the two words and held it there, as if she could press them back into the page.

If she called Tayak now — tonight, or first thing in the morning as she'd promised herself — she would have to explain why her mother had known. Which meant explaining that her mother had read the boundary description and recognized the high ground and said nothing when Wren took the position. Which meant explaining that Wren had found the patent in October and written *mother knew* in the margin of her field notebook and then told Tayak about the patent without telling her that part.

Tayak would hear it in the right order. She would hear: *a firm in Prince Frederick is running the chain on the Calvert land grant.* And then she would hear: *my mother already knew about the connection when I took this job.* And then she would hear the silence where the next thing should be, the thing Wren hadn't said in October, and she would understand what that silence meant.

Wren had been on enough digs to know how Tayak processed information. She didn't perform reactions. She went still and listened until she had the full picture, and then she asked one or two questions, precise ones, and then she decided what to do. The stillness was not reassurance. The stillness was assessment.

She picked up the phone again.

She opened the text thread with Tayak. The last message was from this morning, a logistics note about the equipment van. Before that, the exchange from October — Wren's text saying *I need to tell you something,* Tayak's response, the brief back-and-forth that had ended with Tayak saying *come to the site tomorrow morning, bring the notebook.* That exchange had felt like honesty at the time. It had been partial honesty, which was a different thing, and partial honesty had a shelf life, and she had been watching it expire for six weeks.

She could text. It would be easier to text. She could write it out, the attorney, the quiet title action, her mother's knowledge, the October omission, all of it in order, and send it before she could revise it into something smaller.

She opened a new message. She typed *Tayak, I need to tell you something.* She looked at it. She deleted it.

She thought about the profile wall. The stratigraphy Tayak had drawn by hand in the red notebook, every layer labeled, every transition marked, the whole two-thousand-year sequence visible in a single cross-section. Tayak had said once, early in the dig, that stratigraphy didn't lie — it could be misread, it could be damaged, but the layers themselves recorded exactly what had happened and in what order, and if you read them carefully enough you could reconstruct the truth of the site regardless of what anyone had said or written about it afterward.

The field notebook was open on the table. October 14. Cord-marked sherd. *mother knew.* The layers were already there. The record was already made.

She called.

It rang four times. She was composing a voicemail in her head — neutral, brief, *call me when you can* — when Tayak picked up.

"Wren."

Not a question. Not surprised. Just her name, the way Tayak said it when she was already paying attention.

Wren looked at the margin note.

"There's something I didn't tell you in October," she said. "When I found the patent. There's something I left out, and I need to tell you now, before it surfaces somewhere else."

The silence on the other end was the kind Wren had expected. Not empty. Occupied.

"Go ahead," Tayak said.

She told it in order. That was the only way she knew how.

The attorney first. Her mother's voice on the phone that afternoon, slightly wary the way she always was with unknown Maryland numbers, then the shift when the woman on the other end said *property attorney* and *Calvert land grant* and *chain of title*. Her mother had called Wren twenty minutes later. The firm was in Prince Frederick — Lester Pruitt, property and estate law, quiet title actions listed in the practice areas — and they were running a title search on a parcel in Charles County that traced back to a 1687 patent. They needed to locate any successors in interest to establish clear title. Her mother had taken the firm's number and said she would call back.

"She hasn't called back yet," Wren said. "But they'll try again. They always do when there's a quiet title question."

"Who initiated the search?" Tayak's voice was even.

"I don't know. But the timing—"

"Kimi."

"That's my read. She's been pulling colonial land records for weeks. If she found the patent and flagged the boundary description, someone would have started asking who holds the land now. That's a short chain from there to a title attorney."

Silence. Not the occupied kind this time. The kind where someone was running calculations.

"When did your mother call you?" Tayak said.

"Four-seventeen this afternoon."

"And you're calling me now."

"Yes."

"All right." A pause. "What's the rest?"

Wren had her hand flat on the open field notebook. The yellow light from the streetlamp was still coming through the window above the sink, cutting across the linoleum in a stripe that ended at the table leg. She looked at the margin note. *Mother knew.* Two words in her own handwriting, same pencil as the entry above it, same day.

"When I found the patent in October," she said, "I texted you that night and came to the site the next morning with the notebook. You remember that."

"I remember."

"What I didn't tell you was that I already knew my mother had seen the boundary description. Not recently. She found the patent copy in my grandmother's estate papers after my grandmother died. She read the boundary description — the one that references the Piscataway settlement at the high ground — and she knew what it meant. She knew when I took the field position with you." Wren kept her voice flat. She had decided, sometime in the last hour, that she would not soften this. "She didn't tell me. But when I called her in October, after I found the patent myself, she admitted it. She'd known the whole time."

She heard Tayak breathe.

"I wrote it in the margin of the field notebook that same day," Wren said. "October fourteenth. Right under the cord-marked sherd entry. *Mother knew.* Two words. I wrote it and I didn't tell you, and I've been on site for six weeks since then."

The silence this time was long enough that Wren looked at her phone screen to check the call was still connected. It was.

"Why?" Tayak said.

It was the question Wren had been rehearsing for six weeks and still didn't have a clean answer to. She had several true answers and none of them were good.

"Because I thought I could manage it," she said. "Because I'd already disclosed the patent and it felt like the hard part was done. Because I didn't want to—" She stopped. "Because I didn't want you to pull me from the project."

She said it plainly, the way she'd decided she would. No qualification. No softening. Just the thing itself.

Tayak didn't say anything.

"And now there's a property firm running the chain," Wren said, "and when they find what they're looking for they'll file something, and when they file it Slade's office will see it in the compliance review. They're already watching the site. A quiet title action on adjacent land—"

"Shows up in exactly the kind of search they run." Tayak's voice was still even. "Yes. I know."

"They'll see the boundary description. They'll see my name in the field log. They'll see the disclosure date in October. And if they look at the notebook—"

"They'll see the margin note."

"Yes."

Another silence. Wren heard something on Tayak's end — a chair, maybe, or a door. Then quiet.

"The firm," Tayak said. "Prince Frederick."

"Yes."

"Do you have a number?"

"My mother has the callback number. I'll get it from her tonight."

"Text it to me."

"All right."

"And the notebook," Tayak said. "The one with the margin note."

"It's in front of me."

"Don't alter it. Don't add to it. Don't remove any pages. Whatever's in it stays exactly as it is."

Wren looked at the entry. The cord-marked sherd. The two-word margin note below it in the same hand. "I know," she said.

"I need to think," Tayak said. "I'll call you back."

"Okay."

"Wren."

"Yes."

A beat. "I'm glad you called instead of texting."

Then the line went quiet.

Wren set the phone on the table face-up. The screen stayed lit for a moment, showing the call timer — four minutes and thirty-one seconds — then went dark. She looked at the field notebook. October 14. The cord-marked sherd entry ran three lines in her small, precise handwriting: depth, context, surface treatment, firing temperature estimated by color. Below it, in the same pencil, the two words she had written and not mentioned for six weeks.

She closed the notebook.

She didn't know if Tayak would call back tonight. She didn't know if Tayak would call back at all, or whether the next contact would be something formal — an email, a meeting request, a conversation with the field director about reassignment. She didn't know whether the Calvert connection would become evidence in the recognition case or a liability that had to be cut away, and she didn't know which outcome would be worse for Tayak, or for the site, or for the two-thousand-year record that was still coming out of the ground one careful layer at a time.

What she knew was that the layers were already there. The record was already made. And Tayak, who had taught her that stratigraphy didn't lie, would read it exactly as it was.

The refrigerator cycled on. The yellow stripe of streetlight lay across the linoleum. Outside, the temperature had dropped another degree and the cold-drain smell from the crawlspace was stronger now, the particular smell of Maryland in November — wet clay and old wood and something faintly mineral, like the ground itself breathing.

She left the notebook on the table and did not open it again.
