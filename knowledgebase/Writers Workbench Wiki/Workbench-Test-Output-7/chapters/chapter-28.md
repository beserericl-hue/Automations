The archive smelled of cold air and old paper and something faintly chemical, like the inside of a new binder left in a car. Wren had been here since eight. It was now past eleven, and she had not moved except to turn pages and type.

The reading room held four other researchers, each at a separate table under the long filtered windows. None of them had spoken. The only sounds were the HVAC cycling on and off, the soft roll of a book cart somewhere behind the reference desk, and the occasional muted click of someone's laptop keys. Wren's pencil lay across her notepad. She had stopped using it an hour ago.

On her screen: the Maryland State Archives digitized land records database, 1634–1820. Call number MDL-1687-CC-F-0041. The document she had been staring at for the last twenty minutes was a provincial patent dated the fourteenth day of April, 1687, issued in the name of Charles, Absolute Lord and Proprietary of the Provinces of Maryland and Avalon, Lord Baron of Baltimore.

She had found it first in chapter 10 — or rather, she had found a reference to it, a marginal notation in the 1691 Charles County court folio that Tayak had flagged: *Thomas Calvert, tract called Friendship, 320 acres, bounded east by the Piscataway settlement at the high ground.* She had written the call number in her notebook and told herself she would follow it when she had time. She had time now. Tayak was in meetings all week, the legal situation with Slade and Voss consuming everything, and Wren had been told to keep working the documentary record.

So she was working it.

The 1687 patent on her screen was written in a tight secretary hand, the long-s characters making the text look, at first glance, like a document in a foreign language. The archivist had shown her the transcription file alongside the scan, and Wren had them open in split screen: the original on the left, the transcription on the right, moving between them the way she moved between a field sketch and a photograph — checking one against the other, letting neither one be the only truth.

*To Thomas Calvert, a tract or parcel of land called Friendship, lying and being in Charles County, containing and now laid out for three hundred and twenty acres, more or less, beginning at a bounded white oak standing on the south side of a branch of the Mattawoman Creek, thence running north forty degrees east three hundred and twenty perches to a marked pine, thence south sixty degrees east to the eastern bounds of the Piscataway settlement at the high ground, thence south and west by said settlement to the first bounded tree.*

She had read that sentence four times. The high ground. The mound sat on a terrace above the Mattawoman Creek drainage. She knew this. She had stood on it. She had watched Tayak put a trowel into it.

She opened her notebook and wrote: *Calvert, Thomas. 'Friendship,' 320 ac, Charles Co., 1687. Bounded east by Piscataway settlement at the high ground. Mattawoman Creek branch. This is the site.*

She underlined *this is the site* and then looked at it and felt the particular unease of a researcher who has found something she was not looking for.

She kept going.

The 1712 document was already in her queue. She had pulled it two weeks ago when she first ran the chain-of-title search, but she had not yet read it carefully. Now she did.

*William Sorrell, tract called Mount Friendship, one hundred and twenty acres, being a part of the tract formerly called Friendship granted to Thomas Calvert in the year 1687, the northern boundary running adjacent to the Piscataway settlement at the high ground.* A parcel carved from the original grant, the boundary description copied almost word for word from the 1687 patent, the Piscataway settlement still there in the language twenty-five years later, still named, still adjacent.

Wren typed: *1712. Sorrell's Addition (120 ac) partitioned from Calvert's Friendship. Settlement still referenced in boundary desc. Piscataway still on the ground in 1712 — or at least in the legal record.*

She paused. In the legal record. That was the thing about boundary descriptions. They were conservative. They copied the language of the prior survey because the prior survey was the legal anchor. The Piscataway settlement might have been gone by 1712. The surveyor might have been copying a phrase from a twenty-five-year-old document because it was the easiest way to locate the line. Or the settlement might still have been there. The document could not tell her which.

She flagged the page and moved forward.

The 1741 deed of transfer was harder to read. The ink had foxed badly along the right margin, and the transcription file had three bracketed gaps where the archivist had been unable to recover the text. But the grantee's name was legible: *Anne Calvert, relict of Thomas Calvert the younger, to her son-in-law James Harrington, the tract called Friendship and its appurtenances, being the residue of the original grant of 1687 after the partition of 1712, containing two hundred acres more or less.*

The Calvert name again. Thomas Calvert the younger — a son, presumably, of the original Thomas. Anne Calvert, his widow, transferring the land through a daughter's marriage to a Harrington. The land moving sideways through a woman, as land so often did in this period, the woman herself invisible in the record except as a relict and a conduit.

Wren wrote: *1741. Friendship (200 ac residue) passes from Anne Calvert (widow of Thomas Jr.) to James Harrington by marriage. Calvert name exits the title.*

She sat back. The Calvert name exits the title. She had written that as a neutral observation, a chain-of-title note, the kind of thing she wrote twenty times a day when she was doing documentary research. She looked at it now and felt something shift slightly in the back of her mind, a small movement like a stone turning underfoot, not enough to fall but enough to notice.

Her own name was Calvert.

She had noted this in chapter 10, when she first found the 1687 Thomas Calvert grant. She had written *likely coincidental* in her margin and moved on. Maryland was full of Calverts. The proprietary family had seeded the colony with relatives and clients and hangers-on, and three and a half centuries of intermarriage had spread the name through every county in the state. Her family was from Anne Arundel County, not Charles. Her great-great-grandmother's estate was in —

She stopped.

She opened a new tab and typed her great-great-grandmother's address into the county GIS portal. She had done this before, years ago, when she was first getting interested in Maryland history, but she had not been looking for anything then. She had just been curious about the old property lines.

The map loaded. The parcel boundary appeared in red over the satellite image: a long rectangle running north to south, the house sitting near the northern end, the back fields stretching down toward a creek drainage. The parcel ID number in the sidebar linked to the ownership history.

She clicked through.

The current parcel traced back, through a series of 20th-century consolidations, to a 19th-century estate. The 19th-century estate traced back, through a partition recorded in 1798, to a larger holding. The larger holding was listed under the name of the original tract.

*Friendship.*

The HVAC cut off. The room went very quiet. Wren could hear the researcher two tables away breathing.

She looked at the screen for a long time. The county GIS overlay showed the modern parcel boundary in red. Beneath it, in a lighter dotted line, the 1798 partition boundary. She clicked the layer control and added the 1741 survey overlay, which the county had digitized from the Land Office plats. The dotted lines shifted and settled. The shapes were not identical — three centuries of resurvey and partition had moved some lines — but the northern boundary of the modern parcel still ran, within perhaps forty yards, along the same creek branch that the 1687 patent had used as its starting point.

The same branch of the Mattawoman Creek.

The same high ground to the east.

She picked up her pencil and then put it down again. She did not write anything. She looked at the split screen: the 1687 patent on the left, the GIS parcel map on the right. Thomas Calvert's tract. Her great-great-grandmother's estate. The mound that Tayak was excavating, which sat on the eastern boundary of that original grant, on the land described as *the Piscataway settlement at the high ground.*

The 1798 partition document was already in her queue. She had pulled it along with the others. She opened it now, though she already knew what she would find.

*Estate of James Harrington, deceased, to be divided among his heirs: to his eldest son William Harrington, the northern portion of the tract called Friendship, containing ninety acres, bounded north by the branch of the Mattawoman Creek and east by the high ground formerly described as the Piscataway settlement; to his daughter Mary Harrington, later Mary Calvert by marriage —*

She stopped reading.

*Mary Harrington, later Mary Calvert by marriage.*

The land had gone out under the Calvert name in 1741 and come back under the Calvert name in 1798, a different branch, a different Thomas, the name returning through a daughter who had married back into the family that had first held the ground. The Calverts had never really left.

Wren sat very still. Outside the long windows, a maintenance truck moved slowly across the parking lot, its amber light turning without urgency. She watched it until it went out of frame.

She thought about the mound. She thought about Tayak standing in the trench with the trowel, reading the soil the way Wren had watched her read it a dozen times — patiently, without assumption, letting the ground say what it was going to say. She thought about the 1691 petition, the one Saya had carried to the colonial assembly and pressed her hand into, the one that had ended up in the oilskin in the mound. *The high ground is sacred and holds the community's dead.* She thought about the partial name in the water-damaged folio: *Sioſ—*, the person who had filed against the Mount Friendship grant, arguing the ground was not available for patent because it already held the dead.

Filed against the Mount Friendship grant. Filed against Sorrell, yes. But Sorrell had gotten his 120 acres from Calvert. The original enclosure was Calvert's. The original displacement was Calvert's.

She opened her notepad to a clean page and wrote her own name at the top: *Wren Calvert.* She looked at it. Then she wrote beneath it: *Thomas Calvert, 1687, Friendship, 320 acres, bounded east by the Piscataway settlement at the high ground.*

She drew a line connecting the two names.

The chain-of-title search had taken her six weeks. She had done it carefully, the way Tayak had taught her — document by document, no assumptions, no gaps papered over, every transfer verified against the next. She had done everything right. She had found exactly what was there.

She closed her laptop.

The archive was still quiet. The researcher two tables away had packed up and left without Wren noticing. The reference desk was empty except for a staff member eating a sandwich behind a partition, the faint crinkle of the wrapper the only sound.

Wren sat with her hands flat on the table and looked at the closed laptop and thought about what she had just found and what she was going to have to do with it.

The 1798 partition document was on her screen. She had already read it once. She read it again.

*To his daughter Mary Harrington, later Mary Calvert by marriage, the southern portion of the tract called Friendship, containing one hundred and thirty acres, bounded south by the branch of the Mattawoman Creek, east by the high ground formerly described as the Piscataway settlement at the high ground, west by the road to Port Tobacco.*

She had the family genealogy file in a folder on her desktop. Her mother had sent it three years ago — a PDF compiled by a second cousin who had done the research as a retirement project, the kind of thing that arrived by email with a cheerful subject line and sat unread for months. Wren had skimmed it once and filed it. She opened it now.

The PDF was forty-seven pages. It had a table of contents. She went to the land records section, page thirty-one.

*Mary Harrington (1771–1849), married Thomas Calvert II of Charles County, 1793. Received by inheritance from the estate of James Harrington, 1798: the southern portion of the tract Friendship, 130 acres, Charles County, Maryland. The property descended through the Calvert line and was consolidated with the adjacent Calvert holdings in 1831 under the estate of Thomas Calvert II.*

She stopped. She looked at the GIS map, still open in the other tab. She clicked back to it. The modern parcel boundary in red. Beneath it, in the lighter dotted line, the 1798 partition. She zoomed in on the eastern edge.

The boundary ran along a contour line that the county GIS labeled *high ground, elev. 87 ft.* She knew that contour. She had walked it. She had set stakes along it in the third week of the survey, while Tayak called out measurements and Elspeth recorded them in the field notebook. The eastern boundary of the Calvert parcel ran within thirty yards of the trench.

She went back to the genealogy PDF. Page thirty-three.

*The Calvert estate in Charles County passed through three generations before being subdivided in 1912. The main house, rebuilt in brick circa 1870 on the foundations of the earlier structure, remained in family hands until 1987, when it was sold to —*

She stopped reading again.

Her great-great-grandmother's estate. The house her mother's family still talked about at Thanksgiving, the one with the long driveway and the fields going down to the water, the one her great-great-grandmother had left to two daughters in equal shares and which had been sold out of the family in 1987 because neither daughter could afford the upkeep. Wren had been to it once, when she was eleven, for some kind of family reunion. She remembered a brick house and a very long lawn and a smell of cut grass and mud from the creek at the bottom of the field.

She looked at the parcel ID number in the GIS sidebar. She looked at the parcel ID number in the genealogy PDF, which her cousin had transcribed from the county tax records.

They were the same number.

She sat back in her chair.

The HVAC came back on. The hum filled the room and then became background again, something she stopped hearing. The light through the long windows had shifted while she was working — it was lower now, coming in at an angle that caught the dust on the table's surface and made it visible, a fine pale layer she hadn't noticed before.

She looked at her notepad. Her own name at the top. Thomas Calvert, 1687. The line she had drawn connecting them.

She thought about the 1687 grant, the formulaic legal English on the vellum, the phrase *bounded east by the Piscataway settlement at the high ground* — the settlement named as a boundary marker, the way you would name a tree or a creek, the way you named something that was there but that you were not accounting for, that you were simply using as a reference point for the thing you were taking.

She thought about Saya. About the petition Saya had carried to the colonial assembly, the one Wren had read in the conservation suite under the indirect filtered light while Adaeze worked the humidification chamber. *We the undersigned, being the rightful possessors of the high ground at the Mattawoman branch, do humbly beseech.* Saya had pressed her hand into the ink and left a print at the bottom of the page. The print was still there, three hundred and twenty-seven years later, a little faded, the lines of the palm still legible.

The displacement Saya had petitioned against had been Thomas Calvert's displacement. The grant Sioſ— had filed against in 1691 had been built on Thomas Calvert's 1687 enclosure. Sorrell's 120 acres had come out of the Calvert holding, granted four years after the original patent, a subdivision of the same act.

The mound sat on the eastern boundary of the land her family had held for over two centuries.

She had excavated it. She had laid out the grid, set the datum stake, photographed the stratigraphy, bagged the artifacts. She had been the one to call out the measurements while Tayak troweled. She had held the evidence bags while Tayak lowered the oilskin packet into them, careful, both hands, the way you carry something that cannot be replaced.

She had done all of this on land her family had taken from the people whose ancestors she was documenting.

She did not move for a long time.

A staff member came out from behind the reference desk partition, carrying the sandwich wrapper to a bin in the corner. The paper made a soft sound going in. The staff member went back behind the partition. The room was quiet again.

Wren looked at her hands on the table. They were clean — she had washed the field grit off that morning before coming to the archive. There was still a faint callus on her right index finger from the trowel handle, a ridge of hardened skin she had stopped noticing weeks ago. She noticed it now.

She thought about what Tayak had taught her in the first week of the survey, before they had found anything, when they were still doing the pedestrian walkover and the soil auger tests. *Documentation is not interpretation,* Tayak had said. *You write what you see. You write it accurately. You don't decide what it means until you have enough to decide.* Wren had written it in her field notebook and underlined it.

She had enough to decide.

She opened her laptop. She pulled up the split screen again — the 1687 patent on the left, the GIS parcel map on the right — and took a photograph of it with her phone, the screen reflected faintly in her glasses. Then she photographed the relevant page of the genealogy PDF. Then the 1798 partition document, the section with Mary Harrington's name and the acreage and the boundary description.

She looked at the photographs on her phone screen. Three images. The chain of title, complete.

She thought about calling her mother. She had her mother's number in her phone under *Mom*, the way everyone did, the label so ordinary it told you nothing about the person. Her mother, who had sent the genealogy PDF with a cheerful email, who talked about the old estate at Thanksgiving with a particular kind of pride — *your great-great-grandmother's land, the family has been in Charles County since before the Revolution* — who had no idea, or who had some idea and had never said, or who had known exactly and had never said, which were three very different things.

She put the phone face-down on the table.

She looked at the 1687 patent. The iron-gall ink had browned at the edges of each letter, the way it did when the acid in the ink slowly ate the paper around it — a slow destruction built into the act of writing, into the very medium of the record. The clerk who had written this had been dead for three hundred years. Thomas Calvert had been dead for three hundred years. The Piscataway settlement at the high ground had been displaced, its people scattered, and the ground itself had been plowed and planted and built on and sold and inherited and sold again, the boundary lines shifting and settling and shifting again, until a woman named Mary Harrington married a man named Thomas Calvert and the name came back to the land it had first taken.

And then, two hundred and twenty-six years after that, a woman named Wren Calvert had come to the eastern boundary of that same ground with a trowel and a field notebook and had begun to dig.

She picked up her pencil. She wrote in her notepad, beneath the line connecting her name to Thomas Calvert's:

*Tell Tayak.*

She looked at what she had written. Two words. Obvious. Necessary. The only thing that was clear right now.

She gathered her papers — the printed patent images, the GIS overlays, the partition document — and stacked them in order, the way Tayak had taught her to organize evidence: chronological, no gaps, every transfer verified against the next. She slid them into her folder. She closed her notepad. She put her pencil in her bag.

She sat for another moment with her hands flat on the table, the dust visible in the low-angled light, the HVAC humming, the archive around her very still.

Then she picked up her phone and called her mother.

It rang twice. Her mother answered on the third ring, her voice warm and immediate, the way it always was — *Wren, hi, I was just thinking about you* — and Wren said, *Mom, I need to ask you something about the genealogy file you sent me.* There was a brief pause, just a beat, the kind of pause that was not quite surprise. And then her mother said: *Which part?*

Wren said: *The Charles County land. The estate. How far back did you know it went?*

The pause this time was longer.

*I know,* her mother said.

Wren looked at the folder on the table in front of her. The stack of documents inside it, chronological, no gaps.

*How long?* Wren said.

Her mother's voice was careful now, the warmth still there but underneath it something else, something that had been waiting. *Your grandmother found the original patent copy in 1987, when she was settling the property taxes. There was a title search. The attorney flagged the boundary description. She read it and set it aside.* A pause. *She didn't know what to do with it. So she set it aside.*

*And you.*

*I found her copy in the estate papers after she died. I read it.* Another pause, shorter. *I didn't know what to do with it either.*

Wren said nothing.

*Wren.* Her mother's voice was careful now. *It was a long time ago. The family didn't —*

*Mom,* Wren said.

Her mother stopped.

*I'm sitting in the archive,* Wren said. *I have the 1687 patent in front of me. I have the 1798 partition. I have the GIS overlay. The boundary of our family's land runs thirty yards from the mound I've been excavating for the past two months.* She stopped. She looked at the folder. *You knew when I took the position.*

A silence.

*Yes,* her mother said. Very quiet.

*And you didn't tell me.*

Her mother didn't answer. Outside the long windows, a cloud had moved across the sun, and the angle of light through the glass shifted, the dust on the table disappearing, the surface going flat and anonymous again.

*I have to go,* Wren said. *I have to call Tayak.*

She ended the call.

She did not move for twenty minutes.

She sat with her hands flat on the table and the phone face-down beside the folder and the dust settling back into visibility as the light shifted again through the long windows. The HVAC cycled. The reference desk was still empty. The room was very quiet. She did not pick up the phone. She did not open her laptop. She looked at the folder and the notepad and the two words she had written — *Tell Tayak* — and she sat with them the way you sat with something you had found that could not be unfound, waiting for the shape of it to become clear.

It did not become clearer. It stayed exactly as clear as it had been when she wrote the words, which was clear enough.

She gathered her papers, slid them into her bag, and stood up from the table. She returned the call slip at the desk, collected her bag from the locker, and walked out into the corridor.

The corridor was ordinary. Linoleum floor, fluorescent lights, a bulletin board with departmental notices pinned at irregular angles. A student passed her with headphones on, not looking up. Everything was ordinary.

She found an empty bench near the building's east exit and sat down and took out her phone and found Tayak's number and pressed call.

It rang four times and went to voicemail.

Wren lowered the phone. She looked out through the glass doors at the parking lot, where the afternoon light was coming in low and flat across the asphalt, the kind of light that made everything look both very clear and very far away. She did not leave a message. There was nothing she could say in a voicemail that would be the right shape of the thing. She needed to say it in person, with the folder in her hands and the documents in order, the way Tayak had taught her to present evidence — chronological, no gaps, every claim supported by what came before it.

She put the phone away and sat for a moment longer on the bench, her bag in her lap, the folder inside it.

Then she pushed through the east exit into the cold air.

Her truck was in the second row. She walked to it, unlocked it, put her bag on the passenger seat. She sat in the driver's seat without starting the engine and took out her phone and found Tayak's number again.

She typed instead of calling.

*I found something in the land records. It's about me. I need to tell you in person.*

She read it back. It was not enough and it was exactly enough. It said what needed to be said and it did not say more than she could stand behind right now, in this truck, in this parking lot, with the folder on the seat beside her and the weight of the chain she had just assembled sitting at the bottom of her bag.

She sent it.

She put the phone face-down in the cupholder and waited.

The afternoon light shifted. A crow crossed the parking lot low and fast and disappeared behind the building's roofline. The engine ticked in the cold.

Her phone buzzed once against the plastic.

She picked it up.

Tayak had written back four words: *Where are you right now?*
