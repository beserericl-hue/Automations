The petition copy had fold lines pressed into it from two hours inside Kimi's jacket pocket, and she smoothed them against her thigh now, standing at the chain-link gate with the mound rising behind the orange snow fencing thirty meters back. The paper was a laser-print from the state archive portal — the original iron-gall ink rendered in washed-out gray, the secretary-hand script swimming with long *s*'s that looked like *f*'s, the reference code stamped at the top in small sans-serif type: *MS 1234, Colonial Petitions, Box 7, Folder 2*. She had printed it at the Silver Spring library at seven this morning, standing at the coin machine in her coat.

She read the opening line again, moving her lips.

*The humble petition of the Piſcataway people ſheweth — that the ſd. high ground lying adjacent to Mattawoman Creek hath been held by ſd. petitioners as ancient poſſeſſors thereof—*

Ancient possessors. Judge Notley's phrase, carried forward into the petition as though the petitioners had known it would outlast him. Kimi had looked Notley up at two in the morning and found one footnote in a 1978 Maryland law review article. One footnote in two hundred and forty years.

She folded the paper back along its crease and looked toward the site.

Tayak was already there.

She stood ten feet inside the gate, arms crossed, facing the mound. She wore her field jacket — the faded green one with the chest pocket — and her hair was pulled back tight. She had not turned when Kimi came through the gate. She might have been there an hour. She might have been there since dawn.

Kimi walked toward her and stopped a few feet away. The ground between them was patchy grass and one backfilled shovel-test depression that had collected overnight rain, a dark oval in the dirt.

"You got my message," Kimi said.

"I got your message."

"And you came."

Tayak said nothing to that. She was looking at the mound, which was maybe four feet of rise above the surrounding ground, covered in sweetgum and Virginia pine, the orange fencing running around its base between T-posts. A metal sign on the nearest post read *PROTECTED ARCHAEOLOGICAL SITE — NO DIGGING OR ARTIFACT REMOVAL — STATE LAW §14-502*. Somebody had zip-tied the sign crooked and it listed to the left.

"I told you not yet," Tayak said.

"You told me not yet three weeks ago. The state has the petition. Slade has been watching the site since spring. Voss put your coordinates in an NSF grant." Kimi kept her voice level. "Not yet was running out."

"That's my call to make."

"You weren't making it."

Tayak turned then. She had dark circles under her eyes and a look that Kimi recognized from childhood — the look that meant *I know you're right and I'm not ready to say so* — but what she said was: "If this goes wrong today, it doesn't just hurt you."

"I know that."

"Do you?" Tayak's voice was quiet. "Because there are three cameras coming and a reporter from the Post and the council hasn't voted on public statements and Slade is going to watch every frame of this."

"Slade is already watching." Kimi held up the petition copy. "This is a public document. Anyone can pull it. I'm just the first one reading it out loud."

The wind came off the creek and moved the fencing and the *PROTECTED SITE* sign swung on its zip-tie. Somewhere in the sweetgum above the mound, a red-winged blackbird called twice and went quiet.

Tayak looked at the paper in Kimi's hand. Her jaw was set.

"What does it say?" she asked. Not as if she didn't know. As if she needed to hear it said.

Kimi smoothed the fold line again. "It says the Piscataway people are the ancient possessors of the high ground adjacent to Mattawoman Creek. It says a man named Thomas Calvert received a land patent in 1687 that used the same high ground as a boundary marker — which means the colonial government knew the settlement was there when they drew the grant. The petition is 1697. The grant is 1687. Ten years apart. Both in the state archive. Both public record." She paused. "The Calvert family knew the mound was there. They used it as a survey point."

Tayak was quiet for a moment. A van turned off the county road and came slowly down the gravel track toward the gate — dark blue, white lettering, a telescoping mast folded down along the roof. *NewsChannel 8.*

"Don't overclaim," Tayak said. "The petition establishes presence. It doesn't establish title."

"I know the difference."

"Say it like you know the difference."

"I will."

Another car behind the van. Then a second van, white, with a *7* in a red circle on the door.

Kimi tucked the petition copy back inside her jacket. Her hands were steady, which surprised her — she had expected them to shake. The paper was warm from her body and the fold lines had softened slightly. She thought of Nora at the kitchen table on Billingsley Road, hands flat on the flour-sack cloth, tracing names that went back two thousand years. She thought of a woman named Saya standing in a colonial assembly with ink drying on her palm.

She thought: *someone has to read it out loud.*

"I'm not going to stop you," Tayak said. She had turned back toward the mound. "But I'm not going to stand next to the podium."

"You can stand wherever you want."

"I'll be behind you."

Kimi nodded. The first camera operator was already out of the van, pulling a tripod from the back, the spiked feet ringing against the metal frame. A woman in a navy blazer climbed out after him, checking her phone, a laminated press badge on a lanyard swinging at her chest. Channel 7. Janelle Marsh.

"Tayak," Kimi said.

Tayak looked at her.

"Thank you for coming."

Tayak didn't answer. She looked at the mound for another moment — at the sweetgum and the pine and the orange fencing and the crooked sign — and then she walked toward the gate to let the cameras through, and Kimi watched her go and understood that this was the only permission she was going to get, and that it was enough.

The portable podium was already set up, black plastic, scuffed at the base where someone had kicked it loading out of a car. Three foam mic flags — *4*, *7*, *NewsChannel 8* — had been taped across the top by a volunteer who'd arrived at nine. The mound rose behind it, framed perfectly in every lens. The air smelled of creek water and cut grass and something faintly resinous from the pines, and the humidity was already building toward the kind of late-spring morning that would be genuinely hot by noon.

Kimi pulled the petition copy from her jacket one more time and pressed it flat against her palm.

She had twenty minutes.

She used them to read the document again, all the way through, every long *s* and every archaic spelling and every careful, formal phrase — *humbly beſeeching* — until the words were in her mouth the way her grandmother's stories were in her mouth, worn smooth from handling, and she could say them without looking down.

The NewsChannel 8 operator planted his tripod in the trampled grass twelve feet from the podium, the spiked feet punching into the soft ground with three sharp sounds. He swung the camera up onto the mount without looking at Kimi. The Channel 7 crew set up to his left. A third camera — smaller, a freelancer's rig with a LiveU backpack unit sitting on the ground beside it, status lights blinking green — anchored the right flank. Eleven reporters had spread into a loose semicircle. A few held spiral notepads. Most held phones, screen-up, recording. The radio woman from WAMU had a handheld recorder she'd set on the podium lip without asking, its red light already on.

Kimi looked at the foam mic flags taped across the podium top. *4. 7. NewsChannel 8.* The WAMU recorder. Someone's phone propped against the podium shelf, screen cracked at one corner.

She looked back once.

Tayak was standing six feet behind her, slightly to the left, between the podium and the orange perimeter fencing. Arms crossed. She had not moved since the cameras arrived. Her field jacket was open over a gray shirt and her boots were still muddy from the trench. She was not looking at the cameras. She was looking at the mound.

The Channel 4 reporter — a woman in a bright blue blazer, laminated badge on a lanyard, hair pinned back against the humidity — raised her hand slightly. "Whenever you're ready."

Kimi set the petition copy on the podium shelf.

"My name is Kimi Moyaone," she said. "I'm Piscataway. I run a public history channel called Piscataway Rising. I'm also the granddaughter of Nora Moyaone, who is eighty-two years old and has lived on Billingsley Road in Charles County her entire life." She paused. "I'm not here as a spokesperson for the tribal council. I'm here as a member of this community, reading from a public document that anyone in this room could have pulled from the state archives. I just pulled it first."

The Channel 4 reporter wrote something. The freelancer adjusted his frame slightly, the zoom motor whining once.

Kimi picked up the petition copy. The paper was letter-size, laser-printed on archive-quality stock, the original's iron-gall script reproduced in washed-out gray. The fold lines from three hundred and twenty-seven years showed faintly in the reproduction, ghost creases running through the text. At the top, in small type: *MS 1234, Colonial Petitions, Box 7, Folder 2. Maryland State Archives. Public Record.*

"This is a copy of a petition filed in 1697," she said. "The original is in the custody of the Maryland State Archives. It was entered into the colonial court record by a clerk named Thomas Ware during the winter session of that year. The petition was filed on behalf of the Piscataway people of Charles County, and it states" — she found the line, the long *s* rendered as a clean *s* in the modernized transcript she'd typed out and taped to the back of the archival copy — "that the petitioners are the *ancient possessors of the high ground adjacent to Mattawoman Creek*, and that this ground holds their dead."

She let that sit for a moment. The wind came off the creek and buffeted one of the mics. Someone's notepad rustled.

"Ten years before that petition was filed," Kimi said, "in 1687, a man named Thomas Calvert received a colonial land patent from the Lords Baltimore. The patent is also in the state archives. Also a public record." She turned the page. "The boundary description of the Calvert grant uses the Piscataway settlement at the high ground as a survey marker. I'm going to read that language directly." She found it. "*Beginning at a marked white oak standing on the east bank of the first branch of Mattawoman Creek, thence running north by east four hundred perches to the high ground known as the place of the Piscataway burying, thence east two hundred perches—*"

She stopped reading. She looked up.

"The colonial surveyor knew the mound was there," she said. "He used it as a landmark. That means the colonial government, when it issued the Calvert grant, knew this ground was a Piscataway burial site. They drew the patent boundary around it. And then ten years later, when the Piscataway people filed a petition saying *this is ours, our dead are here*, that petition was entered into the court record — and then it disappeared. For three hundred and twenty-seven years."

The Channel 7 reporter raised her hand. "Ms. Moyaone—"

"I'm not taking questions yet."

The reporter lowered her hand. Kimi heard a camera shutter, rapid, from somewhere to her right — one of the wire-service photographers, working a telephoto. She did not look toward the sound.

She did not look at Tayak either. But she was aware of her. The way you're aware of a fixed point when you're trying to keep your balance.

"The site behind me is Site 18CH-447," Kimi said. "It is currently under excavation by Dr. Tayak Moyaone, a Piscataway archaeologist and faculty member at the University of Maryland. The excavation is operating under a state-issued research permit. In the past several months, artifacts have been recovered from multiple strata of the mound — artifacts consistent with Piscataway occupation of this site spanning more than two thousand years." She paused. "Two thousand years. The Calvert grant is three hundred and thirty-seven years old. The petition is three hundred and twenty-seven years old. The mound is older than both of them by a factor of six."

She set the petition copy down.

"I'm also going to name something else," she said. "There is currently a quiet title inquiry underway on adjacent land, initiated through a law firm with ties to Chesapeake Heritage Development LLC. That inquiry references the Calvert land grant. The same grant I just read from. It is our position that any title action on land whose colonial boundary description references this mound as a Piscataway burial site cannot proceed without full Section 106 consultation — and that the state's obligation to this community under NAGPRA does not pause while a private developer examines its options."

The Channel 4 reporter stopped writing. The Channel 7 reporter had her phone up, reading something on the screen. The WAMU recorder's red light held steady.

"That's the statement," Kimi said. "I'll take questions now."

They came in fast.

"Is Dr. Moyaone planning to make a statement?"

Kimi glanced back. Tayak had not moved. She was still looking at the mound, arms still crossed, the mud still on her boots. She did not look at the cameras. She did not look at Kimi.

"Dr. Moyaone is here," Kimi said. "Her presence speaks for itself."

The wire-service photographer was working the telephoto again, the shutter clicking in short bursts. Kimi knew what he was framing: Kimi at the mic, and behind her, slightly left of center, the figure in the field jacket standing in front of the orange fencing and the sweetgum trees and the mound, face turned slightly away, arms crossed, completely still. The mound rising behind her like a held breath.

"Has the tribal council authorized this statement?"

"I'm speaking as a community member and a researcher. The council speaks for itself."

"Is the Calvert family aware of the petition's contents?"

"The petition has been in the public record since 1697."

"What's the current status of the excavation permit?"

"That's a question for the Maryland Historical Trust."

"Has Garrett Slade's office been notified of the quiet title inquiry?"

Kimi paused. She had not expected that question. The reporter who'd asked it — young, print outlet, paper badge in a plastic sleeve — was looking at his phone, not at her, reading from notes.

"I'd refer you to the Trust," she said.

"Does the tribe intend to file for federal recognition on the basis of this evidence?"

"The tribe's recognition process is ongoing. This evidence is part of a much longer record."

"Ms. Moyaone, you mentioned the petition disappeared for three hundred years. How did it resurface?"

Kimi looked at the reporter. She thought of Wren Calvert in the field, two months on site, nitrile gloves, careful handwriting in the evidence log. She thought of what Wren had told Tayak and what Tayak had told her, and what none of them had said publicly yet.

"Careful research," she said. "The kind that takes time."

The Channel 8 reporter leaned forward. "One more. You said the mound is over two thousand years old. Has the excavation found human remains?"

The wind moved the *PROTECTED SITE* sign on its zip-tie. The sweetgum shifted above the mound. Somewhere on the county road, a truck downshifted.

"That's a question for Dr. Moyaone," Kimi said.

Every camera in the semicircle swung left.

Tayak was still standing where she'd been standing since the cameras arrived. She had heard the question. Kimi could see it in the slight set of her jaw, the way her weight had shifted almost imperceptibly onto her back foot. The photographers were on her now, the telephoto shutter going in short bursts, capturing the stillness of her face, the crossed arms, the muddy boots, the mound at her back.

She did not step forward.

She did not step away.

She looked at the cameras for a long moment — not performing, not posing, just looking, the way you look at something you've decided about — and then she looked back at the mound.

The shutter clicked.

Kimi watched a Channel 4 producer, standing behind the camera operator, mouth something into her phone. She watched Janelle Marsh pull up a contact on her screen and step away from the group, already making a call.

The reporter with the paper badge was writing quickly in his notepad. He had circled something at the top of the page. Kimi couldn't read it from here, but she had a feeling she knew what it said.

*Slade.*

She gathered the petition copy from the podium. The paper was warm again, warmer than before, from the sun now and from her hands. She folded it once along the original crease.

The press conference was over. The thing it had set in motion was not.

Kimi kept the folded petition in her hand as the cameras began to break down. She did not look at her phone. She already knew what it was doing.

Garrett Slade had the livestream open on his desktop, the browser window pulled wide so the mound was visible in the background of the frame. He had been watching since the first minute. His coffee was cold.

When Kimi read the petition aloud — *the high ground known to the Piscataway as the place of their dead, held in continuous use from time beyond the memory of any living person* — he had leaned forward without meaning to. His elbows came down on the desk. His hands came together.

It was not the language that stopped him. He knew the language. He had read the petition in the state archives three weeks ago, the laser-printed copy with the archives' reference code at the top and the faint gray impression of the fold lines showing through the page like a watermark. He had noted the secretary hand, the long *s* that looked like an *f*, the formulaic opening. He had written *see Calvert chain* in the margin of his compliance file and moved on.

What stopped him was the phrase *Calvert land grant*, spoken aloud, on camera, with the Channel 4 mic flag clearly visible on the podium.

He sat back. He looked at the window. Outside, the parking lot of the Trust building held three cars and a service van, and beyond the lot the tree line was full of new green, the kind of green that looks almost yellow in May, and none of it was relevant to the problem that had just materialized on his screen.

The Calvert chain was not a secret. It had never been a secret — that was the whole architecture of a public-record land grant, the whole point of the colonial patent system, the reason Thomas Calvert's 1687 boundary description still existed in a bound volume in a climate-controlled vault in Annapolis. Public. Accessible. Any member of the public could request a copy.

But there was a difference between a document existing in an archive and a document being read aloud into three television cameras with a two-thousand-year-old burial mound in the background.

His compliance review had a quiet title inquiry in it. The inquiry referenced the Calvert grant. The grant's boundary description referenced the Piscataway settlement at the high ground. The high ground was the mound. The mound was Site 18CH-447. Site 18CH-447 was under his Section 106 review.

The chain was not complicated. It had just become impossible to contain quietly.

He reached for his phone. He held it. He set it back down.

He was not going to call Tayak Moyaone. Not yet. Not while the livestream was still running and Janelle Marsh was already stepping away from the group with her phone at her ear. Whatever he said to Tayak in the next hour would be said in the context of whatever she was about to be asked by that reporter, and he did not yet know what he was going to say.

The inquiry had come through Pruitt's firm — Holloway & Breck, Prince Frederick, Calvert County, twenty-two years in practice, former counsel to the State Highway Administration. The client was listed as undisclosed. The inquiry had asked about successors in interest to the 1687 Calvert patent.

On his screen, Kimi Moyaone was taking questions. *Has Garrett Slade's office been notified of the quiet title inquiry?*

He went very still.

She had paused before answering. She had referred the reporter to the Trust. Which meant she knew his name. Which meant someone had given it to her, or she had found it, and either way she had chosen not to use it directly — yet.

He wrote *Pruitt / CHD connection — public record as of today* on the legal pad beside his keyboard. He underlined it. He looked at what he had written.

The compliance review had a thirty-day clock. It had started running the morning he signed the suspension notice with his blue Pilot pen. Twenty-two days remained.

On the screen, every camera swung left toward Tayak Moyaone. She was standing in front of the orange fencing with her arms crossed and the sweetgum trees behind her and the mound behind the trees, and she was not moving, and the telephoto shutters were going, and Slade watched her look at the cameras for a long moment — not performing, just looking — and then look back at the mound.

He reached for his phone again. He held it longer this time.

He put it down.

Elliot Voss was in the parking garage on Level 2 when the livestream loaded. He had pulled into the space, killed the engine, and stayed there because the faculty senate building's signal was better in the garage than in his office, which was a fact he had never understood and had stopped trying to. The phone's screen threw a pale rectangle on the steering wheel.

He had found the stream through a link in a departmental email — someone in the Anthropology admin had forwarded it with no comment, which was itself a comment. He had watched it from the moment Kimi Moyaone stepped to the podium.

He knew who she was. He had known since October, when her channel had broken forty thousand followers and the first inquiry from the *Post* had come to the department's communications office. He had noted her then the way you note a weather system moving in a direction that may or may not affect you.

He watched her read the petition. He watched the cameras. He watched the reporters write.

When she said *Chesapeake Heritage Development LLC*, his jaw tightened.

He had heard that name before. He had heard it in a conversation he had not initiated, at a symposium in Annapolis fourteen months ago, from a compact man with gray at the temples who had used the phrase *heritage-tourism infrastructure* with the fluency of someone who had said it many times in many rooms. Voss had taken a card. He had not called. He had decided it was not his problem.

It was now on a livestream with three television cameras and a two-thousand-year-old burial mound.

The political calculus had been simple, three months ago. The site was significant. Tayak Moyaone was junior faculty with a contested permit and an institutional complaint against a senior colleague. The NSF proposal had been an error — his research coordinator had pulled the wrong file, the attribution had been dropped, it was administrative, the panel had found as much — but the error had created a situation, and situations required management. Getting ahead of the site's institutional profile, positioning himself as a collegial resource rather than an obstacle, had seemed like the correct move.

He had not anticipated a press conference. He had not anticipated the Calvert grant going public in this format. He had not anticipated that the site would become politically radioactive in a direction that pointed not toward Tayak Moyaone's overreach but toward a private developer's quiet title inquiry and a compliance review with twenty-two days left on its clock.

The NSF proposal had his name on it as sole PI. That was in the public record now too, or close enough — the complaint had been filed, the hearing had happened, and Kimi Moyaone had a channel with eighty thousand followers and a demonstrated ability to find documents in archives.

He opened his contacts. He found the number he wanted. He held the phone.

He did not call.

He needed to know if the quiet title inquiry was going to move forward or collapse. He needed to know whether the *Post* was going to run something tonight or sit on it.

He needed, most of all, to know what Tayak Moyaone was going to do next.

Voss watched the telephoto frame tighten on her face. The image was slightly backlit — the afternoon sun catching the sweetgum leaves behind the mound — and her face was half in shadow, and she was completely still, and the mound rose behind her like something that had been waiting a long time to be seen.

He turned the phone over in his hand.

He put it in his pocket.

He sat in the parking garage for another three minutes, and then he started the car.

Janelle Marsh stepped forward from the cluster of journalists the way someone steps through a door they've been waiting in front of. She had a spiral notepad in one hand and her phone in the other, recording.

"Dr. Moyaone." Not Kimi. The other one. "Do you endorse what Ms. Moyaone has just read?"

Kimi turned.

She had been watching Tayak for the last four minutes the way you watch a person you think you know and are suddenly not sure about. Tayak had not moved from her position in front of the orange snow fencing. Her arms were still crossed. The sweetgum trees stood behind her, and the mound rose behind the trees in its low, patient way, and the afternoon light was coming in at a flat angle that caught the leaves and left Tayak's face half in shadow. She had not spoken. She had not nodded. She had not done anything that could be quoted or captioned or clipped to thirty seconds of footage, and Kimi had spent the last four minutes interpreting this as disapproval — as Tayak standing there in controlled silence the way she sometimes stood in the field when Kimi had done something she considered premature.

Now the cameras swung left.

All three of them. The Sony on the tripod with the spiked feet dug into the soft ground near the fencing, the shoulder-mount the Channel 4 operator had been carrying since the opening, the smaller unit the digital crew had set up on a monopod at the back. They swung left and found Tayak, and the telephoto on the Channel 7 tripod tightened, and Kimi watched the frame close on her cousin's face.

One second. Two. A gnat moved through the humid air between the podium and the fencing, and somewhere on Route 301 a truck downshifted, and the sound came across the field in a long, descending note. Three seconds. Marsh did not repeat the question. She had the instinct to hold. Four seconds.

"The document speaks for itself."

That was all. No inflection up or down. No step forward. No gesture toward the petition copy Kimi was still holding, folded once, the laser-printed reproduction with the archives reference code at the top and the washed-out gray image of the old secretary hand beneath it, the long esses that looked like effs, the phrase *humbly sheweth* visible even in reproduction. The document speaks for itself. Kimi felt something shift in her chest that she did not immediately have a name for.

Marsh wrote something on her notepad. Then: "Is the state trying to seize this site?"

The cameras stayed on Tayak.

Tayak said nothing.

Not *no comment.* Not *I can't speak to that.* Not the small deflecting phrase that signals a person who has something to say but has been told not to say it. She said nothing the way the mound said nothing — present, solid, unmoved by the question. Her eyes went briefly to the mound itself, that low rise of compacted yellowish clay and Virginia pine roots behind the orange fencing, and then came back to the reporter, and she held Marsh's gaze without expression for long enough that the telephoto shutter fired six times in the space of two seconds. Kimi could hear it from where she stood — the rapid mechanical tick of the lens capturing Tayak's face, the mound just visible over her left shoulder, the sweetgum leaves catching the light behind her.

Marsh waited. The cameras waited. The question hung in the humid air and did not get an answer, and then the moment was over, and two other reporters moved forward with their own questions, and the press conference began its natural dissolution.

Kimi stood at the podium with the petition copy in her hands and understood something she had not understood before.

She had spent three weeks building this. The research, the posts, the eighty thousand people who had watched her pull colonial deed images and highlight boundary language at her kitchen table in Silver Spring at eleven o'clock at night. She had found the Calvert grant. She had found the 1697 petition in the state archives — a public document, accessible to any member of the public who submitted the request form, available as a high-resolution scan from the colonial court records series, no different from any other public record except that no one had looked at it in the right way before. She had printed the transcript herself, with the modernized spelling and the archives reference code at the top, and she had stood at this podium and read it aloud to three television cameras and a dozen reporters and said the name Chesapeake Heritage Development LLC in a context that would be on the evening news.

She had thought she was doing it despite Tayak. She had told herself that Tayak's *not yet* was caution becoming paralysis, that someone had to act, that the thirty-day compliance clock did not stop for institutional process. She had organized this without permission and told Tayak after the fact and stood at the podium waiting for the moment when Tayak would step forward and pull the microphone away or step back far enough to signal public distance.

Tayak had done neither.

She had stood in front of the orange fencing with her arms crossed and said four words when asked a direct question and said nothing when asked the one that mattered, and Kimi was only now understanding what that meant.

The reporters had their footage. They had Kimi reading the petition. They had the Calvert grant named on camera. They had the 1697 document in Kimi's hands with the mound visible behind her. And they had Tayak Moyaone — Dr. Tayak Moyaone, principal investigator, junior faculty, the person whose name was on the excavation permit — standing at the site in complete silence, arms crossed, not endorsing and not denying, her face half in shadow and the mound rising behind her like a fact that had been waiting two thousand years to be stated.

The silence was not disapproval.

It was a frame. Kimi had built the frame without knowing she was building it, and Tayak had stepped into it and held still, and now every photograph taken in the last four minutes had both of them in it — the speaker and the silent one, the petition and the mound, the words and the thing the words were about — and none of it had required Tayak to say anything that could be quoted in a compliance review or used in a hearing or held against her in the thirty days remaining on Slade's clock.

Kimi had handed her a tool. Tayak had picked it up.

The Channel 4 reporter was asking about NAGPRA. The Channel 7 crew was repositioning toward the mound for a cutaway shot. The digital journalist was already on his phone, filing something. Kimi folded the petition copy a second time and held it against her chest and looked at Tayak, who had uncrossed her arms now and was watching the cameras pack up with the same expression she wore in the field when she was reading the ground — not performing attention, just paying it.

Tayak did not look back at Kimi.

She did not need to. The four seconds of silence had already said everything, and Kimi was still catching up to what it meant — not just for today, not just for the cameras, but for what came next, for the thirty days, for whatever Slade's office was going to do when the evening news ran tonight and the *Post* filed its story and the name Chesapeake Heritage Development LLC appeared in print next to a two-thousand-year-old burial mound and a 1697 petition that was a public document and had always been a public document and had been sitting in the state archives waiting for someone to look at it in the right way.

The question Kimi had not yet asked herself was whether Tayak had planned this from the beginning — had said *not yet* knowing Kimi would do it anyway, had shown up knowing what the cameras would do with her silence, had calculated the frame before Kimi had even printed the petition copy.

She looked at her cousin's still face and the mound behind it and could not tell.

---

The Channel 7 crew was the last to leave. Kimi watched the photographer collapse his tripod — three quick twists, the legs folding in — and carry it back to the news van parked on the gravel shoulder of the access road. The van's microwave mast was already retracted. The coiled XLR cable went into a black bag. Then the door slid shut, and the van pulled away, and the sound of its engine faded into the background noise of Route 301 a quarter mile east, and the site was quiet except for the wind moving through the Virginia pines at the mound's edge and the distant percussion of a woodpecker somewhere in the treeline.

Tayak was at her elbow before Kimi heard her coming.

"Walk with me."

Not a question. Kimi fell into step beside her, the folded petition copy still in her hand, the paper soft now from handling. They moved away from the podium — a borrowed plastic lectern, dark wood-grain finish, already being loaded into Kimi's car by the two community volunteers who had helped set up — and toward the orange fencing at the site's perimeter, where the T-posts threw thin shadows across the trampled grass.

Tayak stopped at the fence line. She did not look at Kimi. She looked at the mound.

"You've started a clock I wasn't ready to start."

Her voice was level. That was the thing about it. Kimi had prepared, in some part of herself, for anger — for the version of Tayak who had said *not yet* on the phone two days ago with a flatness that meant *I mean this*. Anger would have been easier. This was something else. This was Tayak reading the ground.

"You were there," Kimi said. "You stood there."

"I know what I did."

"Then—"

"What clock, Kimi." Not a question either. A correction. She was making Kimi say it.

Kimi looked at the mound. The sweetgum at its northern edge had gone fully green, the star-shaped leaves catching the afternoon light. The multiflora rose along the fencing had small white flowers she hadn't noticed during the press conference. Everything looked ordinary and very old at the same time.

"Slade," she said.

"Slade." Tayak turned toward her now. "His office has been watching this site since before I filed the permit amendment. He has been moving carefully because he could afford to. A quiet compliance review, a thirty-day clock, a formal notice that goes through channels and gives him cover. He did not want this in public." She paused. "He has to respond now. Publicly. Which means his next move will be formal and fast, and it will be on the record, and the petition—"

She stopped. Looked at the folded paper in Kimi's hand.

"The 1697 document you just read on camera," Tayak said, "is now the centerpiece of a public record I cannot control. Every station that ran that footage, every outlet that files tonight — they will all reference the petition. They will all name the Calvert grant. Slade's office will be asked to comment. The state archives will get requests. The MHT will have to issue a statement." She turned back to the mound. "I had a sequence. I was building toward a hearing where we controlled what came in and when. That sequence is gone."

The wind came through the pines. Kimi felt it on the back of her neck, damp and green-smelling, the way air smelled near the Mattawoman in May.

"The thirty-day clock was already running," Kimi said. "You told me that. Twenty-two days left."

"Twenty-one."

"Then what were you waiting for?"

Tayak was quiet for a moment. A crow crossed the sky above the mound, low and direct, and disappeared into the treeline.

"I was waiting for Nora," she said. "There's something in her affidavit I haven't read yet. Something she's held for sixty-two years. I needed to know what it was before I let this become a public fight, because once it's public I can't un-public it, and if what she's held changes the shape of the claim—" She stopped again. "It doesn't matter now."

It did matter. That was the thing Kimi felt land in her chest — not guilt exactly, but the specific weight of having moved a piece on a board without knowing all the pieces. She had known the petition. She had known the Calvert grant. She had done the research, pulled the colonial deed images, spent three weeks at her kitchen table in Silver Spring at eleven at night building the archive that made this possible. She had not known about Nora's affidavit. She had not asked.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I thought—"

"I know what you thought."

"Tayak."

"I know." Tayak's voice did not soften, but it did not harden either. "You thought *not yet* was caution becoming paralysis. You thought someone had to act. You may be right. I don't know yet." She looked at the mound one more time, the way she looked at things she was trying to read. "What I know is that I needed to choose when this became a public fight. You made that choice for me. And now I have to work with what we have."

Kimi's phone buzzed in her jacket pocket.

She took it out. The screen showed a number she didn't recognize, then the name beneath the number that her contacts had auto-filled: *Dana Osei — Washington Post.*

She looked at Tayak.

Tayak looked at the phone.

"Don't answer that yet."

Kimi's thumb hovered. The phone buzzed a second time — a follow-up text, the notification banner appearing below the call: *Hi Kimi — Dana Osei, Post. Watched the livestream. Would love fifteen minutes for a follow-up. My cell is below. Anytime today works.*

She read it. Tayak read it over her shoulder. Neither of them spoke.

The phone stopped buzzing.

Kimi did not answer it. She lowered her thumb and locked the screen and put the phone back in her pocket, and for a moment she felt the specific discipline of that — the same discipline she'd felt at the podium when she'd read the petition slowly, word by word, not rushing, not performing, just reading — and she thought: *this is what Tayak does all day. This is the whole job. Not acting.*

Then she took the phone back out.

She did not call. She did not text. She opened her messages and looked at Dana Osei's name and the text sitting there, unread badge gone now, the message visible and unanswered and not deleted. She looked at it for two seconds. Then she locked the screen again and put it away.

Tayak had watched all of it. She said nothing. But Kimi felt her watching — felt the same quality of attention Tayak brought to the ground when she was reading a layer, the way she didn't look away from a thing just because it was uncomfortable to observe.

"I'll call him back tonight," Kimi said. "After you tell me what to say."

"I haven't decided what I want said."

"Then tell me that. Tell me what you're deciding."

Tayak looked at her. For a moment her face was unreadable — the field face, the institutional face, the face she wore when she was holding something in place while she thought. Then something in it shifted, small and deliberate, the way a layer shifted when you were close enough to see the change in soil color.

"Nora has an affidavit," she said. "Sixty-two years old. She told me she's been keeping something the council needs to hear before the state reads the petition. I don't know yet what it says. I don't know if it changes the shape of the claim or confirms it or does something I haven't anticipated." She paused. "What I know is that you put the petition on camera today, and Dana Osei watched the livestream, and the *Post* is going to file a story whether you call him back or not. And Slade is going to see that story. And whatever he does next, he will do it faster than he would have done it yesterday."

The wind moved through the pines again. Somewhere on Route 301 a truck downshifted, the engine brake loud and then fading.

"So what do we do?" Kimi asked.

Tayak picked up the folded petition copy from where Kimi had set it on the fence post. She looked at it — the washed-out gray reproduction of the old ink, the faint image of fold lines from when it had been docketed, the archives reference code at the top in small clean type. She held it the way she held artifacts in the field: not gripping, just present.

"You go back to Silver Spring," she said. "You don't post anything tonight. You don't call Osei back until I tell you to." She handed the petition copy back. "And you don't delete his message."

Kimi took the paper. "Why not delete it?"

"Because we may need him," Tayak said. "And because I want to choose when."

She turned and walked back toward the site, toward the orange fencing and the mound and the trampled grass where the tripods had stood, and Kimi watched her go — her cousin, the archaeologist, the woman whose name was on the permit — and felt the full weight of the thing she had set in motion, the clock she had started, the public record she had made that Tayak now had to work with instead of toward.

Her phone was in her pocket. Dana Osei's message was on it, unread badge gone, unanswered, not deleted.

Tayak had not looked back. She was already reading the ground.
