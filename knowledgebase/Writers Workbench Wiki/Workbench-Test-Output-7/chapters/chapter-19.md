The morning had gone quiet. Dawes had finished his rounds. Somewhere beyond the tree line, his truck had idled, turned, and faded. Now there was only the creek sound and the cicadas and the scrape of her trowel against the subsoil.

Tayak worked alone. She had sent Wren back to the lab with the previous day's samples at first light, before Wren could ask about Slade again, before anyone could ask her anything. The decision about Slade was sitting at the back of her throat like something she could not swallow, and the only thing that helped was this: the trench, the tool, the next centimeter.

She was kneeling in the stratum-two interface, roughly forty centimeters below the surface across the eastern quadrant. The soil had changed character here: darker, more organic, with a faint smell of old iron she associated with sustained human occupation rather than simple decomposition. She had been following a color shift in the matrix for the past hour, a thin line of reddish-brown running through the pale subsoil like a vein. She did not know yet what it meant. That was the part she still loved, after twenty years. The not-knowing, and the discipline of not guessing ahead of the evidence.

Her trowel tip caught something solid.

Not stone. Stone rang differently, a dead knock. This was a dull, dense resistance, the kind that meant fired clay.

She stopped.

Her hands went still. She set the trowel on the spoil board and reached for the brush, a two-inch horsehair with the bristles worn soft from use, and began to clear the surrounding soil with short, lateral strokes, working away from the object rather than toward it. The soil came away in thin layers. A curve emerged. Then a rim.

She sat back on her heels and looked at it without touching it.

The pipe bowl sat upright in the matrix, which meant it had either been set down deliberately or had come to rest that way in a single depositional event. Not tumbled, not scattered. The rim was intact. No visible fracture lines along the exposed surface. The exterior showed the reddish-brown of iron-rich local clay, darker on one side where it had sat unevenly in whatever fire had made it, paler on the other, the two zones meeting in a soft gradient that no English pipe-maker's kiln would have produced. She had seen that kind of firing cloud before, in a comparative collection at the Smithsonian. A Chesapeake earthenware assemblage from a mid-colonial site on the Patuxent. Local clay. Local fire. Local hands.

The bowl was short and thick-walled, bulbous at the base, nothing like the long-stemmed chalk-white English pipes that turned up in colonial contexts across the county. She could see the faint horizontal bands of incised decoration around the upper bowl, and below them, at the heel where the stem would have joined, something that stopped her breath.

A face.

Small, low-relief, pressed or modeled into the clay before firing. Two shallow eye-sockets, a suggested nose, a mouth that was less a mouth than a groove, as if the maker had drawn a single finger across the wet clay and let it stand. Not decorative. Not incidental. A face put there on purpose, by someone who knew what faces in clay meant.

She reached for her phone.

The light was wrong. The sun had moved behind the tree line and the trench was in partial shadow, so she pulled the small LED ring light from her kit and clipped it to her phone's case. She took the first photograph from directly above, with the scale bar and north arrow placed on the spoil board at the bowl's edge. Then she shifted to a forty-five-degree angle to catch the incised bands. Then a close-up of the heel, the ring light flooding the tiny face with even white light, every depression and ridge thrown into sharp relief.

Her hands were steady. She noticed this the way she noticed things she was grateful for: quietly, without dwelling on it. Twenty years of fieldwork had built that steadiness, and she relied on it the way she relied on her trowel, without thinking about it until it was the only thing between her and a mistake.

She took eleven photographs. Then she put the phone down and looked at the bowl again.

The carbonization inside the rim was thick and glossy, black as tar, a dark ring marking the depth of the tobacco chamber. Someone had smoked this pipe many times, over many years. It was not a ceremonial object set aside for occasions. It was a daily thing, a hand-worn thing, the kind of object that lived in a pocket or a pouch and came out in the evenings, or between rows of a field, or at the end of a long walk. Personal. Intimate in the way that only objects of habitual use become intimate.

She thought about the face on the heel.

A pipe-maker who put a face on the heel of a bowl was making a choice about what the object was for. Not just smoking, but something else. A reminder. A presence. She had read the published work on creolized material culture in the Chesapeake, knew the geometric banding and incised motifs that appeared on locally made pipes of this period, knew that these objects moved through multi-ethnic hands and borrowed from multiple visual vocabularies. But a face was different from a band of incised lines. A face was specific.

She pulled on a pair of nitrile gloves, thin enough that she could feel texture through them, and lifted the bowl from the matrix.

It came free cleanly. No root mass holding it. The soil beneath was slightly darker, a stain in the shape of the bowl's base, the ghost of its long occupation. She turned it in her gloved hands. The weight was right for earthenware, denser than modern porcelain, warmer somehow, though the soil temperature should have made it cool. Where the stem had joined the bowl there was a slight ridge, a seam line from hand-finishing, the place where two pieces of clay had been scored and pressed together by someone's thumb. She could trace it with her fingertip through the glove.

The stem itself was gone. Broken off somewhere in the two hundred and eighty-odd years since it had been placed here, or broken before placement. She could not tell yet. The cross-section of the break showed concentric firing colors: a darker core, a lighter outer edge, the record of oxygen levels in a fire that had burned and gone cold before anyone alive today was born.

Incised geometric banding around the upper register, partial star motif on the south face. Face pressed into the heel, the marks clean and deliberate, a thumbnail or a stylus. Stem broken at the join, the cross-section showing a darker core and a lighter outer edge, low oxygen in the firing pit, the clay breathing unevenly in the heat. She had noted the slight ridge at the join where the stem had been scored and pressed to the bowl while both pieces were leather-hard, the seam line you could still trace with a fingertip.

She wrote: *typologically consistent with mid-18th c. Chesapeake/Piscataway manufacture. Probable date range 1730-1760. Compare Luckenbach typology re: bowl profile and wall thickness. Not English ball clay. Fabric too iron-rich, walls too thick, stem too short. Local manufacture.*

She stopped.

She read what she had written. It was accurate. It was complete. It was the pipe bowl.

She turned the pen in her fingers and looked at the blank space below the last line.

Nora had said: I know whose that was. That's Matta's.

She could not write that.

She sat with it for a moment, the specific quality of not being able to write something you knew. It was not a new feeling. She had felt it in Georgia, standing over a feature she was certain was a burial before the ground had confirmed it. She had felt it in Virginia, reading a deed description that placed a colonial boundary exactly where she knew a village had been. The knowledge preceding the record. The record the only thing that counted in the rooms where decisions were made.

But this was different.

If she wrote *oral tradition attributes this object to an individual named Matta, per Nora Moyaone, age 82, Billingsley Road, Charles County*, if she wrote that in this notebook, in this field record, which was a legal document, which was subject to the permit terms, which Voss could request and Slade's office could audit and a court could subpoena, she had done something she could not undo. She had taken Nora's voice and put it into a system that did not know how to hold it. The chain had survived two hundred and eighty years by moving through people who understood what they were carrying. The moment she filed it as data, it became evidence in someone else's argument. Voss would cite it in a co-authored paper. Slade's office would use it to establish the scope of tribal claims and then find a procedural reason why those claims were insufficient. Some lawyer she had never met would stand up in a hearing and say the oral record is unverifiable, and the notebook entry would be exhibit A for why it should be dismissed.

She thought about Nora saying: sit down, there's more.

The chain did not start with Matta. Nora had said that plainly. Below Matta were other layers, other names, and Nora held them, had held them for sixty-two years, waiting for Tayak to be ready. If Tayak put what she knew tonight into the official record without a legal framework to protect it, without Nora's consent to the form of disclosure, without understanding what came below Matta and how far down the chain went, she would be handing people a fragment and letting them call it the whole thing.

She closed the notebook.

The pen clicked shut. The sound was very small in the dark cab.

Outside, the tree frogs had stopped. The fields were black on both sides of the road, the sky above them carrying the grey-orange wash of the DC corridor, and low on the southern horizon a few stars still showed where the light pollution thinned. The padded tray behind her seat held the pipe bowl in its evidence bag, the bag labeled in her own handwriting with the site number and the date and the initials TM. Matta's pipe bowl, if Nora was right. And Nora had been right about the bead, had known since 1962 that it was there, that it belonged to Saya, that it had been placed in this ground by someone who understood exactly what they were doing.

She had found three artifacts. The bead, the document, the pipe bowl. Each one a layer. Each one pointing down.

The most important thing she had learned today was not in any bag or box. It was in Nora's voice on a phone call she had not recorded, telling her that the chain did not start with Matta, that there was more, that she had been waiting sixty-two years, that Tayak should sit down.

She should have gone to Nora's house three days ago. She had known that and had driven to the field instead, because the field was a problem she knew how to work. You put a trowel in the ground. You read the layers. You documented what you found. There was a protocol. The protocol held.

There was no protocol for what happened when the oral record was older and deeper and more complete than the physical one, and the person who held it was eighty-two years old, and the clock on the permit was running.

Tayak started the truck.

She pulled out of the pull-off and onto the road, headlights cutting into the dark ahead of her. North on 5, toward La Plata. She would sleep at the site tonight, she had the sleeping bag, the small camp stove, the habit of early mornings, and tomorrow she would work the unit through the afternoon, and tomorrow evening she would drive to Billingsley Road. She would bring the notebook. She would not open it until Nora told her she could.

She would sit across from Nora at the kitchen table and she would listen, and she would not write anything down until she understood what she was allowed to write, and in what form, and for whom. And when Nora said sit down, there's more, Tayak would sit down.

She knew, with the particular certainty that came not from evidence but from the shape of things, that when she walked into that house the excavation would change. Not in the field. The field would stay the same, the mound would stay the same, the forty-eight-day clock would keep running. But the thing she was excavating toward would be different from what she had thought it was. She had been thinking of proof. Stratigraphy, artifact typology, provenience data, a report that Voss could not take credit for and Slade could not procedurally freeze.

Nora was holding something that was not proof in that sense. It was older than proof. It was the reason proof mattered.

The road ran straight and dark. A seam hit under the front tires and the truck shuddered once, and behind her the pipe bowl shifted slightly in the padded tray, and she did not look back.
