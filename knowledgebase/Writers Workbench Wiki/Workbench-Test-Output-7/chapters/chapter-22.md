The pain came before the birds.

Matta had learned its habits the way a man learns a difficult neighbor — not with warmth, but with the resignation of long acquaintance. It arrived in the deep of the night, a tightening across his chest like a green-hide cord left to dry, and it stayed until the first gray light, when it loosened enough to let him breathe without counting each breath. He lay still and counted anyway. It was something to do.

When the sky outside the shutter went from black to the color of old ash, he sat up.

The act of dressing took longer than it once had. He did not hurry it. He pulled on his heavy linen shirt, worked each button with fingers that the cold had made clumsy, and sat on the edge of the bed to lace his shoes, bending forward slowly, aware of the cord across his chest tightening again with the effort and then releasing. He had not told anyone about the cord. He had not named it, even to himself, because a thing named becomes a thing discussed, and a thing discussed becomes a thing that takes over the room.

Outside, the November air hit his face like creek water. The path from the house to the workshop was thirty steps across bare ground, and he had walked it ten thousand times, but this morning he placed each foot carefully, as though the ground might have shifted in the night. It had not. The packed earth was exactly where it had always been. The workshop door stood slightly open, and a thin line of firelight showed at the gap.

He stopped.

She had not been asked to come this morning. He had said nothing yesterday about starting early. He stood in the cold for a moment, watching the light at the door, and felt something move through him that was not quite grief and not quite gratitude but lived in the same part of the chest as both.

He pushed the door open.

Tessa was crouched at the small hearth in the corner, feeding sticks to a fire she had clearly built from scratch — the wood was arranged in the careful way he had taught her, three fingers of space between each piece, nothing smothered. She was eleven years old and small for it, and the firelight made her look smaller still. She did not turn when he came in.

"You were slow this morning," she said.

"I was cold."

"You are always cold now."

He said nothing to that. He went to the workbench and stood looking at the clay he had left covered with a damp cloth the evening before. He lifted a corner of the cloth, pressed a thumb into the surface. Still good — soft enough to work, not so wet it would slump.

Tessa came to stand beside him. She was close enough that he could feel the warmth of the fire still on her clothes. She looked at the clay with the focused, slightly tilted attention she brought to everything she was learning, and he thought, not for the first time, that she had her mother's eyes — not the color, but the quality of them, the way they settled on a thing and did not slide away.

"Pipe clay?" she said.

"Pipe clay."

"Same as last week."

"Same as last week." He pulled the cloth back fully and set it aside. "But today you make the stem."

She looked at him then. The stem was the difficult part — thinner than the bowl, easier to crack in drying, and the bore had to run clean and straight or the pipe was useless. He had let her watch him make stems a dozen times. He had not let her try.

"I'll crack it," she said.

"Probably."

"Then why—"

"Because you'll crack it a few times, and then you won't." He cut a portion of clay from the block with the flat knife, set it on the board in front of her. "Roll it first. Just roll it, nothing else. Get it even."

She picked up the clay and began to work it between her palms, and he watched her hands — already competent, already knowing the right pressure without being told, the clay yielding into a cylinder that was nearly even on the first pass. He had been watching her hands for three years. They were his hands, people said, meaning the hands of the family, the hands that knew clay.

He pulled a stool to the bench and sat. Standing for long periods had become its own difficulty, and he had decided some weeks ago to stop pretending otherwise in front of the work. In front of people, still. In front of the work, no.

"Keep it moving," he said. "Don't let it sit or it'll stick."

"I know."

"I know you know. I'm talking so I have something to do."

She almost smiled. Not quite.

The fire popped and settled. Outside, the first birds started — a single call, then another answering from farther away, and then the general rising noise of morning. Matta listened to it and felt the cord in his chest loosen another degree. He breathed.

There was a thing he needed to begin telling her. Not the pipes — the pipes were real work, and the work mattered, but the pipes were not the thing. The thing was the ground. The places he had walked for forty years, the places he had marked in his memory with the precision of a man who understood that memory was the only record that could not be taken and filed in someone else's office. The hollow oak by the north field edge. The stone that sat wrong in the creek bed, too flat, too deliberate, placed by hands not water. The distance from the split-trunk cedar to the low rise — forty-three paces walking normally, thirty-eight if you were in a hurry, and you should not be in a hurry. All of it in his head. None of it written. It could not be written, because written things ended up in the wrong hands, and he had watched that happen to his father's people and his father's father's people, watched the written records become the tools of the men who held the pens.

But the ground could not be taken if someone else knew it. If someone else had it in her feet and her counting and her body's memory of the distance between one tree and the next.

He had been trying to find the way to begin for two months. He had not found it. And now Tessa was here before dawn with the fire already lit, and he understood that she had been waiting for him to begin, and that she would wait as long as he needed, and that she had no intention of making it easy for him by pretending she did not know what he was waiting to say.

"When the stem is done," he said, "I want to walk the north field with you."

Her hands kept moving. "Before it dries?"

"We'll cover it. It'll keep an hour."

"All right."

Just that. All right. No question about why the north field, no question about why this morning. He looked at the side of her face — the careful attention she was giving the clay, the way she was keeping it moving exactly as he had told her — and he thought: she is going to be fine. And then, underneath that, quieter: she is going to have to be.

The clay turned slowly under her hands. The fire breathed. Outside, the light was coming up gray and cold over the November fields, and somewhere in the tree line a crow called once and did not call again.

The cold hit them at the door — not a surprise, but a weight, the kind of November cold that had been sitting on the ground all night and had no intention of lifting. Matta pulled his coat closer and stepped out first, and Tessa followed without being told, her breath coming in small white clouds that dissolved before they reached his shoulder.

The field lay open to the north, pale with frost, the grass bent flat and silver under it. The tree line was still dark. Above it, the sky had gone from black to the particular gray-blue that meant an hour before full light — enough to see by, not enough to be seen from any distance. He had chosen the time deliberately, though he had not said so.

He walked. She walked beside him, a half-step back, the way she always positioned herself when she was learning something. He had never told her to do that. It was simply how she moved when she was paying attention.

They crossed the yard and went through the gap in the fence where the lower rail had rotted out two winters ago. He had not replaced it. He had stopped replacing things that did not strictly need replacing. Tessa stepped through without comment.

The frost crackled under their feet. The sound of it was the only sound for a while.

He stopped at the north edge of the yard, where the ground began to rise slightly toward the field proper, and he turned and looked back at the house. The single window glowed amber — the fire still going, the covered clay stem on the bench, the whole small world of the workshop sitting quiet behind them. He looked at it for a moment, fixing it.

Then he turned north again.

"You see the oak," he said.

She looked. Thirty yards ahead, at the field's edge, a single oak stood apart from the tree line — its crown split, one major limb gone, the scar running down the left side of the trunk in a pale stripe that was visible even in this light.

"Lightning," she said.

"Three years before you were born. You see it?"

"I see it."

"Twelve steps from the base. North. Count with me."

He walked. She walked beside him, and they counted together under their breath — not loudly, not in the way of a lesson being performed for anyone, but quietly, the numbers between them like something passed hand to hand.

Twelve.

He stopped. The frost had not been disturbed here. He looked down at the ground, which looked like every other piece of ground in the field — pale grass, frozen soil, nothing to mark it. That was correct. That was the point.

"Twelve steps north from the lightning oak," he said. "Say it back."

"Twelve steps north from the lightning oak."

"Not eleven. Not thirteen."

"Twelve."

He nodded and turned west. "Now we go left."

They walked along the edge of the field where the ground was rougher, the frost thicker in the low places. He did not hurry. His legs were slower than they had been and he had accepted that, but he still knew how to pace himself, and he knew this ground the way he knew his own hands — every rise, every soft place, every stone that sat wrong in the earth.

The stone was where it always was. It lay half-buried at the field's western margin, roughly the size of a large loaf, its upper surface rounded and smooth, its left end tapered to a blunt point. He had found it when he was young — not placed it, found it — and had understood immediately that it looked like something. A dog sleeping on its side, head down, one leg extended.

He stopped beside it.

Tessa looked at it for a long moment. He watched her face in the gray light — the stillness of it, the way she was taking the stone in without rushing to name what she saw.

"Dog," she said finally.

"Sleeping dog."

"Sleeping dog."

"Left at the sleeping dog. Which means?"

She looked back the way they had come, orienting herself. "East," she said. "We go east from here."

"We go east. Along the ridge line. Count the cedars."

They turned. The ridge rose gently ahead of them, and along its crest the cedars stood at irregular intervals — dark shapes against the pale sky, their branches still, no wind this morning. He walked and she walked with him, and she counted under her breath, her lips moving slightly.

One. Two. Three.

"Fourth cedar," he said, before she reached it.

"Fourth cedar."

"Stop at the fourth. Face south. The ground drops away — you'll feel it before you see it in the dark."

She stopped at the fourth cedar and turned south, and he watched her feet shift slightly as she felt the slope begin.

"I feel it," she said.

"That's the place."

She said nothing. He had expected a question — what place, what is here, what am I standing over — but she said nothing, and he understood that she already knew, or knew enough, or had decided that the knowing would come with the telling and she did not need to reach for it ahead of time.

He respected that. He had always respected that about her.

"Say it all back," he said. "From the beginning."

She turned to face him. She still would not look directly at him — her eyes went to his coat, his hands, the ground between them — and he had stopped trying to decide whether this was grief or respect or simply the way she managed herself when she was holding something large. Maybe all three. Maybe there was no difference.

"Twelve steps north from the lightning oak," she said. "Left at the sleeping dog. East along the ridge. Count the cedars. Stop at the fourth. Face south."

"And?"

"The ground drops. That's the place."

"Again."

She said it again. Word for word, the same rhythm, no hesitation. He had known she would. He had not been testing her memory — her memory had never needed testing. He had been giving her the repetition because repetition was how you made something part of the body rather than just part of the mind, and the body forgot less.

He turned and looked south, the way she was facing. The field was fully visible now, the light coming up fast, the frost beginning to lose its sharp edge. A crow moved across the far tree line and disappeared.

"There is more to it than the place," he said. "There are things in the ground."

She waited.

"Most of them — the pipe, the documents, the things I've been keeping — those can come up again someday, if the time is right. If someone needs to show them. You understand what I mean."

"I understand."

"But the deepest thing stays."

He felt her go still beside him.

"There is a copper piece," he said. "Old. Older than me, older than my father, older than anyone I have known or heard named. It was put there by someone who had reason to put it there, and it has stayed there, and it must stay there." He paused. "Whatever else is moved, whatever else is shown to whoever needs to see it — that piece does not come up. It is not evidence. It is not a thing to be carried to an assembly or laid on a table or used to prove anything to anyone."

The frost crackled somewhere behind them — a bird landing, or the ground shifting as the cold eased.

"What is it then?" Tessa said.

He thought about this. He had thought about it for years, in the way you think about something that does not resolve, that simply sits with you and becomes part of how you understand everything else.

"A promise," he said. "Someone made a promise with it. You don't break a promise because you need the coin."

Tessa looked at the ground. Her jaw was set — the same set he had seen on her mother's face when her mother was deciding something — and after a moment she nodded.

"The deepest thing stays," she said.

"The deepest thing stays."

"I'll know it because—"

"Because you'll feel the ground drop before you see it. Because it's the fourth cedar. Because you'll have said it enough times that your feet will know the count before your mind does." He looked at her. "Say it all again. This time with the copper piece."

She said it. All of it, from the lightning oak to the sleeping dog to the ridge and the cedars and the south-facing slope, and at the end: *the deepest thing stays*.

The light was full now, pale and flat, the frost gone from the grass closest to the tree line. His legs ached with the cold and the standing, and he was aware of his own breath in a way he had not been aware of it a year ago — the slight labor of it, the way his chest had to work a little harder than it used to for the same amount of air.

He did not say anything about that.

"We should go back," he said. "Your stem will be drying."

She turned without a word and walked back the way they had come, and he followed her, watching the way she moved across the frost-pale ground — steady, unhurried, her feet finding the path without looking down. As if she had walked it before. As if she was already beginning to make it hers.

He thought: *good*.

He thought: *it will be enough*.

He was not certain of the second one, but he held it anyway, because some things had to be held before they could be true, and he had learned that late and was trying to make use of what time remained to practice it.

The workshop smelled of wet earth and wood smoke and the faint sourness of clay left too long under damp cloth. Matta pulled back the linen covering the drying rack and checked the stems he had set out the night before. Two had cracked along the channel — the air had been too dry, the fire too high. He set them aside without comment. Tessa hung her coat on the peg by the door and came to stand beside him.

"Take that one," he said.

She picked up the unfired bowl he had left on the board — thumb-sized, pale as ash, the clay still soft enough to mark. She turned it in her fingers the way he had shown her. Feeling for thin spots.

"The wall here," he said, and touched the rim without taking it from her. "Too thick. You'll get a crack when it fires."

She pressed her thumb against the outside and her forefinger inside and worked the clay thinner, her tongue between her teeth. Her hands were cold from the walk — he could feel it when his fingers had brushed hers, cold the way river water was cold in November, the kind that did not leave quickly.

She pressed too hard. The rim buckled.

"Slower," he said.

"I know."

"Then do it slower."

She started again. He watched without speaking. The fire had burned down to a steady low heat, enough to keep the workshop from freezing, not enough to dry the clay too fast. Outside, a woodpecker worked at something in the oak. The sound came in through the gap above the shutter — three strikes, a pause, three more.

Tessa's bowl was improving. The wall was evening out. But she was holding the piece too tight — her knuckles had gone white — and he could see the clay beginning to compress unevenly under the grip.

"Here." He stepped behind her and put his hands over hers. Her fingers were rigid. "Loose," he said. "Like you're holding water."

She let out a breath and her hands went soft under his. He guided the rotation — slow, the same pressure all the way around — and after a moment she had it. He stepped back.

She kept going. The bowl turned in her hands with a steadier rhythm now, the wall thinning to the right depth, the rim coming true. He watched her find it and not lose it.

"Better," he said.

She did not answer. She was concentrating.

He sat down on the stool by the board and picked up a half-finished stem — one he had been working for three days, rolling it smooth against a flat stone, drawing the channel with a length of wire. The stem had to be thin enough to draw air cleanly but thick enough not to split at the joint. He had broken six of them this winter before his hands had remembered the right pressure. His hands were not what they had been.

He rolled the stem. The clay gave off its faint mineral smell, cold and close, like turned earth.

"The angle," he said. "When you set the bowl to the stem. You remember what I told you."

"Fifteen degrees," Tessa said. "From the horizontal."

"Not fifteen. Not a number. A feeling. Fifteen is what you say if someone asks. What you do is different."

She looked up from the bowl.

"Hold your wrist," he said. "Flat. Now tilt it — just enough that the smoke rises without the tobacco falling forward." He demonstrated with his own wrist, the stem laid across it. "That's the angle. You'll know it because the piece will sit right in your hand. If you're fighting it, the angle is wrong."

She tried it with the bowl, miming the join. He watched her adjust twice and then find it.

"Yes," he said.

She set the bowl down carefully on the board, away from the edge. He went back to rolling the stem.

They worked without speaking for a while. The woodpecker had stopped. The fire settled with a soft collapse of ash. Matta's chest was warm from the walk and the fire both, and he was grateful for it — the warmth had a way of making the labor of breathing less noticeable, and he preferred not to notice it.

"The channel," he said. "Wet the reed before you draw it. Dry reed tears."

Tessa picked up the short length of river reed from the board and dipped it in the water bowl. She pressed it into the stem she had started the day before — still soft enough to work, barely — and drew it through in one slow pull. A clean channel. She held it up to the window light and looked through it.

"Good," he said.

"My grandmother did this," she said. Not a question.

He set the stem down. "Your grandmother's mother. Before her." He paused. "It goes back further than that, but I only know the names back to her."

Tessa lowered the stem. She was quiet for a moment, the reed still in her hand, the water dripping from its tip onto the board.

"Who taught you?" she said.

"My grandmother."

She nodded. She did not ask anything else. He did not offer anything else. The thing that was between them — the thing neither of them was saying — sat in the workshop the way smoke sat, filling the upper air without getting in the way of the work.

He picked up his stem again. She went back to the channel.

After a time he said: "A pipe is not just a pipe."

She waited.

"A person who knows how to read it can tell where the maker came from. The clay — where it was dug. The shape of the bowl — whose hands shaped it, whose style they learned from. The angle of the join." He turned the stem in his fingers. "It keeps a record of the person who made it, whether they meant it to or not."

Tessa looked at the bowl on the board. She was thinking.

"So someone could find one of yours," she said. "And know."

"Know something. Not everything. Enough."

"And mine?"

He looked at her hands — the clay still on her fingers, the marks her thumbs had left in the bowl's wall. "Yours will look like mine for a while," he said. "That's how it should be. And then it will start to look like yours, and that will be different, and that will also be how it should be."

She picked up the bowl again and turned it slowly, the way he had shown her. Feeling for thin spots.

The fire gave off a low, even heat. The clay on the board was beginning to stiffen at the edges — they had maybe another hour before the pieces would need to be covered again. Matta's hands moved without hurry, rolling the stem smooth against the stone, and beside him Tessa worked in silence, her cold fingers finding the rhythm of the thing, and the workshop held them both in its ordinary smell of smoke and earth and damp cloth, and outside the light lay flat and pale over the frozen ground, and neither of them said anything more about grandmothers or records or what was kept and what was passed, because the work itself was saying it, and they both knew how to listen.

The fire had burned down to coals by midday. Matta set the covered board of pipe stems in the corner away from the draft and washed the clay from his hands in the bucket by the door. The water was cold enough to ache. He dried his hands on the rough cloth hanging from the peg and did not look at how long it took to get the feeling back in his fingers.

Tessa had already ladled the soup. Two bowls on the table, steam rising from both. She sat on the near bench with her hands wrapped around her bowl and her eyes down, the way she always sat when she was thinking about something she had decided not to say yet.

He sat across from her. The soup was corn and dried fish, the same as it had been most of the winter, and it was good. He ate slowly. His chest was quiet today, which was a gift, and he did not examine it too closely.

Outside, the light had gone white and flat. Snow coming, maybe by nightfall. The window let in very little of it.

They ate without speaking. The fire ticked and settled. Somewhere in the wall a mouse moved once and was still.

He had been thinking, while he worked, about what he needed to say. He had been thinking about it for several weeks, carrying it the way you carry a stone in your shoe — aware of it at every step, not yet willing to stop and deal with it. But the winter was more than half gone, and his hands were not what they had been, and there were things she needed to know that she could not learn from watching him work.

He set his bowl down.

"There is a name," he said, "in the county record."

Tessa looked up.

"A baptismal name. Thomas. Thomas Pipe, they wrote it, because they could not be bothered to learn the other one." He said it without heat, the way you state a fact about weather. "That name is on a list at the church. It is on the record of the pipe-maker's license. If someone from the county comes and asks who lives here and what work he does, that is the name they will find."

She was watching him steadily.

"The pipes I sell to Harker's store — those are Thomas Pipe's pipes. The clay I buy from the merchant on the post road — that goes on Thomas Pipe's account." He picked up his bowl again, not to eat from it but to have something in his hands. "Thomas Pipe is a Christian man who makes tobacco pipes and pays his license fee on time and does not trouble anyone."

"I know," Tessa said.

"I am telling you so that you know what to say if—"

"I know, Grandfather." She said it quietly, not to cut him off. "I have always known."

He stopped.

She was looking at her bowl now, her thumbs moving slowly around its rim the way they moved around a pipe bowl when she was checking for thin spots. Not agitated. Just — precise.

"Since I was small," she said. "You had two names. Everyone who came to the house at night used one of them. Everyone else used the other."

He had thought he had been careful. He had been careful. But she had been watching him for twelve years with those quiet eyes, and apparently careful had not been careful enough, or perhaps careful had never been the point.

"The things in the ground," he said.

She nodded, just once.

"If you are ever asked—"

"I know nothing." She said it the way you say something you have memorized. Not reciting — settled. "I am the granddaughter of Thomas Pipe, a Christian pipe-maker. I know nothing about any ground."

He looked at her. The flat winter light lay across her face and showed him the line of her jaw, which was her mother's jaw, and the set of her mouth, which was something older than her mother, something he recognized from further back. She had her great-grandmother's stillness. He had not noticed that before, or had noticed it and not named it.

"Good," he said.

She lifted her bowl and drank the last of the broth. He watched her throat move.

"You should eat more," he said. "It is cold work, the clay."

"You should eat more," she said, without looking up.

He almost smiled. He picked up his bowl and finished the soup.

The fire shifted. A coal broke apart and the light in the room changed for a moment, warmer, and then settled back to what it had been. The mouse in the wall moved again, a short purposeful sound, and then nothing.

He had meant to say more. He had rehearsed it, in the way he rehearsed things now — lying awake in the early morning when the cough kept him from sleep, turning the words over, finding the right order. He had meant to say: *if I am gone before the spring thaw, the second marker is the bent oak, and you count from the stone, not from the path, because the path has moved twice in my lifetime and will move again.* He had meant to say: *do not tell anyone, not even someone you trust, because trust is a thing that changes under pressure and the ground cannot afford that.* He had meant to say: *I am sorry I waited so long to show you.*

But she had said *I have always known*, and he understood now that she had not meant only the double name, the cover story, the careful lie maintained for the county record. She had meant the other thing. The thing he had not said and had not planned to say.

He had not planned to say it because there was nothing useful in saying it. It would not change the work. It would not change what she needed to learn. It would only make her face do the thing her face had not yet done, and he did not want to be the one who made her face do that.

She knew anyway.

He had been a fool to think she didn't. She had her great-grandmother's eyes.

He set his empty bowl on the table. She set hers beside it, edges touching. Neither of them moved to clear them.

Outside, the first snow had begun — he could tell by the way the light shifted, going softer, the shadows losing their edges. It would be a light snow. Not enough to stop work tomorrow.

"The bent oak," he said. "Past the second crossing."

"I know," she said.

"I know you know. I am saying it again so it stays in you."

She folded her hands on the table. "The bent oak past the second crossing. Count from the stone, not the path. Twelve paces north-northeast, which is toward the place where the sun rises in the cold months, not the warm ones."

"Thirteen," he said. "Thirteen in my stride. Yours will be different. You need to walk it."

"We walked it this morning."

"Walk it again tomorrow. And again after the snow."

She looked at him then. Not the way she had looked at him over the pipe work — that was a student's look, attentive, measuring. This was different. This was the look of someone who has decided to bear a thing without flinching, and is showing you that she has decided it, so that you will not feel you have to protect her from it.

He held her look for a moment. Then he looked at the fire.

"We should cover the stems before the temperature drops," he said.

"Yes," she said.

She stood and took the bowls to the shelf. He pushed himself up from the bench — slowly, the way he did everything slowly now, without making a performance of it — and crossed to the corner where the board waited under its damp cloth. He lifted one edge of the cloth and checked the pieces. The stems had firmed a little in the midday cool. The bowls were holding their shape. He lowered the cloth back.

Tessa came and stood beside him, close enough that he could feel the warmth of her. She looked at the covered board the way he had looked at it.

"They're good," she said.

"They're good," he agreed.

He did not say: *I will not see them fired.* She did not say: *I know.* They stood together in the workshop's close smell of smoke and damp clay and the faint mineral cold of the winter air coming under the door, and neither of them said the hard thing, because neither of them needed to, and because the work was still there, and the ground was still there, and the names she carried in her body were still there, and that was enough. That was what there was.

After a moment he said, "Wet the stems again before you cover them for the night. They'll crack if they dry too fast."

"I know," she said.

"I know you know."

She almost smiled. He did not see it — he had already turned back to the bench — but he heard it in the small sound she made, not quite a breath, not quite a word, and he was glad of it. He was glad of it the way you are glad of warmth when you have stopped expecting it.

The light was going.

Matta watched it happen the way he watched most things now — without hurrying it, without looking away. The window above the bench held a grey that was thickening toward dark, and the fire had burned down to a steady orange pulse that gave more warmth than light. He had not added wood. He would, in a moment. He was not ready for the moment yet.

Tessa was still at the bench. She had her back to him, working the pipe bowl he had set aside for her two weeks ago — the one he had shaped the base of and then stopped, deliberately, leaving the rim unfinished, the walls slightly uneven on the left side where her thumb would need to correct them. He had not told her he left it that way on purpose. She had not asked. She had simply picked it up each evening and worked it a little further, the way you work a thing you know is yours.

He watched her hands.

She had her mother's hands — broad across the palm, the fingers not long but precise, the nails kept short the way a working person's nails stay short without thinking about it. She was using the trimming knife now, shaving the rim in small strokes, turning the bowl a quarter-turn between each pass. She had learned that herself. He had not taught her that particular motion. It had come out of her, the way craft comes out of a person who has been paying attention long enough.

He thought: she will be better than I was.

He thought it without grief. It was simply true, the way the bent oak was simply there past the second crossing, the way the stone was simply the right place to count from. Some things were measurements, not losses.

"Tessa."

She turned. The bowl was in her left hand, the knife in her right, and she held them both still with the ease of someone who has learned not to set things down every time she is interrupted.

"Before the next new moon," he said. "You walk the map again. Alone."

She waited.

"Not with me. Without me. The whole of it — from the field edge to the burying ground, all the markers in order, the paces from the stone, the direction from the cold sun. You walk it and you say it aloud as you walk, because the saying and the walking have to happen together or only half of it stays in you."

"I understand."

"And when you have a child old enough—" He stopped. The cough was not coming; it was only the habit of stopping that the cough had made in him, the small pause before each long sentence to be sure the sentence would arrive whole. He went on. "When you have a child old enough to walk the ground, you teach it the same way. You walk it together until the child can walk it alone. Not written down. Never written down."

"Never written down," she said. Not a question. A confirmation — the way you confirm a measurement you have already taken twice.

"The writing can be taken," he said. "The ground can be surveyed away. But what is in the body—" He opened his hand, the way he did when he was reaching for a word that was not in English. "What is in the body goes where the body goes. They cannot survey that."

She looked at him for a moment with that steady look — the one that did not flinch and did not perform its steadiness. Then she turned the bowl in her hand and looked at it instead, and he understood that she was giving him privacy for what he had just said. It was a considerate thing. She had always been considerate in ways she did not announce.

"Finish it yourself," he said. He meant the bowl.

"I intended to."

"I know. I am saying it so you hear me say it. It is yours. The work in it is yours. When it fires, it will be what you made it."

She turned the bowl once more in her hands, slowly, feeling the walls with her thumb the way he had taught her — not pressing, just reading the clay, finding where it was even and where it still wanted attention. Her thumb stopped on the left side, just below the rim. She felt the slight hollow there, the place where the wall had thinned a little too much in the shaping.

"This will need building up," she said.

"Yes."

"I can do it before it dries further."

"You have time. The cold is slowing the drying."

She set the knife down on the bench and reached for the small pot of slip — the thin clay-water she mixed herself now, the right consistency without being told, a little thicker than milk, a little thinner than cream. She loaded her fingertip and worked it into the hollow with three small strokes. He watched her hands. He did not watch her face.

Outside, the snow had stopped. He could tell by the silence — not the silence of snow falling, which was its own kind of sound, a muffled pressing-down of the world, but the silence after, when the air was simply cold and still and the ground held its white without comment.

"Go," he said. "Before the path is dark."

She cleaned her hands on the rag at the bench's edge. She wrapped the bowl in the square of cloth he kept for pieces in progress — linen, worn soft from years of use, smelling of clay and the particular mineral dryness of fired work. She tucked it under her arm with the care of someone carrying something that is not yet finished and therefore not yet itself, but will be.

At the door she stopped. She did not turn around.

"The bent oak past the second crossing," she said. "Count from the stone. Thirteen paces in your stride, different in mine. North-northeast, toward the cold sun. The names of the ones who lie there, in the order they were laid. Say it walking, walk it saying."

He said nothing. That was the answer.

She went out. The door closed behind her with its familiar sound — the latch dropping, the frame settling, the brief cold that came in around the edges before the room sealed itself again.

Matta sat.

The fire needed wood. He did not get up.

He held the lesson in his mind the way you hold a finished piece before you set it down — turning it, checking the weight of it, feeling for the places where the work had gone right and the places where it had only gone well enough. He thought: the bent oak. He thought: the stone, not the path. He thought: her thumb finding the hollow without being told. He thought: she will walk it before the new moon. He thought: she will teach it to her child. He thought: the child will teach it to its child. He thought: the ground will still be there.

He was very tired.

He had not known, until this moment, how tired he had been keeping himself from being. It was the kind of tiredness that had been waiting behind the work, patient as the cold, and now that the work was done it came in the way the cold came in around the door — not all at once, but steadily, filling the room's lower air first and then rising.

He let it rise.

The fire pulsed. He watched it. Outside, the path Tessa was walking would be white now, her footprints the only marks in it, going away from his door toward the field edge and the track that led home. He could see it without seeing it — the way you see a thing you have walked so many times it lives in you past the need for eyes.

He thought: she will be strong enough.

He thought it the way he had thought *they're good* about the pipe stems — not as hope, not as prayer, but as measurement. He had walked the ground with her. He had watched her hands. He had heard her say the names in the right order without prompting, had heard her correct him on the direction of the cold sun when he had tested her with a small deliberate error, had seen her face when she understood what the mound held and what it meant that it had held it this long.

She would be strong enough. She was already strong enough. The question was not her strength.

The question — and he felt it settle into the room the way the cold settled, quiet and without announcement — was whether the ground would give her time.

He did not know the answer to that. He had never known the answer to that. He had only known what to do with the time he had, and he had done it, and now the bowl was in her hands and the map was in her body and the names were in her mouth, and that was what there was.

He reached for the wood.
