The city smelled of fresh-cut timber and horse dung and the salt-flat breath of the bay, and Saya stood in the street before the assembly hall and let the smell settle into her, the way her father had taught her to read weather by what the air carried.

She had arrived before the lamplighters finished their rounds. Two tallow lanterns still burned at the hall's entrance, their flames pulling sideways in the November wind off the water, throwing orange light across the brick face of the building. The bricks were new. She could see it in the mortar, still pale between the courses, not yet darkened by rain and time. The whole city was like that — raw and certain of itself, going up fast on the high ground above the harbor as though the men building it wanted no one to remember what had been here before.

She had walked two days. Her feet, inside moccasins worn thin at the heel, had stopped hurting somewhere on the second morning, which she knew from experience was not a good sign.

The petition was folded against her breastbone, inside the wool trade-cloth coat she had borrowed from Tomah's wife. Three pages of rag paper, written in her own hand. She had copied it four times before she was satisfied with the English, and even now, standing in the cold before the building, she found herself pressing her palm flat against her chest, feeling for the slight stiffness of the folded sheets through the cloth.

*Humble petition.* Her father had used those words once, reading to her from a document he'd copied for a Jesuit. She had been perhaps six years old, and she had asked him what humble meant. He had thought for a long moment before answering. *It means you know they have the power*, he said, *and you say so first, so they cannot use it against you as a surprise.*

She had not fully understood him then. She understood him now.

The street was beginning to fill. Men came down from the houses above the harbor, their breath clouding in the cold, their long-skirted coats dark against the pale morning. Most wore wigs, the curled kind that sat on the shoulders, and broad-brimmed hats they had not yet settled on their heads. They walked in pairs or threes, talking, and none of them looked at her. She was standing at the edge of the street, near the wall, and she understood that this was the correct place to stand — not because she had been told, but because she had been reading the geometry of power in rooms and streets since she was a child, and she knew where a woman like her was expected to be.

She watched the men enter the hall. Forty of them, perhaps more. Planters, merchants, men who owned the land by a piece of paper and a surveyor's chain. They wore their wealth in the cloth — good English broadcloth, blues and dark reds, the cuffs turned back with care. One man's coat had silver buttons that caught the lantern light as he passed. He did not look up.

She thought of Lena, asleep in the winter shelter. Four years old. Could write her own name in English letters, which Saya had taught her in the dirt with a stick, letter by letter, the way her father had taught her. *L-E-N-A*. The girl had laughed when she saw it, as though it were a game. Saya had not corrected the impression.

She thought of Paka, whose knees had kept him from making this journey. *Then we have work to do*, he had said, after she read the petition aloud at the fire. Etta had challenged her on the Wekomi omission. Siwa had sat with his eyes closed and said nothing until everyone else was finished, and then had said only: *She knows what she is doing.* Saya had not been certain that was true. She was more certain now, standing here, that it did not matter whether it was true. She was the one who could read and write English. She was the one who had walked two days. She was here.

The lamplighter came back along the street and extinguished the lanterns at the hall's entrance with a long-handled snuffer, and the brick face of the building went gray and plain in the early light. Saya watched him go. Then she looked at the door.

Her fear, stated plainly: she would walk into that room and open her mouth and the forty men would look at her and see an Indian woman in a borrowed coat and hear nothing — not the words, not the argument, not the sixteen years of presence on that land, not her father's voice in the English phrases she had memorized and practiced until they sat in her mouth like her own teeth. They would hear a sound that did not require a response.

Her hope, stated just as plainly: that one of them would listen. Not all forty. One.

She had seen it happen. She had seen Paka speak to a county clerk in La Plata and watch the man's face change — not into kindness, but into attention, which was a different thing and more useful. Attention could be worked with. Kindness was unpredictable.

She pressed her palm once more against the petition through the coat. The paper was warm from her body.

Then she crossed the street and went up the steps and pushed open the heavy door.

The smell hit her first: tallow smoke and wet wool and the particular staleness of a room where men had been arguing since before she arrived. The chamber was long and low-ceilinged, with plastered walls gone yellow-gray from fireplace smoke, and heavy beams overhead that the morning light barely reached. Boots had worn the wooden floor to a dull shine near the entrance. The benches were already filling, men settling into their places with the ease of habit, setting hats under arms, arranging papers on the long tables. At the far end of the room, on a raised platform, a man in a dark coat sat in a chair that was slightly larger than all the others.

No one stopped her at the door. She had not expected anyone to stop her, because she had not looked like someone who expected to be stopped.

She walked to the clerk's table, which sat to the left of the entrance, and placed her hand flat on the surface. The clerk looked up. He was young, with ink on two fingers of his right hand and the expression of a man who has not yet learned which surprises to show on his face.

"I have a petition," Saya said. Her voice came out level. She had practiced this. "For the honorable assembly. In the matter of the lands at the high ground in Charles County, held by the Piscataway people from time before memory."

The clerk looked at her hand on the table. Then at her face. Then at the door behind her, as though checking whether someone had let her in by mistake.

"Your name," he said.

"Saya." She held his gaze. "Daughter of—" She stopped. Her father's name was not a name she gave to men who looked at her the way this man was looking at her. "Saya of the Piscataway," she said. "The petition is signed and witnessed."

The clerk picked up his quill. He dipped it. He wrote something in the journal on the table before him, and she could not see what he wrote, and she made herself stand still and not try to read it upside down, because that would show him something she did not want him to see.

"You'll wait," he said. It was not a question.

"Yes," she said. "I'll wait."

She stepped back from the table and found a place near the wall where she could see the room without being in the center of it. The benches were full now. The man on the raised chair at the far end had put on his hat. Someone called *Order* in a flat, carrying voice, and the murmur of the room dropped by half.

She unfolded her hands. She folded them again.

She had the petition. She had her English. She had the name of Judge Notley's ruling, which she had memorized: *ancient possessors*, the judge had written, and she planned to say those words in this room, in front of these men, and watch their faces when she said them.

What she did not have was any way to know, standing here against the wall in a borrowed coat with cold feet and two days of road behind her, whether any of it would be enough.

The clerk rose from his table and walked toward the raised chair. She watched him lean down and speak quietly to the man sitting there. The man listened. He did not look up.

The clerk came back.

"The assembly," he said, "will hear your petition after the morning's other business is concluded."

*After.* She heard the word and understood its weight. After meant: last. After meant: when the men are tired and want their dinner. After meant: when the light in the room is poor and attention has already been spent on things that matter.

"Thank you," she said, because her father had taught her that *thank you* in English was not the same as gratitude — it was a form of patience, and patience was a kind of power, and she needed every kind she had.

The morning's other business took two hours.

She stood through all of it.

No one offered her a chair. No one offered her a place on the benches. The men nearest the wall glanced at her once or twice in the first hour and then stopped, the way you stop noticing a post or a beam — something that is simply there and not a problem, not yet.

She counted them. It gave her something to do with her eyes while her feet ached on the cold floor. Forty men, or close enough: planters in good broadcloth, merchants with ink-stained cuffs, two men whose wigs sat slightly crooked from the journey. One man near the front had a coat the color of a winter sky, deep blue wool with brass buttons, and he kept one hand flat on the table as though steadying himself against weather that wasn't there. Another picked his teeth with a folded edge of paper. A third had fallen asleep sitting upright, his chin dropping and catching, dropping and catching, while the man beside him pretended not to notice.

They debated a road. Then a mill. Then a question about tobacco inspection fees that produced more heat than the fireplace.

Saya stood.

The fire at the far end of the room smelled of green wood — pine, not oak — and it put out more smoke than warmth. The tallow candles on the tables had burned down an inch since she came in. Above her, the heavy beams caught the smoke and held it, and the plastered walls had gone from yellow-gray to simply gray in the dimming morning light. Through the small panes of the east window, she could see the harbor below: masts, a shallop being poled out, the flat shine of the water. She fixed her eyes on the masts when the cold in her feet moved up into her knees, and she breathed through her nose, and she counted the men again to make sure her count was right.

Forty. Yes. Forty.

The road was settled. The mill was referred. The tobacco fees were tabled — she heard that word and felt it land differently now, knowing what it meant, knowing it was a kind of burial — and then the clerk stood and said something to the man in the large chair, and the man in the large chair looked up for the first time and looked directly at her.

He had a long face, reddish from weather or drink, and a periwig that had been good quality once. He said something she couldn't hear from across the room.

The clerk came to her.

"The assembly will hear the petition," he said. "You'll stand at the end of the room."

He meant the far end. Away from the door. Away from the fire. He meant: the place where a person stands who is not a member of anything.

She walked the length of the room. Forty pairs of eyes, or most of them — the sleeping man had woken up, she noticed, and was watching her with more attention than he had given the tobacco fees. Her boots made a sound on the worn floor, and she was conscious of the sound, and she kept her pace even. She reached the far wall and turned to face them.

From here she could see the whole room: the long tables, the benches, the candles, the fire, the door she had walked in through two hours ago. The man in the large chair was six feet to her left, on his raised platform, and he did not look at her again.

The clerk returned to his table. He lifted a paper. Her paper — the top copy, the one she had handed him. He cleared his throat.

"The humble petition of Saya, of the Piscataway people, to the honorable the Governor and Assembly of Maryland," he read, in the voice of a man reading a list of goods received at a warehouse. "In the matter of the high ground in Charles County, being the lands held by the said people from time before memory, and now threatened by survey and grant."

He set it down.

The room was quiet. Not the quiet of listening — she had heard the quiet of listening when she read the petition to Paka and Siwa and the Wekomi elder, and it had a texture to it, a leaning-in. This was a different quiet. This was the quiet of men who have already decided and are waiting for the formality to finish.

"The petitioner may read," the clerk said.

He held the paper out toward her.

She walked back up the length of the room. She took the paper from his hand. She walked back to the far wall.

She looked down at the words. Her own words, in her own hand, with the corrections Tomah had made in three places — *therein* for *within*, *aforementioned* for *said*, *humbly beseeching* for *asking* — and the whole of it copied fair onto the rag paper she had bought in La Plata with the last of what she had. The ink had gone slightly brown at the edges where the cold had gotten to it on the road.

She looked up.

She began.

"Your petitioner represents that the lands at the high ground in Charles County, lying along the western branch of the Potomac watershed, have been held and occupied by the Piscataway people from time before memory—"

Someone coughed.

She did not stop.

"—and that the said lands contain the burial places of the said people's ancestors, and their places of ceremony, and the gardens and fishing grounds by which they have sustained themselves in this country since long before the coming of any English grant or survey—"

The man with the blue coat had his hand flat on the table again. He was looking at the surface of the table.

She kept her voice level. She had practiced this until the words sat in her mouth like her own teeth, and now she let them come out the way they had practiced: one after the next, without hurry, without apology. She read the description of the boundaries — the ridge, the creek, the stand of great oaks on the eastern flank, the mound where the people kept their dead. She read the names of the families who had lived there. She read the reference to Judge Notley's ruling, and she said the words *ancient possessors* clearly and without emphasis, the way you say a thing that does not need emphasis because it is simply true, and she watched three men shift in their seats when she said it.

That was something.

She read the request: that the assembly acknowledge the prior claim of the Piscataway people to the said lands, and that no further grant or survey be permitted to proceed until the matter of prior occupation was fully heard and determined.

She stopped.

The room was still for a moment. Then the man in the large chair said, "Is that the whole of it?"

"Yes," she said.

He looked at the clerk. The clerk looked at his journal. Someone near the back said something quietly to the man beside him, and the man beside him nodded.

"The assembly thanks the petitioner for her attendance," the man in the large chair said. He had the voice of someone who had said *the assembly thanks* many times and meant it less each time. "The matter will be considered."

She waited.

He said nothing more.

She said, "I would ask, honored sir, that the assembly note for the record that the said lands hold the burial places of the Piscataway dead, and that any survey ordered to proceed—"

"The matter," he said, with slightly more weight, "will be considered."

She stopped. She had heard the wall in his voice. She knew what the wall meant. She had known it was there before she walked in the door, and she had walked in anyway, and she had said the words anyway, and the words were now in the room and in the clerk's journal and could not be taken back.

She folded the petition. She put it inside her coat.

The clerk rose. He said, "The question is put: shall the petition of the said Piscataway woman be referred for further survey of the lands in question, the consideration thereof to be deferred until such survey be made and returned into this house?"

*Those in favor.*

A sound like a tide going out: low, collective, indifferent.

*Those against.*

Silence.

"The petition is referred," the clerk said, and wrote in his journal, and the man in the large chair was already turning to the man on his left to speak about something else.

She stood at the far end of the room for a moment longer, because she needed that moment, because she would not walk out of this room in a hurry. Then she walked the length of the floor again — forty men, or most of them, already past her, already on to whatever came next — and she stopped at the clerk's table.

She had taken three steps toward the door and stopped. She did not know why she stopped, except that there was a question she had not asked, and she had come three days on the road to ask questions, and she would ask this one.

"How long," she said. Her voice carried in the room because the room had gone quiet around the next matter being shuffled into place, papers moving on the clerk's table, the man in the large chair settling his coat. Several heads turned. "How long will the survey take?"

The clerk looked up. He had the face of a man who managed interruptions by outlasting them.

"The survey," he said.

"The survey ordered by the assembly. The one that must be completed before the petition is heard. How long will it take?"

He set down his quill. He did so with the careful deliberateness of a man demonstrating patience to an audience.

"The county surveyor," he said, "will be directed to make inquiry as to the lands in question and to return his findings to this house."

"When."

"At such time as the survey is completed and returned."

"That is not an answer."

A man near the front bench — the one called Harwood, who had spoken earlier, who had asked whether her people constituted a recognized body capable of holding land claim under provincial law, whose voice had been the voice of a man asking a question he already knew the answer to — Harwood made a sound. Not a word. A sound. The sound a man makes when a woman continues to speak past the moment he has decided she should stop.

She did not look at him.

"I am asking," she said, still to the clerk, "because the lands in question are presently subject to survey by a party already in the field. I have told the assembly this. If the county surveyor is not directed to proceed within the fortnight, the grant survey will be complete and filed before this house reconvenes. In which case the petition will be heard against a completed record, not an open one."

The clerk looked at the man in the large chair. The man in the large chair looked at the clerk.

"The petitioner," Harwood said, from his bench, "has had her hearing."

He said it pleasantly. That was the worst of it — the pleasantness, the tone of a man explaining something to someone who could not be expected to understand it fully but who deserved, out of charity, to have it explained.

"The assembly," he continued, "has acted with considerable generosity in admitting this petition to the floor. The matter is now referred in the ordinary course. The surveyor will proceed as the assembly directs, in the assembly's time."

She looked at him then. He had a wide, well-fed face and a periwig that sat slightly crooked, and he wore his coat the way men wear coats when they have never had to wonder whether they would be cold. He looked back at her with the mild, settled expression of a man who has never lost anything he wanted to keep.

She said, "Then the assembly should know that its ordinary course will cost the Piscataway people their land."

Harwood opened his mouth.

The man in the large chair raised one hand, a small gesture, and Harwood closed it.

"The petitioner's concern is noted," the man in the large chair said. He had the voice of someone who had said *noted* many times and meant it less each time than the time before. "The clerk will record that the petitioner raised the question of survey timing. The assembly will take the matter under advisement."

The clerk was already writing. She watched his quill move across the page — small, rapid strokes, the iron-gall ink going on black and shining before it dried — and she understood that what he was writing was not what she had said but a version of what she had said that would fit inside the assembly's record without disturbing it.

"I cannot say," the clerk said, without looking up, "how long the survey will take."

She stood still for a moment.

The room had already moved on. She could feel it — the way a room moves on, the way attention shifts like water finding a lower level, the murmur of the next matter beginning to gather at the edges of the silence. Forty men, or most of them, their minds already elsewhere.

She went to the clerk's table.

He looked up. She reached past him and lifted the ink bottle from the table. It was a small green-glass bottle, the stopper loose, the iron-gall ink inside still fresh from the morning's session. She did not ask. She simply took it the way you take a thing that belongs to the moment.

The clerk's hand moved halfway toward it and stopped. He went very still.

She pulled the stopper free and pressed her right palm flat into the open mouth — not a thumb, not a mark, her whole hand, spread wide, the ink cold and thick against her skin. Then she reached into her coat and drew out the copy she had kept — the one she had made by hand at the camp table two nights ago with Okwi holding the candle steady — and pressed her palm to the bottom of the page, beside her written name, and held it there.

The paper gave slightly under the pressure. She could feel the grain of it through the ink — rag paper, laid lines running under her palm like the lines of a field.

She lifted her hand. The print was full and dark: the heel of her palm, the four fingers, the thumb, the whorls of her skin pressed into the page as clearly as a face.

Behind her, she heard a bench scrape. One of the two delegates still in the room — the younger one, who had voted with Harwood and then looked at his hands — got up and walked to the window. He stood with his back to her and looked out at the harbor. The other delegate, older, with a rust-colored coat gone thin at the elbows, watched her from across the room. He did not look away. His face held something she could not name — not sympathy, not hostility, something more like the expression of a man watching a thing he knows he will remember.

The clerk said nothing. His quill was suspended above the journal page, a small bead of ink forming at the tip and threatening to fall.

She stoppered the bottle and set it back on his table in precisely the place she had taken it from. She looked at her right hand — ink-black from palm to fingertip, the lines of her skin raised and dark. She did not wipe it. She folded the document carefully, right side in, the handprint against itself so it would not smear the rest, and tucked it inside her coat against her ribs where she could feel it.

"The copy is mine," she said to the clerk. "The assembly said so."

He nodded once, a small, tight movement.

"The record," she said, "should show that the petitioner kept her copy."

He wrote it. She watched him write it — four words, five — and she thought: *that, at least, is true.*

She turned and walked to the door.

The older delegate in the rust-colored coat said, quietly, to no one in particular, "She'll have a hard road home."

She pushed the door open and the cold hit her — salt water and horse dung and the thin, clean smell of ice on the harbor — and she went down the steps and into the street without looking back, her right hand still black with ink, the document pressed warm against her ribs, the masts of the shallops rising above the low rooftops at the bottom of the hill.

She thought of Okwi at home with the third copy and the oilskin. She thought of Lena, four years old, who could write her own name and did not yet know what the name was for.

She walked faster.

The street was unpaved and rutted from last week's frost, the mud frozen now into ridges that caught her boot heels and tried to turn her ankles. She did not slow down.

The harbor sat at the bottom of the hill, a quarter-mile of grey water with three shallops at anchor and a fourth working out toward the bay, its single sail pale against the November sky. She had come in on a boat like that one, two days ago, watching the town rise above the water — the brick walls of the new State House on the high ground, the frame houses going up along the streets below, the smell of fresh-cut lumber and lime mortar and the particular cold of salt water in late autumn. She had thought, coming in, that it looked like a town that believed in itself. She thought the same thing now and found it did not move her.

She turned north at the bottom of the hill, away from the docks.

The petition had failed. She held that fact the way you hold a stone — not gripping, just feeling the weight of it. The assembly would order a survey in spring. The surveyor would come with his Gunter's chain and his compass and his notebook, and he would walk the high ground and measure it and put numbers to it, and the numbers would go into a plat, and the plat would go into the assembly journal, and after that the high ground would be a document rather than a place, and documents could be bought and sold and granted and re-granted until there was no memory left of what had stood there before the chain.

She knew how surveys worked. She had watched one, when she was eleven, on the eastern boundary of her father's land. Two men with a chain and a compass, moving through the trees, calling numbers to each other. She had not understood then what they were doing. Her father had stood at the edge of the field and watched them with his arms crossed and said nothing, and she had stood beside him and watched him watch them, and she had understood from his silence that the numbers they were calling were not measurements of the land but measurements of something else — of how much of the land would remain his when they were done.

He had been right to be quiet. The survey had gone against him. He had not argued. He had walked back to the house and taught her three new words in English that afternoon, sitting at the table with the hornbook between them, his voice patient and even, as though the lesson were the only thing that mattered. She had not understood that either, not then. She understood it now.

She walked north along a street she did not know the name of, past a cooper's yard where a man was stacking barrel staves in the cold, past a house with a smoking chimney and a smell of boiling salt pork coming through the shuttered window, past a woman in a brown wool mantua who looked at Saya's ink-black hand and then looked away. The hand had dried to a dull, cracked black, the lines of her palm raised and dark, the ink settled into the whorls of her fingers. She looked at it as she walked. She did not wipe it on her coat.

The document was still pressed against her ribs, inside the coat, warm from her body. She could feel the slight stiffness of the folded paper when she breathed — the rag paper, the laid lines she had felt under her palm when she pressed it flat. She thought of the clerk's face when she had reached past him for the ink bottle. The small, arrested movement of his hand. The way he had gone still.

She thought of the older delegate in the rust-colored coat. *She'll have a hard road home.* He had said it to no one, to the room, to himself, and she had not answered because there was no answer to give a man like that — a man who could see clearly and still do nothing with what he saw. She did not hate him for it. She had stopped expecting that kind of man to be anything other than what he was, and stopping had made her lighter.

She thought of Paka's hands on the corner of the first page — the touch of a man who had held documents before and knew what they cost and what they were worth. She thought of Etta's face when Saya had read the Wekomi passage aloud, the still, broad face with its deep lines, the eyes that missed nothing. She thought of Siwa sitting with his eyes closed, listening, outliving every argument he had ever lost. She thought of the Wekomi elder's tired, unsurprised stillness when she had told him of the omission, and the way he had said *I will think on it* in a tone that meant he had already thought on it, had been thinking on it for years, and had arrived at a patience she did not yet have.

She had not cried in the assembly chamber. She had not cried on the steps, in the cold, with the salt smell of the harbor coming up the hill. She did not cry now. She had made a decision somewhere between the moment the clerk had read the resolution — *that the said petition lie upon the table pending further survey* — and the moment she had pressed her hand into the ink, and the decision had dried her out from the inside, the way a cold wind dries a wound. Grief would come later, in the winter shelter, in the dark, when Lena was asleep and Okwi was asleep and there was nothing left to decide. She would let it come then. Not now.

She had two days on the water before she reached the creek landing. Two days to think through what came next.

The petition would not stay in the assembly's hands. The assembly would table it, order the survey, and the survey would do what surveys did — it would make the high ground into a number, and the number would belong to someone else. She could not stop the survey. She had known that before she came. What she had not known, before she stood in that room with forty men and read her words aloud into their silence, was how completely they intended to proceed as though she had not spoken at all — not with hostility, not even with contempt, but with the polite, procedural indifference of men who had already decided that her words were a category of thing that required no answer, only management.

She had spoken anyway. She had read every word. And now the words were in the record — the clerk had written them, she had watched him write them — and the record was a thing that could not be entirely unmade, even by men who intended to unmake it. The language was there. *Ancient possessors.* Judge Notley's phrase, carried forward in the petition, entered into the assembly journal in the clerk's careful hand. Somewhere in that building, in that journal, her words were drying in iron-gall ink on rag paper, and they would stay there until the paper rotted or the building burned.

But paper rotted. Buildings burned.

She had thought about this for a long time — longer than the petition, longer than the winter she had spent copying the Notley ruling and drafting the language and checking it with Tomah and reading it to Paka and Etta and Siwa by firelight. She had thought about it since she was nine years old, standing at her father's side watching two men with a chain measure his land into someone else's property. The thing that outlasted paper was not another document. The thing that outlasted paper was a place.

The high ground would hold what the assembly would not.

She had the document against her ribs. She had Okwi at home with the third copy and the oilskin, waiting. She had the bead — her mother's bead, the cobalt glass bead that had come south along the Potomac with a trading party and stayed, the way her mother had stayed, the way Saya had stayed, the way they had all stayed when staying was the harder thing. She had the mound on the high ground above the creek, the mound that had held the community's dead for longer than anyone living could say, longer than any survey, longer than any grant.

Okwi would put the petition into the ground with the bead. Both of them together, wrapped in oilskin, buried deep in the mound where the survey chain could not reach and the assembly's indifference could not follow. Not as defeat. As testimony. As the kind of proof that does not require a clerk to record it or a judge to acknowledge it or an assembly to vote on it — proof that simply *is*, in the way that ground is, in the way that bone is, in the way that a handprint pressed into paper is: a fact, waiting for whoever comes next with enough patience to look.

She did not know who would come next. She did not know how long the waiting would be. She thought of Lena, four years old, who could write her own name and did not yet know what the name was for. She thought: *I will tell her.* Not everything — not yet. But enough. Enough so that Lena could tell her own daughter, and her daughter could tell hers, and the telling could carry the thing forward through time the way the ground carries bone: without language, without paper, without the assembly's permission.

The street opened onto a wider track heading north, away from the harbor and the town's new brick ambitions, toward the landing where the shallop would be waiting. The cold had sharpened. The sky above the bay was the color of pewter, and the wind off the water smelled of ice and distance. She pulled her coat tighter with her clean hand, the ink-black hand held slightly out from her side, the fingers spread, the lines of her palm dark and clear.

She walked faster. Behind her, the State House sat on its hill above the harbor, its windows small and bright with tallow light, its records drying in their journals, its survey ordered and its petition tabled, its business already moving on to the next thing. She did not look back at it.

She was already thinking about the ground.
