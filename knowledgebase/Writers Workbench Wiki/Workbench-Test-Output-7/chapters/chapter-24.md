The motion sensor had already killed the office lights twice. Each time, Slade raised one hand without looking up, and the fluorescents blinked back on.

The Washington Post article sat in the left half of his screen. He had read it four times. The headline was measured: *Piscataway Archaeologist's Find May Reopen Land Recognition Fight.* The photograph underneath it was not measured at all. Dr. Moyaone stood at the edge of a trench, one knee on the ground, holding something small between two gloved fingers. She was looking at the camera the way people look at cameras when they have forgotten the camera is there. Behind her, the mound rose against a grey November sky like a held breath.

He moved the article to a corner of the screen and opened the Notice of Review form.

The form was twelve pages in the Trust's standard template, with the department seal at the top: a blue oval, the state's silhouette, the words *Maryland Historical Trust, State Historic Preservation Office* in a font that had not changed since 1994. Below the seal sat the address block: 100 Community Place, Crownsville. The phone number. The .gov email. All of it exactly as it had been the last time he had used this form, and the time before that.

He typed the site number first. *18CH-447.* The trinomial was already in the system. He had filed his own site visit notes three weeks ago, after the permit was issued. He could see his own handwriting in the scanned attachment: *earthen mound feature, possible multi-component site, stratigraphy profile pending.* Wren Calvert had produced a clean profile since then. He had seen it in Moyaone's field notes, which Voss had forwarded without being asked.

He did not like that Voss had forwarded them without being asked.

He typed the Principal Investigator field. *Dr. Tayak Moyaone.* The cursor blinked. He left it there and picked up his coffee tumbler, found it empty, and set it back down on the ring it had already made on the desk blotter.

The legal memo from the Trust's counsel was open in a second window. He had read it twice this morning and once last night at the kitchen table while his wife moved around him putting things away. The memo was twelve paragraphs. The operative sentence was in paragraph four: *Given the recovery of what may constitute a funerary object within a prehistoric earthen mound feature, the agency's obligation to initiate NAGPRA review is not discretionary.* Below that, in a separate paragraph, the sentence he had been thinking about since Tuesday: *Failure to issue a timely notice creates liability exposure and, in the event of subsequent litigation, constitutes bad facts in the record.*

Bad facts in the record. He understood what that meant. It meant that if he did not write this letter, and if the dig produced human remains, and if anyone later sued, the first document opposing counsel would request under FOIA would be this one. Its absence, rather.

He understood it. He did not feel better for understanding it.

He typed the subject line. *Re: Notice of Suspension of Excavation Activities at Site 18CH-447 (Mound Feature, Charles County) Pending NAGPRA Review.* He read it back. It was correct. It was, in the language the form required, airtight.

The article was still in the corner of his screen. He did not look at it directly, but he was aware of the photograph. The woman kneeling at the edge of her own trench.

He had been in that field three weeks ago. He had stood at the mound's base and looked at the stratigraphy profile Calvert had pinned to the site board, clean work, the kind of work that takes patience, and he had thought without quite deciding to think it: *someone has been at this a long time.* Not Moyaone. Whoever built the thing. Whoever chose that particular rise in that particular field and began to carry earth to it, basket by basket, year by year, until the ground remembered what they wanted it to remember.

He pulled up the NAGPRA compliance database in the third window. The interface was the same pale blue it had been since the 2019 upgrade, with dropdown menus for tribal affiliation, site type, discovery category. Under *Discovery Category* he selected *Possible Funerary Object, Pending Determination.* Under *Site Type* he selected *Prehistoric Earthen Mound.* The database auto-populated three fields and flagged a fourth: *Consultation Required, Affiliated Tribe(s) Not Yet Identified.*

That was the paradox, and he knew it, and the form did not care.

The consultation the law required, the government-to-government process, the letters to affiliated tribes, the invitation to share Traditional Cultural Knowledge about the site, could not meaningfully happen while the dig was running. You could not ask a community to weigh in on a living excavation and expect the answer to arrive before the next trowel stroke. So the notice would stop the dig. And the dig, if it continued, might produce the very evidence that would answer the affiliation question the notice was asking. The law had not been written to handle this situation cleanly. The 2024 revisions to 43 C.F.R. Part 10, effective January 12th, had tightened the timelines without resolving the underlying logic.

He typed the operative paragraph. He knew it almost by memory.

*Pursuant to the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act (25 U.S.C. §§ 3001, 3013) and the implementing regulations at 43 C.F.R. Part 10, as revised effective January 12, 2024, all excavation and earth-moving activities at Site 18CH-447 must cease in the vicinity of the mound feature pending a determination of whether the site contains human remains or associated funerary objects subject to NAGPRA protections. This notice does not constitute a finding of cultural affiliation. It constitutes the initiation of the consultation process required by statute.*

He stopped. Read it back. He had written *must cease* where the statutory language read *shall cease.* He changed it. You did not improvise on statutory language.

He typed the CC line. The agency's NEPA coordinator. The cultural resources officer. The tribal liaison, that was Harkins, who was out on leave and whose email would bounce to an auto-reply until December. The State Historic Preservation Officer. He added Voss's name, then removed it, then added it again. Voss had filed the required disclosure. Voss was in the record. Leaving him off the CC line would look like a choice.

The motion sensor killed the lights.

Slade sat in the dark for a moment. Through the window, the Severn River was the color of old pewter, flat and cold under an overcast sky. A cormorant was working the near bank, diving and surfacing, diving and surfacing, indifferent to the cold.

He raised his hand. The lights came back.

He scrolled to the signature block. His name was already there, pre-populated by the template: *Garrett Slade, Cultural Resources Program Manager, RPA.* Below it, the date field. He typed today's date.

The cursor sat in the signature field, waiting.

He thought about the photograph again. The woman kneeling. The mound behind her. He thought about what his daughter had said the last time they talked, which was three weeks ago, before the Post story, before any of this: *The problem, Dad, is that the law protects the process and not the outcome.* Elena had been talking about a different case entirely, a water rights dispute in New Mexico, but he had written it down afterward on the back of a gas station receipt because it seemed like the kind of sentence worth keeping.

He looked at the form.

It was procedurally airtight. It would hold up to scrutiny. It named the right site, the right investigator, the right statutory authority. It was, in every technical sense, correct.

He saved the draft and closed his laptop.

He sat for a moment with his hands flat on the desk. Then he opened the laptop again, printed the form to the shared printer down the hall, walked to retrieve it, came back, and picked up the Pilot G2 from his left drawer. Blue ink. The same pen he used for everything. He had bought a box of twelve at the GSA supply depot in October, and they sat in a rubber-banded stack. He was not going to reach for a better pen. He was not going to pretend this was a different kind of moment than it was.

He held the nib above the signature line.

The field assessment was in his filing drawer. Forty-three pages, spiral-bound, Moyaone's name and credentials in the top left corner. He had read it once before the site visit and skimmed it a second time after. The executive summary's final paragraph was still precise in his memory: *Further excavation is essential to determining whether NAGPRA protections apply to this site.*

Which was true. Exactly true. And also the sentence his notice would make impossible to act on for the foreseeable future.

The cormorant on the Severn dived and surfaced. Dived and surfaced.

He signed.

Garrett A. Slade. Cultural Resources Program Manager. RPA. The letters came out even and practiced, the signature of a man who has signed a great many things and learned not to make them look like what they cost him.

He set the pen down.

The date stamp was on the right side of his desk, a self-inking model the office had ordered in 2019. He pressed it to the lower right corner of the signature page. *4:52 PM.* The ink was dark blue, slightly uneven at the edges where the stamp pad was going dry.

He straightened the three pages, tapped them once against the desk to align the edges, and slid them into the pre-addressed envelope. Trust compliance office, Annapolis. The address he had typed himself because his assistant had gone home at four. He sealed it with the moistened sponge from the corner of his desk and pressed the flap flat with his palm.

He held the envelope for a moment. Then he set it in the outgoing tray.

He shut down his computer. The dual monitors went dark in sequence, left then right, and the room was lit only by the window, which showed the Severn going grey-black under the last of the afternoon light. He put on his coat, picked up his bag, and turned off the desk lamp.

In the lobby, Phil, the desk attendant who had worked the county records room on Tuesdays for as long as Slade could remember, now covering a shift at the Trust building's front desk, raised a slow palm-out wave without looking up from the sign-in sheet.

"Night, Mr. Slade."

"Night, Phil."

The door opened onto cold air and the smell of the river, brackish and faintly metallic, the way tidal water smells when the temperature drops and the surface goes still. He walked to his car. A 2021 RAV4, silver, with a crack in the passenger-side mirror he kept meaning to fix. He sat in it for a moment without starting the engine.

Then he started it and drove home., -

The drive to Severna Park was forty minutes in light traffic. It was not light traffic.

He took Route 50 west and merged onto 97 north and found himself in the thickening red of the evening commute, moving in increments, the radio on low. A woman was talking about the Fed. He turned it off.

He began, without deciding to, to think about Carol.

She would be home. Tuesday was her late day at the practice, but she was usually back by six, and it was five-fifteen now. She would have started something on the stove. She had a habit of starting things and then reading while they cooked, so there was always a small controlled crisis when he arrived, something that needed stirring or turning, and he had learned to come through the door ready to help without being asked. He liked this about their evenings. It was one of the things he liked.

She would ask about his day.

She always asked, and he always told her, or told her enough: the bureaucratic texture of it, the emails, the drive to the field site if there had been one, the particular quality of whoever he had dealt with. She was good at listening to the parts that mattered and letting the rest go. She had been a social worker for eleven years before the private practice, and she had a clinician's patience for other people's institutional frustrations. He had never had to explain to her why a compliance notice was not simply a compliance notice.

So he would have to be careful.

He moved forward six car lengths. A truck ahead of him was signaling to change lanes and nobody was letting it in. He let it in.

The day's sequence arranged itself in his mind the way he knew it would: the morning email from Marsh with the risk assessment attached, the forty-five minutes spent reading Tayak Moyaone's preliminary field report, the drive down to Charles County, the site. He had not planned to go to the site. He had planned to send a letter. But he had read Wren Calvert's stratigraphy profile at seven in the morning and had found himself, at nine, in the parking garage, his bag already in the car.

He would tell Carol he had gone to the site. That was not the part he needed to think about.

He would tell her about the Post article, she might have seen it already, she read the Post over breakfast, and about the NAGPRA review, and about the permit suspension. He would explain the paradox, because she would see it herself if he didn't: the law meant to protect the site was the same law that required him to stop the work proving the site needed protecting. She would find this interesting in the way she found systemic failures interesting, with a kind of weary precision, the way she described insurance company denials to him. *It's not malice,* she said once, about something else entirely. *It's architecture.*

He would tell her it was standard procedure. Which it was.

He moved through the interchange at 97 and the traffic loosened slightly, enough that he could hold a steady forty. The sky to the west was the color of old pewter, the light gone flat and cold. He turned his headlights on.

He would not tell her about the pen.

He recognized this as the thing he was editing. He had not decided to edit it. It was simply not appearing in the version of the day he was assembling, the way a word drops from a sentence in revision and you only notice it's gone when you read the sentence aloud and it sounds cleaner than it should.

He had held the pen above the signature line for how long? Forty seconds. Perhaps less. Perhaps more. Long enough that the cormorant on the Severn had dived and surfaced twice. Long enough that he had read Tayak Moyaone's sentence again in his head: *Further excavation is essential to determining whether NAGPRA protections apply to this site.* Long enough to know that he was not going to put the pen down.

He had signed. The letters had come out even.

There was nothing to tell about the pen. It was a Pilot G2, blue ink. He had not reached for a better pen. He had signed the way he signed everything, and the letter was in the outgoing tray, and in the morning it would go to Annapolis, and Tayak Moyaone would receive her copy by end of week, and the site would go quiet. That was the sequence. That was what had happened.

He took the exit for Severna Park and came down the ramp and turned onto the surface road, past the dry cleaner and the pharmacy and the Korean restaurant he and Carol had been meaning to try for two years. The streetlights were coming on. Someone had put Christmas lights on a maple in their front yard, the white kind, the ones that look like stars when you are not looking directly at them.

Carol would ask if he was all right.

She would not ask it directly. She would ask something adjacent: whether he was hungry, whether he wanted wine or tea, whether the drive had been bad. And he would say the drive was fine, and he was hungry, and wine would be good, and she would hear whatever she heard in that, and she would not press. This was also something he liked about their evenings. The space she left.

He pulled into the driveway and turned off the engine.

The kitchen light was on. Through the front window he could see the blue flicker of the television, which meant she was watching something while she cooked, which meant the stove crisis was already in progress. He sat for a moment with the engine ticking as it cooled.

A compliance notice. Standard procedure. A site with credible indicators of NAGPRA-covered material, and a legal obligation to halt ground disturbance pending review. He had done what the statute required. He had done what Marsh and the agency attorney had advised. He had done what any responsible Cultural Resources Program Manager would do, faced with the same facts and the same risk assessment and the same six CC lines.

He picked up his bag and got out of the car.

The air smelled of someone's woodsmoke and the cold salt edge of the bay two miles east. His breath showed white. He locked the car by habit, the double-chirp loud in the quiet street, and walked to the front door and went inside.

The kitchen smelled of garlic and something with wine in it. Carol had the small television on the counter turned to a British baking show, the sound low, the contestants moving in anxious silence behind a man explaining something about choux pastry. She was standing at the stove with a wooden spoon and did not turn around when he came in.

"Thirty minutes," she said.

He hung his coat on the hook by the door and set his bag at the foot of the stairs and washed his hands at the kitchen sink, standing beside her. The pan held chicken thighs in a dark sauce, shallots going soft at the edges. She had opened a bottle of Barboursville Viognier, a Virginia white she kept for weeknights when she wanted something with enough body to hold up to a pan sauce.

"Pour me one?" she said.

He poured two glasses and set hers on the counter where she could reach it without moving the pan. She picked it up without looking, took a sip, and put it back.

"Bad day?" she said.

"Complicated."

"Sit. Tell me."

He sat at the kitchen table, which was already set: placemats, the good cloth napkins she used when she knew he was coming home tired, which he had not told her but which she had apparently known anyway. He turned his glass by the stem.

"Did you see the Post this morning? The piece on the Charles County mound site?"

"The burial mound." She stirred the pan. "I saw the headline. I didn't read past the first paragraph. Route 301?"

"The connector, yes. There's an active excavation on the footprint, a Phase I survey that turned into something more significant. A Piscataway archaeologist, Moyaone, found a 1697 petition in the second stratum. Colonial-era document, hand-pressed thumbprint, buried in oilskin."

Carol turned around. She had a line between her brows that appeared when something interested her against her will. "In the ground."

"In the mound. Deliberately buried. Her field notes indicate it was placed alongside a trade bead, cobalt glass, pre-contact layer beneath."

"How far down?"

"Deep enough to matter." He drank. "The Post piece ran this morning. By this afternoon I had Marsh on the phone and a risk assessment memo from legal, and by three o'clock I had drafted a suspension notice."

Carol turned back to the stove. "What kind of suspension?"

"Excavation halt, pending NAGPRA review. The site has indicators: the burial context, the mound morphology, possible funerary objects. Under the statute I am required to stop ground disturbance until we can determine whether human remains or associated objects are present."

She was quiet for a moment, stirring. The baking show cut to a commercial.

"So you stopped the dig," she said.

"I issued the notice, yes."

"That signed today?"

"That signed today."

She moved the pan off the heat and brought her glass to the table and sat across from him. She had flour on her left forearm she hadn't noticed.

"Will she be able to finish before the bulldozers get there?"

"I don't know. The review process has a timeline. Thirty days minimum, more likely sixty once consultation is factored in. The Route 301 schedule, " He stopped. "It's tight."

"Tight meaning probably not."

"Tight meaning I don't know."

Carol looked at him. She had grey eyes that had been green when they met, or that he had thought were green, and he still wasn't sure which was true. "Would that be a loss?" she said. "If she couldn't finish."

"The process will determine whether, "

"Garrett."

He stopped.

She did not say anything else. She just looked at him, the way she looked at him when she had already heard the answer and was waiting for him to catch up to it. It was not an unkind look. It was the look of someone who has lived with a person long enough to know the shape of what they're leaving out.

He drank his wine.

Outside, a car passed on the street, its headlights sweeping briefly across the kitchen ceiling. The pan on the stove ticked as it cooled.

"Yes," he said. "It would be a loss."

She nodded, once, and got up and went back to the stove and moved the pan back onto the low burner. "Ten more minutes," she said, and turned the baking show off.

They ate at the table with the placemats and the cloth napkins and the rest of the Viognier, and he told her about the drive down to Charles County, the flat light over the Patuxent, the cormorant on the Severn on the way back. He told her about the site: the low mound rising out of the winter field, the stratigraphy profile that had made him drive two hours instead of mailing a letter, the way Tayak Moyaone had stood with her arms at her sides while he explained the Section 9(c) request, not moving, not arguing, just listening with the particular stillness of someone filing everything away. He told her the notice was standard procedure, which it was. He told her the NAGPRA framework existed precisely for situations like this, which it did.

Carol ate and listened and did not ask anything else.

The omission sat on the table between the placemats, next to the salt cellar, taking up no space at all.

He had held the pen for forty seconds above the signature line. He had read Tayak Moyaone's sentence again: *Further excavation is essential to determining whether NAGPRA protections apply to this site.* He had signed anyway, because the statute required it, because Marsh had advised it, because the risk assessment had said *compliance risk* and *bad facts in the record*, because he was a Cultural Resources Program Manager and not a judge, and because the law did not have a provision for the forty seconds. He had signed with a Pilot G2, blue ink, the letters coming out even. He had put the pen down. He had not told Carol about the pen.

She refilled his glass without being asked.

"The Korean place," she said. "On the way home. Were they open?"

"I didn't look."

"We should go. Before it gets cold enough that we stop wanting to go out."

"We should," he said.

She smiled at him, a small sideways smile that meant she knew they probably wouldn't, not for a while, but that it was good to say it anyway. He smiled back.

He helped her clear the plates. He rinsed and she stacked the dishwasher, and they moved around each other in the kitchen the way they had moved around each other for twenty-six years, without looking, without needing to. The flour was still on her forearm. He didn't mention it.

Later, in bed, with the lamp off and Carol's breathing already slow and even beside him, he looked at the ceiling and thought about the notice sitting in the outgoing tray, and about Tayak Moyaone, who would receive her copy by end of week. He thought about the forty seconds. He thought about whether the forty seconds would matter: whether, when the letter arrived and the site went quiet and the clock ran down toward the bulldozers, his private hesitation would leave any mark at all on what happened next.

He did not know. That was the honest answer. He did not know whether a man's pause, unnamed and unfiled, counted for anything once the pen had moved.

He closed his eyes.

The notice would reach her Friday.
