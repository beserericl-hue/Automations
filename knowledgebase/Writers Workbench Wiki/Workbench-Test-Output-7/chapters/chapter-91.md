The cart had a bad wheel.

Marcus had noticed it on the sidewalk outside the parking garage on Twelfth Street, a leftward drag that required constant correction. He had noticed it again crossing Constitution Avenue. He noticed it now on the wide concrete apron in front of the Department of the Interior building, where the wheel caught on an expansion joint and the cart lurched and he grabbed the handle with both hands and stopped walking.

He stood there for a moment, holding it steady.

The building in front of him was the color of old bone, sandstone blocks stacked in the New Deal style, massive and unhurried, as if the federal government had decided once and for all what permanence looked like and poured it in place. The morning sun came off the stone at a low angle and made the facade look almost warm. Above the entrance, the words UNITED STATES DEPARTMENT OF THE INTERIOR were cut directly into the stone. No flourish. Just the name of the thing.

Marcus looked at the cart.

Four document boxes, each sealed with a strip of brown packing tape and labeled in Cassie's careful hand: PISCATAWAY TRIBAL NATION OF CHARLES COUNTY, FEDERAL ACKNOWLEDGMENT PETITION, BOX 1 OF 4. Box 2 of 4. Box 3 of 4. Box 4 of 4. Each label also carried the case number the OFA had assigned at the preliminary inquiry stage, printed from the email Cassie had framed and taped to the council room wall: OFA-MD-24-0117.

Fourteen hundred pages. He knew the number because he had counted the index entries himself, sitting at his kitchen table at eleven-thirty on a Tuesday night with a glass of water going warm beside him. The commission ruling was in Box 1, tabbed as Exhibit A. Nora's affidavit, the six typed pages plus her three handwritten lines at the bottom, was Exhibit B. Tayak's forty-three-page field report ran Exhibit C through Exhibit C-7, cross-referenced to Wren's field documentation, which ran Exhibit D through D-22 and included the stratigraphic drawings, the chain-of-custody logs for all recovered artifacts, and the Total Station survey data for the full site.

The rubber bands were still on the boxes. He had put them on himself, two per box, crosswise, at six this morning in his hotel room. He did not know why. The boxes were sealed. The rubber bands did nothing. He checked them anyway.

A woman in a gray coat walked past him toward the entrance, badge already out, and glanced at the cart with the mild curiosity of someone who had seen petition deliveries before and found them neither remarkable nor unwelcome. Marcus watched her go through the glass door and let it close behind her.

He had been standing here for four minutes. He had checked his phone twice.

The thing he could not stop thinking about was a drawer.

Not a specific drawer. He had never been inside this building. But he had assembled a composite drawer from every government office he had sat in over the past three years. The kind with a metal runner that needed a lift on the handle to close all the way. The kind where the files at the back went undisturbed for months because the ones at the front were always more urgent. He had seen Harkins's auto-reply bounce back from his email for six weeks running. He had watched the thirty-day commission clock tick down while a state liaison was at a conference in Baltimore. He understood, at a cellular level, how bureaucratic time worked. Not maliciously. Not with intent. But with the slow, inexorable patience of a system that had been here before you arrived and would be here after you left.

Fourteen hundred pages in a drawer was still fourteen hundred pages. He knew that. He told himself that.

He gripped the cart handle and started walking.

The entrance required a photo ID, a visitor log, and a temporary badge printed at the front desk, a paper rectangle in a plastic sleeve on a lanyard, his name spelled correctly, which he noted with mild surprise. The security officer, a broad man with a gray mustache and the settled demeanor of someone who had run this checkpoint for many years, directed Marcus to place his bag on the conveyor belt and walk through the magnetometer. The cart went around on a separate check. The boxes were opened, inspected, resealed with fresh tape from a dispenser the officer kept on the counter, and placed back on the cart without comment. The officer handed Marcus the cart handle as if returning a coat.

The lobby opened into a wide corridor with terrazzo floors the color of river clay. WPA murals ran along both walls, painted figures in earth tones, men and women working land and water, the kind of art that had been commissioned to say something about the country and had ended up saying something true by accident. Marcus walked past them without stopping. He had a nine o'clock appointment.

The OFA office was on the third floor. The elevator was slow. When it opened, Marcus pushed the cart out into a hallway that smelled of toner and the particular stale warmth of a building with sealed windows, and found the suite number he had written on his hand that morning because he did not trust himself to remember it.

The woman who met him at the door was younger than he expected. Early thirties, dark hair, a lanyard with her Interior badge clipped to the pocket of her blazer. She introduced herself as Jennifer Calls-from-the-Water, which she said without the slight self-consciousness Marcus had learned to watch for in people who expected him to comment on a name. She did not offer her hand, which he had also learned meant nothing, and she looked at the cart with the practiced eye of someone who had processed large submissions before.

"Mr. Redcloud," she said. "Let's get these inside."

The conference room was small and government-issue: a laminate table, four chairs, a Dell desktop on a side credenza with two monitors showing a screensaver of blue federal seals rotating slowly. A shared multi-function printer-scanner stood in the corner, its display lit green. On the table, someone had already placed a printed sheet, a checklist for preliminary completeness review.

Jennifer set the first box on the table and broke the tape with a letter opener.

"We'll verify the table of contents against the petition index," she said. "And I'll need to date-stamp the title page of each volume. After that, the submission goes into the tracking system and you'll receive a formal receipt letter within ten business days." She looked up. "You brought digital copies?"

Marcus reached into his bag and set two USB drives on the table. Cassie had labeled them with the case number in black marker, her handwriting smaller and neater than his own.

"Searchable PDFs," he said. "Full index. The stratigraphic drawings are in a separate folder, high-resolution."

Jennifer nodded and made a note on the checklist. She opened Box 1 to the first tabbed section and began reading the table of contents against her sheet, her finger moving down the page at a steady pace, neither hurried nor slow. Marcus sat in the chair across from her and put his hands flat on the table and watched.

The commission ruling was on top. Twelve pages, the state seal on the cover, the language he had read so many times he could recite the operative paragraph from memory. Below it, separated by a yellow tab marked EXHIBIT B, was Nora's affidavit. The six typed pages from Ida's 1962 document, the manual typewriter keys slightly uneven, the letter *e* riding a fraction high throughout, and then Nora's three lines in blue ballpoint, added below Ida's signature block in the summer of this year. Marcus had read those three lines in Nora's kitchen while she sat across from him with her hands around a coffee mug, watching him read.

*I have held this name unchanged since Ida gave it to me. I will say it in person, at the base layer, when the time comes. It is not for paper.*

He had not said anything when he finished reading. Neither had she. After a moment she had refilled his coffee without asking.

Jennifer turned to Exhibit C and ran her finger down the cross-reference list for Tayak's field report. Her expression was neutral and attentive, the face of someone doing careful work without editorial comment. Marcus watched her and felt, for the first time that morning, something that was not dread.

She was reading it. That was the thing. She was actually reading it.

He had spent three years imagining this moment as a transaction. Paper handed over, paper received, a stamp applied, a drawer opened. He had not imagined someone sitting across a table and reading the table of contents with the same attention she would give to anything that mattered. It did not resolve anything. It did not guarantee anything. But it was not nothing.

"The Wren Calvert field documentation," Jennifer said, without looking up. "D through D-22. Is the stratigraphic column for Stratum Seven included? I'm seeing a reference in the narrative but I want to confirm the exhibit is present."

Marcus opened Box 3 and found the tab. "D-19," he said. "The column drawing and the Total Station survey overlay."

She found it, checked it against the list, made a mark. "Good."

They worked through the remaining boxes in forty minutes. Jennifer date-stamped the title page of each volume with a red stamp that left a clean impression, the date, the OFA case number, the words RECEIVED BY OFA. She affixed a bar-coded records label to the spine of each binder and scanned them with a handheld reader that beeped once for each label, a small, clean sound in the quiet room.

When she finished, she set her pen down and looked at Marcus directly for the first time since they had begun.

"Mr. Redcloud, I want to be straightforward with you about timing," she said. "Our current caseload puts estimated processing at eighteen months to two years from the date of receipt before active consideration begins. That's per the Part 83 regulations and our current queue. I can't give you a firmer number than that."

Marcus looked at the four boxes stacked on the cart, labeled and stamped, the bar-coded labels catching the fluorescent light.

"Eighteen months," he said.

"At minimum. Possibly two years."

He nodded slowly. Outside the sealed window, a pigeon landed on the sandstone ledge and stood there with its chest out, looking at nothing in particular, and then flew away.

"We've been waiting since 1697," Marcus said. "We can wait two more years."

He had meant it to come out measured, maybe even wry. Instead it came out plain, the way a fact comes out when you have lived with it long enough that it stops needing emphasis. Jennifer Calls-from-the-Water looked at him for a moment with an expression he could not entirely read. Not pity, not discomfort. Something more like recognition. Then she wrote something on her checklist and capped her pen.

"The receipt letter will come to the address on file," she said. "And the petition will be placed in the queue for active consideration after preliminary completeness review. If there are any deficiencies, we'll contact you within sixty days."

"There won't be deficiencies," Marcus said. Not boasting. Just another fact.

She almost smiled. "No," she said. "I don't think there will be."

He loaded the empty cart back onto the elevator. The boxes stayed. He had known they would stay. That was the point, the entire point. But he stood in the elevator for a moment after the doors closed and felt the absence of them, the way you feel the absence of something you have been carrying for a long time and have finally set down.

The terrazzo floor of the lobby caught his footsteps and gave them back to him. He walked past the WPA murals, the painted figures working their painted land, and through the glass door and out into the October morning, where the sun had moved off the sandstone facade and the building was its ordinary bone color again, and the bad-wheeled cart rattled beside him on the concrete apron, pulling left.

He left the cart at the security desk. He did not need it anymore.

On C Street the wind came off the Mall in a long, cold push that smelled of cut grass and exhaust and the particular mineral smell of the city in autumn. Marcus stood on the sidewalk with his hands in his coat pockets and looked back at the building for a moment, its rows of tall windows, its New Deal solidity, its absolute indifference, and then he turned and walked toward the Metro.

He had a flight at six. He had four hours. He bought a coffee from a cart near the Constitution Avenue entrance, black, in a paper cup that burned his fingers through the sleeve, and he sat on a bench near the reflecting pool and drank it and did not think about anything in particular. The water in the pool was very still. Two joggers passed. A woman with a stroller stopped to look at her phone, then moved on.

He thought about Tayak standing in the trench at the base layer with the rain coming down, the copper pendant in the tray, Nora's affidavit in her hands. He had not been there for that. He had been in Annapolis, in a parking garage, on the phone with Ashby. But Cassie had sent him a photograph. Tayak's face in profile, the trench wall behind her, the light the flat grey of a wet October afternoon. He had looked at it for a long time before putting his phone away.

He thought about Nora's kitchen. The coffee mug. The blue ballpoint lines. *It is not for paper.*

He finished the coffee and dropped the cup in a trash can and stood up.

He called Tayak from the bench before he moved.

She picked up on the second ring.

"It's done," he said.

A pause. He could hear wind through the phone, or something like wind. The site, maybe. Or her car.

"Case number," she said.

He read it from the photograph he'd taken of the receipt letter: the petitioner designation, the OFA tracking code, the date stamp in the upper right corner. He read it twice, slowly, the way you read a measurement back to confirm it.

"Got it," she said.

Neither of them spoke for a moment.

"Good," she said.

The word was flat and complete, the way she said most things. He had learned, in the months since Annapolis, that *good* from Tayak was not a small word. It was the word she used when something had been finished that she had not been certain could be finished.

"How's the mound," he said.

"Closed."

He waited. She didn't add anything.

He thought about asking what that meant, the tarp, the backfill, the sequence of steps that turned an open excavation into a protected site. But he knew what it meant, and she knew he knew, and neither of them needed to say it out loud. The mound was closed. The petition was filed. The case number existed in a federal database in a building on C Street NW. These were the facts. What they meant would take longer than either of them had right now.

"Eighteen months," he said. "Maybe two years."

"I know."

"Jennifer Calls-from-the-Water said she'd send the preliminary completeness review in sixty days."

"Okay."

"I'll forward it when it comes."

"Okay," Tayak said again.

He could hear her breathing. He thought about Cassie's photograph, Tayak's face in profile, the trench wall behind her, the flat grey light. He had not been there. He had been in a parking garage in Annapolis on the phone with Ashby, doing the thing that needed doing at the same time she was doing the other thing that needed doing. That was how it had worked. The two of them in different places doing different parts of the same thing for months now, and it had worked, and the mound was closed, and the petition was filed, and the case number was in her phone.

"Get some sleep," he said.

A pause. "You too."

He hung up.

He sat with the phone in his hand.

He thought about 1697. He thought about Saya standing in a colonial assembly with ink drying on her palm. He thought about Okwi wrapping the petition in oilskin and pressing it into the earth at the base of the mound, two copies, because two forms of keeping were better than one. He thought about Nora's kitchen, the blue ballpoint lines, the careful handwriting that said *it is not for paper.* He thought about the binders on the cart, the bar-coded labels on their spines, the date stamp on the receipt letter, the number he had read to Tayak twice so she would have it right.

Two forms of keeping.

He stood up, pocketed his phone, and walked toward the Metro.

The gate café at Reagan National smelled of burnt coffee and the particular staleness of recirculated air. Marcus found a stool at the counter, ordered a turkey sandwich, and opened his phone to buy the ticket home.

The flight was three hours and forty minutes. It left at 6:15. He had forty-two minutes.

He bought the ticket. He ate the sandwich. He did not taste it.

Outside the window, a 737 was being pushed back from the adjacent gate, its wing lights blinking in the grey November afternoon. The ground crew moved in the efficient, unhurried way of people who have done this ten thousand times and expect to do it ten thousand more. Marcus watched them and thought about Pruitt.

Not the way he had been thinking about Pruitt for the past eight months. The sick, circling thought that came at 2 a.m. and would not resolve. This was different. This was the way you think about a calculation you got wrong and then corrected: with clarity, and without mercy, and without the luxury of pretending it had been someone else's hand on the pen.

He had signed the MOU in a conference room on Bladen Street in Annapolis. Gwen Ashcraft had laid it across the table in a manila folder. Farrow had said something about partnership, about the tribe's future, about heritage tourism as a form of sovereignty. Marcus had heard all of it and understood all of it and signed it anyway, because sixty thousand dollars was sixty thousand dollars and the federal petition was stalled and the council was tired. He had told himself that the consultation clause was a formality, a hedge, the kind of language lawyers put in documents to make both sides feel protected.

He had told himself that for six months.

He had not told the council.

He put the last quarter of the sandwich down. He had told himself many things during those six months. That the clause would never activate. That Pruitt's people were genuinely interested in heritage tourism and not in the quiet title inquiry Lester Pruitt was running in parallel at Holloway and Breck. That the alternate road alignment, the one that would have cleared the site entirely, was a real offer and not a positioning move. That he could manage it. That managing it was the same as solving it.

The 737 completed its pushback. A baggage cart threaded between two ground vehicles with the casual precision of long practice.

What he had not told himself, and what he understood now with the fluorescent lights of the gate café pressing down on him and the sandwich sitting in his stomach like wet newspaper, was that he had been afraid. Not of Pruitt. Of the council. Of standing in that room and saying: *I signed something I should not have signed, and I did not tell you, and I am telling you now.* The fear had been so ordinary and so total that he had dressed it up as strategy, as patience, as waiting for the right moment, until Cassie's text had arrived at 11:30 p.m. and the right moment had already passed and there was only what he did next.

He had stayed. That was the thing he kept coming back to.

He had stood in the council room and said it plainly. He had given them the MOU and the clause and the February breach and the Ashby analysis and all of it, the whole shape of what he had done and what it had cost and what it might still cost, and he had not tried to frame it or soften it or offer them a resolution before he gave them the problem. He had just told them. And then he had stayed in the room and let them respond.

Darius had been furious. Eleanor had been quiet in the way that was worse than fury. Cassie had read the MOU in the time it took him to pour a glass of water and looked at him with an expression he would not describe to anyone.

He had stayed.

And then he had testified. And then he had carried the petition to Washington. And now the binders were in a federal building on C Street NW with a bar-coded label on each spine and a case number in Tayak's phone, and the mound was closed, and he was sitting in an airport café eating a sandwich he couldn't taste, and the gate agent was announcing pre-boarding for the 6:15 to Baltimore-Washington.

He gathered his bag. He threw away the rest of the sandwich.

The plane was a 737. He had a window seat over the wing. He put his bag in the overhead bin. He sat down. He buckled his seatbelt.

The man in the middle seat was already asleep before the door closed, head tilted back, mouth slightly open, the total unconsciousness of someone who had made this flight many times and had nothing left to prove to it. The woman on the aisle had noise-canceling headphones and a laptop open to a spreadsheet. Neither of them acknowledged Marcus. This was fine. This was correct.

He looked out the window at the dark tarmac and the wing lights and the ground crew moving in the dusk with their orange wands, and he thought: *We've been waiting since 1697.*

He had said it to Jennifer Calls-from-the-Water and he had meant it, and it had come out of him without calculation, which was how he knew it was true. Not a rhetorical move. Not the kind of thing you say to a federal officer to make her feel the weight of the moment. Just the fact of it, which he had known abstractly for years and which had become, sometime in the last eight months, a fact he carried in his body rather than his head.

Saya standing in the colonial assembly. Okwi pressing the petition into the earth in oilskin, two copies, because two forms of keeping were better than one. Nora at eighty-two, holding a name in her mouth for sixty-two years because the time had not yet been right to say it. And now the binders in the federal building, the case number, the eighteen months or two years or however long it would take, and him on a plane home.

He had not destroyed it. He had come close. Close enough that he would not pretend otherwise to himself or to anyone who asked directly. But he had not destroyed it.

The cabin lights dimmed for takeoff. The engines rose in pitch. He felt the runway move under him, the slight forward press of acceleration, and then the lift, that moment when the ground releases you and there is nothing to do but go.

He looked out the window. The Potomac was visible below, catching the last of the evening light, a broad silver-grey curve through the dark land. He had driven along it a hundred times. He had never looked at it from above before and understood it as a road.

The river moved south. He watched it until the cloud cover closed below them and there was nothing to see but grey.

He did not intend to sleep. He was aware of that clearly, the way you are aware of things in the last few seconds before awareness stops mattering.

He slept.

Somewhere over the Chesapeake, he slept without dreaming, without the 2 a.m. inventory of what he had done and what it had cost, without Pruitt and without Ashby and without the MOU and without the council room and without any of it. Just the hum of the engines and the cool air and the dark, and the seatbelt across his hips, and the small irregular pressure of the window against his shoulder, and the long, unguarded rest of a man who has carried something as far as he can carry it and has, for now, set it down.

He would not know he had passed over the Chesapeake until later, when he looked at the flight map on his phone and saw the route arcing northeast over the Bay.

The descent woke him.

Not the announcement. He heard that afterward, the flight attendant's voice coming through the cabin speakers with the standard phrases about electronic devices and seat backs. What woke him was the pressure change, the slow equalization in his ears that registered as a kind of pulling, a drawing back toward the earth. He swallowed. He blinked. The cabin lights were still dimmed. The woman on the aisle had closed her laptop. The man in the middle seat had not moved.

Marcus turned to the window.

Below the wing, through a break in the cloud cover, he could see lights. Not the dense grid of the city, that was north, the orange wash of D.C. spreading toward the horizon. These were the scattered, sparse lights of the counties south of the Bay. Charles County. St. Mary's. The dark stretches between them where there were no lights at all, which was where the farms were, and the creek bottoms, and the high ground above Mattawoman Creek where the mound sat under its closed tarp in the November dark.

He looked at the lights for a long time.

He thought about Nora.

Eighty-two years old. Living on Billingsley Road eight miles from the site, in a house where a quilt hung on a frame in the front room with a back panel that held a notation system two thousand years old. She had held a name in her mouth for sixty-two years. She had testified in front of the commission and not looked away from anyone in the room. She had pressed her palms flat to the earth at the mound's base layer and said the name aloud, and Tayak had heard it, and the chain had not broken.

He did not know if she would live to see the ruling.

He did not know if he would.

He reached into his jacket pocket and found the receipt. A single folded sheet, government letterhead, the seal of the Department of the Interior at the top and below it the case number, *83-3847-PIS-MD*, and the petition name, *Piscataway People of Charles County, Maryland*, and the date-stamp in blue ink, *November 14, 2024*, and Jennifer Calls-from-the-Water's signature at the bottom, neat and unhurried, the signature of a woman who had signed many of these and understood what each one meant and did not perform the weight of it.

He did the arithmetic again.

Sixty days for the preliminary completeness review. Then placement on the ready-for-active-consideration register. Then the queue. Eighteen months minimum from placement. Two years likely. Possibly longer, depending on the OFA's workload, depending on whether the petition raised threshold questions requiring additional documentation, depending on things he could not control and would not be able to anticipate.

Two years.

Nora had held the name for sixty-two. Saya had pressed her handprint into the colonial assembly's record in 1697 and the petition had gone nowhere and she had died before the century turned. Okwi had buried the oilskin copies in the mound because two forms of keeping were better than one, and the mound had held them, and Tayak had found them, and they were now in the binders in the federal building with a bar-coded records label and a case number.

*We've been waiting since 1697.*

He folded the receipt and put it back in his pocket.

The plane broke through the lower cloud layer and BWI was below them, the runway lights in their long parallel lines, the red and white approach indicators, the dark Maryland land on all sides pressing close to the airport's edge. He could see the highway interchange to the north, the parking structures, the terminal building with its lit windows. He could see a single plane on the adjacent runway, its wing lights blinking, waiting.

The wheels came down with their mechanical clunk and the airspeed changed and the ground rose to meet them and then there was the jolt of contact, the roar of thrust reversers, the long deceleration that pressed him forward against his seatbelt, and the runway lights streaming past in the window, and then the slower taxi, the ordinary world of ground vehicles and yellow line markings and a gate agent visible through the terminal glass, checking something on a tablet.

He had not destroyed it.

He would need to say that to himself a few more times before he stopped needing to say it. That was all right. He had learned, in the last eight months, to be honest about the things he still needed.

The seatbelt sign went off with its chime. Around him people stood immediately, pulling bags from the overhead bins with the focused efficiency of people who had somewhere to be. The man in the middle seat woke with a start, looked around, and began gathering himself. The woman on the aisle was already standing, laptop bag on her shoulder, headphones around her neck.

Marcus waited. He always waited. There was no point in standing in the aisle when the door was still closed.

He looked out the window at the jet bridge connecting to the fuselage. Through the gap between the bridge and the terminal wall he could see a strip of Maryland sky, dark blue going black, the last of the evening. Somewhere under that sky the mound sat on its high ground above the creek, the tarp weighted at its corners, the base layer sealed, the copper pendant in the conservation suite in College Park, the binders in the federal building with the case number on their spines.

*83-3847-PIS-MD.*

He would call Nora in the morning. He would let Darius and Cassie and Eleanor read the preliminary receipt and ask their questions, and he would answer them, and then they would all settle into the long patience of waiting for something that could not be hurried.
