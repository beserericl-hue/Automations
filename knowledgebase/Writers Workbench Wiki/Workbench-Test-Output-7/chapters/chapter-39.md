The call ended at 8:47 p.m.

Marcus knew because he looked at his phone the moment Tayak's voice cut off. Not to check the time. His hands needed something to do, and picking up the phone was the first thing they found. He set it face-down on the desk. The screen lit up once from underneath, then went dark.

He sat without moving. The tribal office was closed. He was home, in the back room he used as a study, and the house was quiet in the particular way it got on evenings when he had not turned on the television or the radio. The refrigerator's hum from the kitchen was the only sound, low and steady, like something breathing in its sleep.

Tayak had called from the parking lot outside Voss's building. He could hear the wind in the line, the occasional car. She wanted legal advice. She had asked him, specifically, about contractual language in heritage-site designations, whether a protected-site status could trigger obligations to third parties, and he had said the right things, careful things, things that were true but incomplete. She had noticed. He could tell by the pause before she said *okay.* Not the pause of someone satisfied. The pause of someone filing a discrepancy away for later.

He was good at reading pauses. He had been chair for six years, and reading what people were not saying was most of the job.

He opened the bottom drawer of his desk.

It was a deep drawer, the kind built for hanging files, and it locked with a small brass key he kept on the same ring as his car key. He had locked it not because anyone was likely to go through his desk. He lived alone, had lived alone for three years. The act of locking it had felt like a decision, at the time. A way of marking the thing as separate from the ordinary papers of his life.

The folder was at the back, behind a hanging file of grant correspondence and another of OFA submission drafts, the folders worn soft at the edges from handling. The one he wanted was manila, thicker than the others, with a printed adhesive label on the tab: *MOU_CHD_FINAL.* He had typed the label himself, which now struck him as absurd. As if giving it a tidy name had made it a manageable thing.

He set it on the desk.

The bourbon was already poured. He had done that before the call, when the evening had still seemed like an ordinary evening, when he had imagined sitting with it and reading through the OFA draft one more time. The glass stood at the corner of the desk, two fingers of amber catching the light from the lamp. He did not touch it.

The folder sat in front of him. He did not open it.

The document inside was six pages. He knew every clause. He had read it twelve times in the first month after signing, then less, then not at all for the past four months. That was its own kind of decision: the decision to let the thing exist without looking at it directly, the way you can live with a crack in a wall if you stop walking past it.

Chesapeake Heritage Development LLC. Registered in Maryland, with a registered agent at a suite in the Dobbin Center office park in Columbia that he had looked up once and confirmed was a registered-agent service. The kind of address that meant the company existed on paper in a room it had never occupied. The principal was a man named Garrett Pruitt, who wore good shoes and spoke carefully about *authentic visitor experience* and *curated storytelling* and the economic modeling his firm had commissioned, which projected four million in annual tourist spending and forty-seven jobs within three years of the site opening. Marcus had sat across from him in a conference room in La Plata, in January of 2023, and had thought: this is what it looks like when someone wants something from you and has learned to dress it as generosity.

He had signed anyway.

The recognition petition had been eleven years in the making by then. Eleven years of historical research, genealogical documentation, legal filings, expert reports. The OFA process was a machinery that consumed paper and money and time at a rate that outpaced every grant cycle, every crowdfunding push, every appeal to the state humanities council. They were short. They had been short for two years. The petition was close, genuinely close, the closest it had been, and they were forty thousand dollars short of the final legal review that would bring it to the submission threshold.

Pruitt's firm had offered sixty thousand. Unrestricted, he had said. A contribution to the tribe's recognition effort, with no conditions on the petition itself. The only agreement was the MOU. A memorandum of understanding, not a contract, Pruitt had said, as if the distinction were meaningful. The tribe would grant Chesapeake Heritage Development a right of first consultation on any heritage-tourism development associated with any site the tribe held, managed, or identified as a Traditional Cultural Property, in the event of formal federal or state recognition.

*Right of first consultation.* The phrase was in Section 4, under the heading *Preferred Partner Status.* Marcus had read it and understood it and told himself it was narrow. Consultation, not control. He had told himself that if recognition came and the mound became a protected site, he would find a way to negotiate the clause down, to limit its scope, to make it mean less than it said.

He had not told the council.

He had told himself he would, once the petition cleared. Once there was good news to frame it in. He had told himself that every week for eighteen months, and the weeks had passed, and the petition had moved forward, and the mound had turned into something none of them had anticipated. Not a theoretical TCP in a recognition filing but an active excavation, a legal fight, a news story, a thing that Tayak was standing in the middle of with her hands in the ground and her name on the permit. The right-of-first-consultation clause had moved from a theoretical problem to a real one, the way a crack in a wall moves from cosmetic to structural without announcing the transition.

If the mound was designated a protected site, and it would be, Tayak would make sure of it, she was the most methodical person he had ever worked with, she would make it happen, then Chesapeake Heritage Development LLC had a contractual foothold in the tribe's most sacred ground. The ground the petition was built on. The ground where Tayak was digging up the dead.

He looked at the folder.

The desk lamp threw a yellow circle across it. The room smelled of old paper and toner dust, and faintly underneath, the bourbon in the glass. Outside, a car passed on the road, headlights sweeping briefly across the window blind, gone.

He thought about Tayak's pause.

He thought about the way she had asked the question, *contractual obligations to third parties,* with that careful precision she used when she already suspected the answer and was checking whether he would give it to her straight. She had learned that precision from the work, from eleven years of watching the state find procedural ways to deny what was plainly true. She used it the way a surveyor uses a plumb line. Not to wound. To locate.

He had not given her the answer she needed.

He put his hand on the folder. The paper was slightly cool under his palm, the manila surface smooth except where the adhesive label sat, slightly raised. *MOU_CHD_FINAL* in twelve-point Arial. A tidy name for a thing that was not tidy.

He did not open it.

Not yet. He sat with his hand on it and the bourbon untouched and the refrigerator breathing in the kitchen, and he thought about George Swann, his predecessor, who had sent him a sealed envelope three days after the election with a note that said *you'll know when you need it.* He had never opened it. He had put it in the same drawer as this folder, behind the grant correspondence, and he had not opened it because he had told himself he would know when the time came and he would be ready.

He was not ready.

He was a man sitting alone in a room with a document he had signed and not disclosed, and the woman who needed to know about it had just called him from a parking lot in the dark, and he had told her careful, incomplete things, and she had filed the discrepancy away.

He picked up the glass. He set it down without drinking.

Then he opened the folder.

The first page was what it always was: the header, centered and bold, the developer's full legal name in capitals. CHESAPEAKE HERITAGE DEVELOPMENT LLC. Followed by its Maryland registration address, a suite number at the Dobbin Center in Columbia. Below that, the title. MEMORANDUM OF UNDERSTANDING. Below that, the opening recital in twelve-point Times New Roman, the margins exactly one inch on all sides, the document as clean and neutral as a hospital corridor.

*This Memorandum of Understanding ("MOU") is entered into as of the 14th day of November, 2022, by and between Chesapeake Heritage Development LLC, a Maryland limited liability company ("LLC"), and the Piscataway Conoy Tribal Council, acting by and through its duly authorized representative ("Tribe"). This MOU shall become effective on the date last signed by the parties hereto.*

November 14. He had not thought about the date in months. It was the same date he had given Jonah Twobirds at the council meeting, the November 14 deadline, the recognition milestone, the date he had offered as a commitment with a straight face while this document sat in the drawer behind him. He had not planned that. It had come out of him automatically, the way a practiced lie does, not because he intended to deceive but because the number was already in his head, already associated with the petition, and he had reached for it without thinking about what else it was attached to.

Both parties had signed on November 14, 2022. The DocuSign timestamp at the bottom of the signature page confirmed it: *Signed November 14, 2022, 4:43 PM EST.* There was no separate effective date. The signing date and the effective date were the same, because both parties had executed on the same day, and the recital language said *effective on the date last signed.* He had understood this when he signed. He had simply stopped thinking about it.

He turned to page two.

Section 1, *Purpose.* The language was smooth. It described the MOU as a framework for collaboration between the LLC and the Tribe to advance *heritage preservation, cultural interpretation, and sustainable tourism development* in the Chesapeake watershed. It said the parties shared a commitment to *authentic representation of Indigenous history and lifeways* and to *the responsible stewardship of Traditional Cultural Properties.* It said the LLC recognized the Tribe's *unique cultural authority* over such properties and sought to *partner with, not supplant, tribal leadership* in any interpretive programming.

Marcus had read these words eighteen months ago and found them reassuring. He read them now and heard Pruitt's voice behind them. A man who had learned the vocabulary of Indigenous rights the way a salesman learns the vocabulary of whatever he is selling. *Authentic representation. Unique cultural authority.* The words were correct. They meant nothing.

He had met Pruitt twice. The first time at a conference in Annapolis, a heritage-tourism symposium where Marcus had presented the tribe's recognition timeline to a room of state agency people and developers who took notes and asked careful questions and then went to lunch. Pruitt had found him at the coffee station afterward. A compact man in his late forties, gray at the temples, with the kind of unhurried confidence that comes from never having needed to hurry. He had introduced himself as a principal of Chesapeake Heritage Development and said he had followed the tribe's OFA petition with interest. He had used the phrase *OFA petition* correctly, which meant he had done his homework. He had given Marcus a card and said he would be in touch.

The second meeting was in this room. Marcus had invited him here because he had not wanted the conversation in a tribal office, had not wanted anyone to see it. That was the first sign that he had already made up his mind.

He turned to page three.

Section 2, *Scope.* This was where the geography lived. The MOU defined a *heritage corridor* as the area encompassing any parcel of land the Tribe held, managed, identified in its OFA petition as a Traditional Cultural Property, or subsequently designated as a TCP under applicable federal or state review processes. The corridor was defined not by coordinates but by category. It moved wherever the petition moved. It expanded as the tribe's claims expanded.

He had read this clause in November 2022 and told himself it was broad language, the kind of thing lawyers put in to cover contingencies. He had told himself it would narrow at the implementation stage, that the right-of-first-consultation provision in Section 4 was the operative clause and that *consultation* was not *control.*

He turned to Section 4.

*Preferred Partner Status. In the event any parcel within the designated heritage corridor achieves protected status under state or federal statute, including but not limited to designation as a Traditional Cultural Property, listing on the National Register of Historic Places, or protection under applicable provisions of NAGPRA or the National Historic Preservation Act, LLC retains right of first consultation on all commercial development, interpretation, and public access programming associated with or adjacent to such parcel. The Tribe agrees not to enter into agreements with any third party for such purposes without first providing LLC with a period of not less than sixty (60) days to propose terms. This right shall survive termination of this MOU and shall run with any formal recognition or protected designation achieved during the term or within five (5) years thereafter.*

He stopped at *shall survive termination.*

He had read that phrase in November 2022. He had understood what it meant. He had initialed the page anyway. His initials were there, *M.R.,* in blue ballpoint, bottom right corner, slightly cramped the way his handwriting always was when he was trying to look unhurried. He had told himself that survival clauses were boilerplate. He had told himself the clause would never activate because recognition was still years away and the mound was a theoretical TCP in a filing, not an active site, not a thing anyone was standing in.

He set the page down.

The refrigerator cycled off in the kitchen and the house went quiet. Outside, nothing moved. The desk lamp made its yellow circle and everything beyond it was dark.

He thought about the sixty thousand dollars. He thought about where it had gone: twenty-two thousand to the historical research firm in Annapolis that had spent four months pulling county records and genealogical documentation for the petition's community-history section; fourteen thousand to the legal review that had gotten the petition past the OFA's first threshold review; nine thousand to the expert reports, the linguist, the ethnohistorian, whose letters had been the difference between a petition the OFA reviewer had set aside and one that had moved to active consideration status; the remaining fifteen thousand into the tribal general fund, which had been running on fumes. That was the thing Marcus had not said to Pruitt but which Pruitt had understood, because Pruitt had done his homework and knew that a tribe forty thousand dollars short of a petition threshold was a tribe that would consider options it would not otherwise consider.

He had not told the council. He had told himself he would tell them once the petition cleared the next review stage, that he would frame it as a funding solution that had worked, that he would show them the petition progress and the MOU together and the council would see that the trade had been worth it. He had been telling himself that for eighteen months. The petition had moved. The mound had appeared. Tayak had started digging. Slade had filed the Section 106 review. Voss had inserted himself. And Marcus had kept the folder in the drawer and told himself the right moment had not come yet.

He looked at the signature page.

His name was printed below the signature line: *Marcus Redcloud, Tribal Chair, Piscataway Conoy Tribal Council.* Above it, his signature, the real one, not the initialed abbreviation but the full signature he used for official documents, the one that looked like authority. Beside it, Pruitt's signature, and below that: *Duly authorized to sign on behalf of Chesapeake Heritage Development LLC.*

The DocuSign timestamp sat at the bottom of the page in a gray box: *Signed November 14, 2022, 4:43 PM EST.* The certification number was a long string of characters that meant the document was legally executed and timestamped and that there was a record of it in a server somewhere that neither he nor Pruitt controlled.

He had thought about that server, occasionally, in the months since. He had thought about what it would take for someone to find this document. A FOIA request to the wrong agency would not reach it. The MOU was between private parties. A search of Maryland LLC records would surface Chesapeake Heritage Development's registration but not its contracts. The document existed in Pruitt's files, in Marcus's drawer, and in DocuSign's servers, and in the email he had sent to himself at his personal Gmail account the night he signed it, subject line *Fully executed MOU, Chesapeake Heritage Development,* which he had never deleted because he had been afraid that deleting it would feel like hiding something. That logic had not prevented him from hiding the thing itself.

He put the pages back in order. He closed the folder.

He picked up his phone. Tayak's name was still on the recent-calls screen, the timestamp reading 8:47 PM, forty minutes ago. He looked at it for a moment. Then he set the phone face-down on the desk.

He could call her. He could tell her now, on the phone, in the dark, while she was still in that parking lot or driving home or wherever she had gone after he had given her careful, incomplete answers. He could say: there is a document you need to know about. I signed it eighteen months ago. I did not tell the council. If the mound is designated a protected site, and it will be, then a developer has a contractual claim to the heritage programming. The mound is in the corridor. I am sorry.

He could say all of that. He did not pick up the phone.

What stopped him was not cowardice, or not only cowardice. It was the calculation he had been making for eighteen months, the one he could not stop making even now. If he told Tayak tonight, she would tell the council. If she told the council, the MOU became a crisis, not a problem to be managed but a public fracture, a thing that Slade or Voss or whoever was watching the excavation most closely could use to argue that the tribe's leadership had compromised its own claim. The petition was at active consideration. If the OFA reviewer saw a news story, and Kimi would make it a news story, Kimi made everything a news story, about the tribal chair signing a secret development agreement over the site at the center of the recognition petition, the petition would stall. It might not recover. Eleven years of work. His predecessor's work, and his predecessor's predecessor's, and Tayak's fieldwork, and Nora's testimony, and Delia Swann's twenty years on the council. All of it running through a pipe that he had, eighteen months ago, partially handed to a man in a Columbia office park.

He opened the desk drawer. Behind the folder, in the back, was the sealed manila envelope from George Swann. His name on the front in George's handwriting, the flap sealed with packing tape. *You'll know when you need it.*

He looked at it for a long moment. Then he closed the drawer without taking it out.

He needed to find the clause. Not Section 4. He knew Section 4. He needed the termination provision, the scope definition, the definition of *heritage corridor.* He needed to know whether the mound's current status, active excavation under a state permit, Section 106 review pending, no protected designation yet, fell within the corridor as defined, or whether the clause activated only on formal designation. If designation was the trigger, he had time. If the corridor definition was broad enough to capture an active Section 106 review site, he might already be in breach of the exclusivity provision simply by virtue of Tayak's permit existing.

He opened the folder again. He turned to Section 2.

He read it slowly, the way he had not read it eighteen months ago, when Pruitt's sixty thousand dollars had been a solution and the mound had been a line in a filing and the word *corridor* had sounded like a metaphor rather than a legal instrument.

*…any parcel of land the Tribe held, managed, identified in its OFA petition as a Traditional Cultural Property, or subsequently designated as a TCP under applicable federal or state review processes.*

*Subsequently designated.* The mound was not yet designated. It was under Section 106 review, which was a process that could lead to designation but was not itself a designation. The Section 106 review and a TCP designation were separate things under the National Historic Preservation Act. There was a difference. He was nearly certain there was a difference.

He needed a lawyer. Not Tayak. Tayak was inside the problem. He needed someone outside, someone who could read this document and tell him whether the gap he was looking at was real or whether he was a man finding comfort in punctuation.

He thought about Jonah Twobirds, with his law school application on the kitchen table. He thought about Cassie Threatt, who had a master's in public policy from Georgetown and who would read this document in thirty seconds and understand exactly what it meant and exactly what he had done. He thought about Delia Swann, who had been on the council for twenty years and who had voted no on the excavation and who would look at this folder and feel confirmed in every reservation she had ever expressed about Marcus's judgment.

He sat with the folder open in the lamplight. The bourbon was still in the glass, the ice long melted, the surface flat and still.

His phone buzzed. He turned it over. A text from Kimi: *Did you see the Voss statement? He's positioning himself as the institutional contact for the site. If he gets that role before Tayak pushes back*

He turned the phone face-down again.

He looked at the clause. *Shall survive termination.* Five years. If the tribe achieved recognition in the next five years, and it would, they were close, they were genuinely close, the clause would be active. Pruitt would have his sixty days. Pruitt would propose terms. And Marcus would have to bring those terms to a council that did not know the MOU existed, and explain to Delia Swann and Eleanor Runningwater and Harold Swann and Jonah Twobirds, who had stood in this building and pressed him for a firm commitment and accepted it, that he had signed something he had not disclosed, that he had funded the petition's final push with money that had a string attached, that the string ran from the tribe's most sacred ground to a registered-agent address in a Columbia office park.

He closed the folder. He put it back in the drawer. He left the bourbon on the desk.

He turned off the lamp and sat in the dark for a moment, the room reduced to the faint orange glow of the streetlight through the blind, the refrigerator starting up again in the kitchen, the sound of the road outside, ordinary and indifferent.

Then he picked up his phone and opened his contacts and scrolled to a name he had not called in eight months: Robert Ashby, an attorney in Annapolis who had done contract review for the tribe twice before and who was discreet, who understood tribal governance, who would not ask why Marcus was calling at nine-thirty on a weeknight with a question about an MOU he had not disclosed to his own council.

He stared at the name. He did not press call.

He thought: *tomorrow.* He had been thinking *tomorrow* for eighteen months. But Tayak had called tonight, and Voss was moving, and Kimi had sent a text, and the document in the drawer had a survival clause and a five-year window and a sixty-thousand-dollar origin story, and tomorrow the excavation would still be happening, and the Section 106 review would still be pending, and Chesapeake Heritage Development LLC would still be registered in Maryland with a right that Marcus had signed over in a room he had kept dark.

He pressed call.

It rang four times. Ashby's voicemail picked up, a brief professional recording, his name and number and the instruction to leave a message.

Marcus waited for the tone. Then he said: "Robert. It's Marcus Redcloud. I have a document I need you to look at. There's a consultation clause that may have activated, or may be about to. I need to know the exposure before I can decide how to move." He paused. "Call me when you can. It's not an emergency." He stopped. "It might be an emergency."

He ended the call. He set the phone on the desk. In the drawer, behind the MOU, George Swann's sealed envelope sat in the dark, unopened, waiting for a man who would know when he needed it.

Marcus was not sure, anymore, that man was him.

He did not sleep.

At two in the morning he got up, went to the kitchen, and made coffee he did not want. He stood at the counter while it brewed, watching the thin column of steam rise and flatten against the cabinet above. The streetlight came through the window over the sink and laid a pale stripe across the floor. He thought about Robert Ashby's voicemail and whether leaving it had been the right move or whether he had simply handed someone else a piece of the problem he had spent eighteen months keeping to himself.

He poured the coffee. He went back to the desk.

He opened the drawer and took out the folder again.

The MOU was nine pages. He had read it twice already tonight, but he had read it the way a man reads a document he already understands, looking for the familiar shape of the thing, confirming what he knew. Now he was going to read it the way Cassie Threatt would read it. Clause by clause, looking for the mechanism that would let him out.

He turned to the table of contents. Section 1, Purpose. Section 2, Scope. Section 3, Term. Section 4, Roles of the Parties. Section 5, Consultation Rights. Section 6, Exclusivity. Section 7, Marketing and Branding. Section 8, Termination. Section 9, Miscellaneous.

He went to Section 8.

*8.1, Termination for Convenience.* Either party may terminate this MOU upon sixty days' written notice to the other party. *8.2, Termination for Cause.* Either party may terminate immediately upon material breach by the other party, provided written notice of breach is delivered and the breaching party fails to cure within thirty days.

He had read this before and felt something loosen in his chest, because sixty days' written notice seemed manageable, seemed like a door he could open if he needed to. But he had also read Section 8.3, which came directly after, and which he had not let himself look at squarely until now.

*8.3, Survival of Rights. Notwithstanding termination under Sections 8.1 or 8.2, the Consultation Rights set forth in Section 5 shall survive termination and remain in full force and effect for a period of five years from the Effective Date, provided that the TCP Trigger Event has occurred prior to termination.*

There it was. He had terminated nothing. He had never tried to terminate anything. But even if he had, even if he walked to his printer right now and drafted a sixty-day notice and signed it and scanned it and emailed it to the registered agent in Columbia, the consultation right would survive. Five years from the effective date. The effective date was November 14, 2022, the date both parties had signed. Five years from that date was November 14, 2027. The tribe's OFA petition was pending. Recognition, if it came, would come before 2027. He knew it would come before 2027. He had staked the last four years of his life on it.

Section 8 was not a door. Section 8 was a wall with a door painted on it.

He turned to Section 5.

*5.1, Consultation Rights. Upon the occurrence of a TCP Trigger Event, Chesapeake Heritage Development LLC shall have the right of first consultation with respect to any heritage tourism development, interpretive programming, cultural visitor experience, or associated commercial activity conducted at or in connection with the Designated Property. The Tribe shall provide written notice to LLC within fifteen days of the TCP Trigger Event. LLC shall have ninety days from receipt of such notice to propose terms for a Heritage Development Agreement.*

He read it again. He read it a third time.

*Ninety days from receipt of such notice.*

He put the paper down. He picked up his pen, a black Bic from the cup on the desk, the same cup that held three highlighters and a ruler he never used, and he wrote on the notepad beside the lamp: *90 days from notice.*

He looked at what he had written. He thought about what happened inside those ninety days.

If the TCP Trigger Event occurred, if the mound was formally designated as a Traditional Cultural Property under federal or state review, Marcus had fifteen days to notify Pruitt. Pruitt then had ninety days to propose terms. Ninety days during which the LLC had a contractual position at the table. Ninety days during which any Heritage Development Agreement would be negotiable, which meant ninety days during which Pruitt could propose something that Marcus would have to bring to the council. Ninety days during which the council would learn, for the first time, that the MOU existed.

Unless.

He sat very still.

Unless the site was closed before Pruitt's ninety days ran out.

He pulled the notepad closer. He wrote: *TCP designation, 15 days notice, 90 days for LLC to propose terms.* Below that he wrote: *If site closed, reburial complete before 90 days, no commercial activity to develop, clause moot?*

He put a question mark on *moot* because he was not a lawyer and he did not know if that was how it worked. But the logic seemed to hold. The consultation right was a right of first consultation *with respect to heritage tourism development.* If there was no heritage tourism development, if the site was closed, the remains reburied, the mound sealed under a protection order that prohibited public access, there was nothing for Pruitt to consult about. The right would exist on paper and have nothing to attach to.

He could feel the shape of it. He could feel the way it might work.

He wrote two words on the notepad: *reburial first.*

He looked at them.

He thought about Tayak, standing in the parking lot outside Voss's building at nine o'clock at night, calling him because she needed legal advice and trusted him enough to ask. He thought about what she was doing at the site, the careful methodical work of it, the field notebook and the trowel and the stratigraphy profile and the forty-three-page assessment and the 1697 petition and the copper pendant and all of it, layer by layer, the ground giving up what it had kept for two thousand years. He thought about how long she had been building toward this. He thought about Eleanor Runningwater, who had voted no on the excavation because she believed the obligation to the ancestors was prior to any argument about evidence. He thought about Delia Swann, who had said the same thing in different words. He thought about Jonah Twobirds pressing him for a November 14 deadline, and about Harold Swann's single nod, which had meant *I am watching you.*

If he pushed the council toward rapid reburial, if he framed it as honoring the elders' concerns, as meeting the obligation to the ancestors that Eleanor and Delia had always insisted on, he could close the site before Pruitt's window ran. He could make the clause moot. He could keep the MOU buried in the drawer where it had been for eighteen months, and no one would ever need to know it existed, because the right it granted would have nothing to attach to and would expire in November 2027.

He looked at the two words on the notepad.

He picked up the pen. He drew a single line through them, slow and deliberate, the way you cross out something you want to remember crossing out.

The line did not make them disappear. The ink was still there beneath it: *reburial first.* He could read it perfectly well.

He thought about what it would mean in practice. He would have to work against Tayak's timeline without telling her why. He would have to argue, in council, for positions he did not hold, using language about ancestral obligation that Eleanor and Delia had earned and that he would be borrowing to serve a purpose they knew nothing about. He would have to watch Tayak fight Voss and Slade for the right to keep excavating, and he would have to undercut that fight from inside the council, and she would not understand why, and she would not be able to ask him directly because she did not know the question existed.

He would be doing to her what Voss was trying to do to her. The mechanism would be different. The motivation would be different. The outcome, for Tayak, would be the same.

He turned to Section 6.

*6.1, Exclusivity. During the Term and for a period of five years following the TCP Trigger Event, the Tribe shall not enter into any agreement with any third party for heritage tourism development, interpretive programming, or cultural visitor experience at the Designated Property without first offering LLC the right to match such agreement on equivalent terms.*

He had not focused on Section 6 before. He had read it, but he had read it as boilerplate, as the kind of language that appeared in every development agreement and meant less than it said. Now he read it again and understood that it was not boilerplate. It was the mechanism that made the consultation right real. Even if he found a way to argue that the ninety-day window had lapsed, even if he could make a case that rapid reburial had mooted Pruitt's consultation right, Section 6 would still be active for five years after the trigger event. If the tribe, in that five-year window, ever wanted to do anything with the site, any interpretive programming, any cultural visitor experience, any educational access, Pruitt would have the right to match any agreement they made with anyone else.

He wrote on the notepad: *Sec 6, exclusivity, 5 yrs post-trigger.* He drew a box around it.

He turned back to Section 5 and read it again, more slowly, looking for a termination provision he had missed. There was none. The consultation right had no sunset clause of its own. It ran until the five-year survival period under Section 8.3 expired, and the exclusivity right under Section 6 ran independently for five years from the trigger event. The two clocks were different clocks and could overlap in ways that extended the total exposure well beyond what he had originally understood.

He put the document down. He picked up his coffee. It was cold. He drank it anyway.

The ambiguity was real. He had been right about that. The ninety-day window was real. If the site was closed fast enough, before Pruitt exercised his consultation right, there might be an argument that the right had lapsed. But Section 6 would still be there. Section 6 did not require Pruitt to exercise anything. Section 6 simply prohibited the tribe from going to anyone else without offering Pruitt a match. And a right of first refusal attached to a burial mound that had been sealed under a protection order was not nothing. It was a claim. It was a cloud on the title of everything the tribe might ever want to do with that ground.

He needed Ashby to read this. He needed Ashby to tell him whether his reading of the ninety-day window was correct, and whether rapid reburial would actually moot the consultation right, and whether Section 6 survived that argument or had its own vulnerability. He needed Ashby to tell him these things before he could decide anything, because he was a man reading a contract with a pen and a notepad at two in the morning and he was not a lawyer and the thing he most wanted to believe, that there was a clean exit, that he had found it, that the crossed-out words on the notepad represented a path he had chosen not to take, might be wrong in ways he could not see.

His phone was face-down on the desk. He turned it over. No missed calls. No response from Ashby. A notification from Kimi's Piscataway Rising account. She had posted something new, probably about Voss, probably at midnight when she should have been asleep. He did not open it.

He looked at the notepad. The crossed-out words were still legible. *Reburial first.* Below them, his box around Section 6. Below that, the two clocks he had drawn, the ninety-day clock and the five-year clock, running in parallel, neither one stopping for him.

He thought about George Swann's envelope in the drawer, behind the MOU, sealed for the better part of a year. *You'll know when you need it.* George had been chair for nineteen years before Marcus. George had navigated the tribe through two failed recognition petitions, a land dispute with the county, and a period when the tribal office had run on grant money that arrived three months late and left them unable to pay the copy machine lease. George had sent the envelope three days after the election with a note in his careful unhurried hand, and Marcus had put it in the drawer because he had not wanted to need it yet. Needing it felt like admitting he was not ready. He had believed, had genuinely believed, that he would be the chair who did not make the kind of mistake that required an envelope from his predecessor.

He reached into the drawer. He touched the envelope. He did not take it out.

He closed the drawer.

He sat with the MOU open in front of him, the lamp making a small circle of yellow light on the desk, the rest of the room dark. Outside, a car passed on the road, its headlights sweeping briefly across the blind. The refrigerator in the kitchen cycled off and the house went quiet. Somewhere in the quiet was the fact that Tayak was still out there, at the site or driving home from it or sitting in her truck in a parking lot, doing the work that would eventually produce the trigger event that would start the clocks running.

He picked up the pen. Below the crossed-out words, below the box around Section 6, he wrote a single question: *What does Ashby say?*

He looked at it. It was the right question. It was the only question that mattered tonight, because until Ashby called back, he did not actually know what he was working with. He did not know if the exit he thought he had found was real. He did not know if crossing out two words on a notepad meant he had chosen not to take it, or simply that he had not taken it yet.

He turned the notepad face-down on the desk.

He did not close the MOU. He left it open, Section 5, the ninety-day window, the text he had read four times, and he sat in the lamplight and waited for morning, and for Robert Ashby to call him back, and for the answer to a question he should have asked eighteen months ago, in a room he had kept dark.

He had turned the notepad face-down but he could not turn off the memory of her voice.

*Are there any encumbrances on the site I should know about?*

He had been standing in the kitchen when she called, seven hours ago, the overhead light still off because he had not yet decided to start the day. She had called from the parking lot outside Voss's building. He could hear traffic in the background, the particular flat echo of a concrete structure. She had been controlled, the way she was always controlled, giving him the situation in order: Voss's permit filing, the timing, what she thought it meant, what she needed from him. He had listened and said the right things, the careful things, the things that were true without being complete.

Then she had asked that question.

*Are there any encumbrances on the site I should know about?*

Not: *Is there anything I should know?* Not: *Is there a problem?* She had used the word *encumbrances.* A real property term. A title-search term. The kind of word you use when you already have a shape for the thing you are looking for and you want to see whether the other person flinches.

He had not flinched. He had said: *Not that I'm aware of at this stage.* Which was technically true in the narrowest possible sense. The MOU attached to the recognition petition, not to the site itself, and only became an encumbrance on the site if the site was designated protected, which had not happened yet. He had told himself this distinction as he said the words and he had believed it for approximately four seconds, which was how long it took her to say *okay* in a tone that meant the opposite.

She had not pressed. That was the thing that stayed with him. She had not pressed, because she was Tayak, and Tayak did not press when she suspected she was being managed. She filed the information and she waited, and the waiting was worse than any argument she could have made.

He picked up his phone.

The screen showed 2:47 a.m. No call from Ashby. The Piscataway Rising notification he had not opened. A text from Cassie Threatt at 11:30 p.m., *have you seen Kimi's post,* which he had also not opened, because opening it would require him to respond, and he did not know yet what he would say.

He found Tayak's name in his contacts. The number he had called a hundred times in the last three years, during the recognition petition, during the OFA review, during the two failed hearings, during the night the county tried to reclassify the Mattawoman bottomland. He had called her from this same desk, in this same lamplight, with the same HVAC unit cycling in the ceiling above him, pushing warm air that smelled faintly of dust and old ductwork.

He pressed call.

It rang once.

He thought about her voice when she said *okay.* The flatness of it. The precision of the word *encumbrances,* which she had not used by accident, which she had used because she had already done enough title research to know what shape of problem she was looking for.

It rang twice.

He thought about what he would say. *Tayak, I need to tell you something.* Yes. That was the beginning. *I signed an agreement eighteen months ago with a development company called Chesapeake Heritage Development LLC.* Yes. And then he would have to say the rest of it. Section 5, the ninety-day consultation window, Section 6, the right of first refusal that attached to any protected designation, the fact that he had signed it to fund the recognition petition, the fact that he had told no one, the fact that he had been sitting alone in this room for three hours reading a document he should have shown her before she ever put a trowel in the ground.

He ended the call.

He sat.

The phone was warm from his hand. He turned it face-down on the open MOU, on the page with Section 5, on the text he had read four times. The screen went dark. The room was very quiet. The refrigerator was still off. Outside, nothing moved on the road.

He had told himself, when he ended the call, that it was because he needed Ashby's read first. That he could not explain the ninety-day window or the Section 6 question without knowing whether his analysis was correct. That calling Tayak with a half-understood problem was worse than waiting. That he owed her accuracy before he owed her disclosure.

He sat with that for a moment.

Then he picked up the pen and wrote on the notepad, below the crossed-out words, below the box, below the two clocks, below the single question he had already written:

*You ended the call.*

He looked at it. It was true in a way that *Not that I'm aware of at this stage* had not been true. He had dialed her number at 2:47 in the morning and let it ring twice and ended the call, and the reason was not that he needed Ashby's read first. The reason was that he had heard her voice say *okay* and he had understood, in the way you understand things you have been avoiding, that when he told her, she would not be angry. She would be still. She would listen to all of it, the way she had listened to him in the parking lot tonight, and then she would say something measured and correct, and the measurement and the correctness of it would be the thing he could not face.

He turned the notepad face-down on top of the phone.

The MOU lay open between them, Section 5 facing up, the lamp making the laser-printed text look slightly raised under the light, the way it always did. The words *right of first consultation* sat in their clause the way they had been sitting for eighteen months, patient and contractual and entirely indifferent to what he needed them to mean.

The envelope had been in the bottom drawer for fourteen months.

Marcus knew exactly where it was, had always known, the way you know where a thing is that you have decided not to touch. A standard nine-by-twelve manila envelope, the flap sealed with the self-adhesive strip and then reinforced with a strip of clear tape, which was not something George Swann had done by accident. George did not do anything by accident. He had chaired the council for nineteen years and he taped things shut when he meant them to stay shut.

The note on the front was in George's handwriting, blue ballpoint, the letters large and deliberate the way they got when he was being careful: *You'll know when you need it.*

Marcus had read that note the morning the envelope arrived, three days after the election, and he had put the envelope in the bottom drawer and closed the drawer and gone back to the transition binder Cassie had prepared, because he had not known when he would need it and because George's certainty that he would know had made him uneasy in a way he had not wanted to examine. Fourteen months. He had moved the envelope twice, once when he reorganized the drawer in January, once when he needed the space for the OFA response files in March, and both times he had put it back without opening it, because both times he had not yet known.

He knew now.

He pulled the bottom drawer open. The envelope was where he had left it, under a thin folder of old meeting agendas, the manila gone slightly soft at the corners from the drawer's humidity. He lifted it out. It was lighter than he remembered. He set it on the desk next to the MOU, next to the phone, next to the face-down notepad, and looked at it for a moment in the lamplight.

Then he peeled the tape back carefully, because he did not want to tear the envelope, and lifted the flap.

One sheet of paper, folded in thirds. Standard copy paper, the fold sharp and deliberate. He unfolded it.

A list of names. Twelve of them, handwritten in the same blue ballpoint. No header. No explanation. Just the names, one per line, in alphabetical order, with a date beside each one. A column of dates running from March to November of 2019. Five years before Marcus had signed anything. Five years before he had ever heard of Chesapeake Heritage Development LLC.

He recognized every name on the list.

He read it twice. Then he sat back in his chair and looked at the ceiling, where the HVAC unit was cycling again, pushing warm air through the vent above the desk, the faint smell of dust and old ductwork that had been there since the building was converted from a church annex in 2009 and would be there until the building fell down.

George had known.

Not just known in the general sense, known the way you know that developers circle tribal land the way crows circle carrion, which everyone knew, which was not knowledge so much as weather. George had known specifically. He had known the LLC's name, or known enough to track the contacts, because the only way to compile a list like this, twelve names, twelve dates, five years of careful watching, was to have been paying attention long before anyone brought him a proposal. He had watched them work the community one conversation at a time, the way you work a room before you make your ask, and he had written down what he saw, and he had sealed it in an envelope and given it to whoever came next, because he had known they would come back.

*You'll know when you need it.*

He had not left it as an accusation. That was the thing Marcus kept returning to, sitting in the lamplight with the list in his hand. There was no note attached, no explanation, no instruction. No *do not sign anything* and no *I told you so* waiting in the fold of the paper. Just the names. Just the dates. Just the record of a man who had seen this before and had decided that the most useful thing he could do was leave a map for whoever found themselves standing in the same place.

Marcus looked at the names again.

Three of them were current council members. Two were elders whose opinions he had cited in the OFA petition as evidence of community continuity and political authority. One was a woman who had stood at the last recognition hearing and testified for forty minutes about the unbroken chain of Piscataway presence on this land, her voice steady throughout, and he had been proud of her and had told her so afterward in the parking lot.

None of them had signed anything. He understood that immediately. George would not have kept quiet about a signed agreement, not for five years. They had been approached and they had declined, or they had listened and walked away, or they had done what people do when a well-dressed stranger asks them to consider something. They had said *let me think about it* and then thought about it and said nothing. They had not told George directly, probably. George had simply noticed. George had always simply noticed.

He had been tempted, at least some of them. That was what the list said. Not that they had fallen, but that they had been standing at the edge of the same drop, looking down, feeling the particular pull of a problem that has a solution attached to it if you are willing not to look too closely at the shape of the solution.

Marcus set the list down on the desk.

He looked at his own name. It was not on the list, because in 2019 he had not been chair, had not been anyone the LLC would have bothered to approach. He had been a council member with a portfolio in land affairs and a reputation for asking procedural questions that slowed things down. They had not come to him in 2019. They had come to him in late 2022, when he was chair, when the recognition petition was stalled and the OFA review had come back with a request for additional genealogical documentation that would cost forty thousand dollars they did not have, and they had come with a number and a clause and a story about heritage tourism and authentic visitor experience and cultural interpretation, and the story had been good enough that he had spent three weeks believing it before he signed.

He thought about Tayak's voice on the phone. *Okay.*

He thought about the twelve names on George's list, and the fact that not one of those twelve people had signed, and the fact that he had.

He folded the list back along its creases and slid it into the envelope. He set the envelope on top of the open MOU, on top of Section 5, on top of the words *right of first consultation* and *any site designated as a Traditional Cultural Property or protected archaeological feature,* and he looked at the two documents together in the lamplight. The sealed envelope on top of the open contract. George's handwriting on top of his own signature. The warning on top of the thing it had been warning against.

The room was very quiet. The HVAC had cycled off. Outside, a car passed on the road, its headlights moving across the window blind in a slow diagonal, and then it was gone and the road was empty again and the clock on his phone read 3:11 a.m.

He had been sitting in this room for four hours. In that time he had read the MOU four times, called Tayak and ended the call before she answered, written and crossed out three versions of an explanation he could not finish, and opened an envelope that had been sitting in his bottom drawer for fourteen months telling him exactly what he needed to know about the company he had given a contractual claim to the mound.

He was not the first person to have been tempted.

He was the first person to have said yes.

The difference between those two things was the difference between George's list and his own signature, and he sat with that difference in the dark office and did not look away from it, because looking away from it was what he had been doing for eighteen months and it had brought him here, to this desk, to this lamplight, to these two documents, and there was nowhere further to go in that direction.

He picked up the pen.

He turned the notepad back over. Below everything he had written, below *Not that I'm aware of at this stage,* below the crossed-out words, below the box, below the two clocks, below *You ended the call,* he wrote:

*If you tell her, the petition may survive.*

He looked at it. Then he wrote below that:

*If you don't, the mound is lost anyway. Only slower. With your name on it.*

He put the pen down.

The two sentences sat on the notepad the way the two documents sat on the desk. The shape of the problem, finally stated plainly, without the management language, without the distinctions he had been making between the MOU and the site and the recognition petition and the encumbrance that only attached upon designation. Without any of the architecture he had been building around the thing to make it look like something other than what it was.

He had one call to make. He had known for the last four hours that he had one call to make, and he had spent four hours not making it, and the four hours had not changed anything except to make it 3:11 in the morning, which was not a better time to call Tayak Moyaone than 11 p.m. had been, but which was the time he had.

He picked up the phone. He found her name. He pressed call.

It rang once.

He did not end it.
