The council office sat at the end of a gravel lane off Berry Road, a low cinder-block building painted the color of old cream, its aluminum gutters sagging on the north side where last winter's ice had pulled them. Tayak parked on the gravel and sat for a moment with the engine off. Through the windshield she could see the tribal flag hanging limp above the entrance — the red and blue against a white field, unstirred in the still morning air. No wind. The sky was the flat white of a sheet of paper held up to a window.

She had driven the forty minutes from La Plata turning the same calculation over and over. The OFA proposed finding was due for response in eleven weeks. The dig had reached stratum five. The copper pendant was in conservation. If Marcus resigned today, the federal acknowledgment office would want to know who held political authority for the tribe, and that question alone could pause the review. Six months, minimum. Maybe more.

She got out of the car.

Kimi was standing to the left of the entrance door, arms crossed over her chest, breath coming in small visible puffs. She was wearing a dark green parka that was too thin for November and her sneakers were damp at the toes from the grass. She had not dressed for standing outside. She had dressed for somewhere warm and then come out here to wait.

"How long have you been out here?" Tayak said.

"Since seven-fifteen." Kimi uncrossed her arms and pushed off the wall. "Joel unlocked the door at seven but I didn't want to be in there. Delia got here at six-forty. She was already on the phone when she pulled into the lot."

"Who was she calling?"

"I don't know. But she made two calls before she went inside, and she was walking while she did it." Kimi demonstrated — a tight, contained circuit of three steps each way, the walk of someone keeping her voice low and her thoughts moving.

Tayak looked at the door. Through the narrow window beside it she could see the fluorescent lights were on, casting the entrance hallway in that particular institutional white that made everyone inside look like they hadn't slept.

"What exactly did Marcus tell them?" she said.

"He sent an email at six a.m. to all council members. Not to me," Kimi said. "Cassie forwarded it." She pulled out her phone and read from the screen: "'In light of recent disclosures regarding the MOU with Chesapeake Heritage Development LLC, I believe the council deserves the opportunity to determine whether my continued service as chair is in the best interest of the tribe and the ongoing federal recognition process. I am prepared to offer my resignation at this morning's emergency session, effective immediately or at the council's direction.'"

Tayak took the phone and read it herself. The email was three paragraphs. The second paragraph cited the recognition petition by its OFA docket number. The third expressed confidence in the council's judgment.

She handed the phone back. "He cited the docket number."

"I noticed that."

"He's not resigning. He's performing the offer of resignation and making sure the council knows what's at stake if they accept it."

Kimi put the phone back in her pocket. "Darius called me at six-thirty. He says the vote will be four to four without Marcus and it'll come down to Paul."

"Paul Swiftwind."

"He voted yes on the excavation authorization. But Delia's been working him since dawn, apparently." Kimi paused. "Darius also says Raymond is furious about the MOU but doesn't want to lose Marcus right now because Raymond thinks the petition dies without him."

Tayak turned and looked back at her car, then at the building, then at the flag, still motionless above the door.

"Where does that leave Delia?" she said.

"Delia wants him out. She's been saying since the excavation vote that his judgment can't be trusted. This confirms it for her." Kimi's voice was careful. "She's not wrong, Tayak."

"I know she's not wrong."

"But."

"But she's wrong about the timing." Tayak turned back to face the door. "If Marcus goes today, the OFA review stalls. The state's watching this. Slade is watching this. If the council fractures publicly during a recognition review, the OFA can note it as evidence of political instability and downweight the petition under criterion (b). Continuous political authority. That's what they're testing."

Kimi was quiet for a moment. A car turned into the gravel lot — Harold Swann's white pickup, which parked crookedly near the far fence. Harold got out, saw them, raised a hand without smiling, and went inside.

"So what do you do?" Kimi said.

"I don't have a vote."

"They're going to ask you to speak."

"I know."

"What are you going to say?"

Tayak looked at the door again. Through the narrow window she could see Harold's back as he moved down the hallway. She could see the edge of the council chamber door, propped open, and through it a slice of the room — the long horseshoe table, the nameplates, the U.S. flag and the tribal flag on their stands behind the chair's seat. She could see Delia Swann already seated, her hands flat on the table in front of her, looking at nothing.

"I'm going to say the truth," Tayak said.

"That's not an answer."

"It is. The mound is the argument. Not Marcus." She said it quietly, the way you say something you've been turning over in your head for forty minutes and have finally resolved. "The mound is two thousand years of continuous presence on this land. Criterion (a) through (g), every one of them, answered by what's in that ground. Marcus's mistake is real but it's not the petition. If I can make them see that, it doesn't matter whether he stays or goes."

Kimi looked at her. "You really believe that?"

Tayak picked up her bag from where she'd set it on the hood of the car and slung it over her shoulder. Her field notebook was inside it, the one she'd brought from the site the night before. She had not planned to bring it. She'd put it in the bag without thinking, the way you reach for something familiar when the ground feels uncertain.

"I believe the pendant is real," she said. "I believe the document is real. "Dr. Moyaone," he said. "They're ready to start."

"I know."

"Text me."

Not whether Marcus is chair or former chair or whatever he is right now." She did not look at Marcus. "The state cares about one thing: whether the evidence comes out of the ground before Garrett Wills's equipment arrives. If it does, you have a recognition argument that no attorney can dismantle. If it doesn't, you have a petition and a story and a very good oral history, and the OFA will take four years to evaluate it and the site will be a parking structure."

Raymond said, "How long."

"Three weeks. Maybe twenty-two days if the weather holds."

"And if it doesn't hold."

"Then I work in the rain."

Jonah said, "What are you expecting to find."

"I don't know." She said it plainly, because it was true and because the room needed to hear her say it. "That's not how it works. I know what I have. I know the stratigraphy is intact below the fifth layer. I know the mound was built in phases and that each phase corresponds to a period of community use. I know there are objects in that ground that predate every colonial record in this state. What I find when I get there — I find when I get there."

"That's not very reassuring," Jonah said.

"No," she said. "It isn't."

She picked up the notebook and opened it to the stratigraphy profile she had drawn by hand — four layers, inked in careful cross-section, each stratum labeled with its depth and composition and the artifact recovered from it. She turned it so the council could see. The drawing was small, precise, the kind of thing that looks like nothing until you understand what it represents.

"This is what two months looks like," she said. "Layer one: glass bead, 1690s. Layer two: ceramic pipe bowl, mid-eighteenth century. Layer three: shell gorget, mid-eighteenth century, earlier in type. Layer four: a second gorget, older. Layer five: copper pendant, 200 to 400 CE, possibly earlier. Each layer sealed by the one above it. Each layer documented, photographed, chain of custody intact." She closed the notebook. "That is continuous presence. Not argued. Not asserted. Measured, in centimeters, with a Marshalltown trowel and a Munsell chart."

Delia said, "And Marcus."

"I'm not here to tell you what to do about Marcus." She said it without heat. "That's your decision. It was your decision before I walked in and it's your decision now. I'm telling you that whatever you decide, the excavation has to continue. If you spend the next three weeks in an emergency election, the window closes. If the state uses the MOU to claim a consultation right and freezes the permit, the window closes. If Kimi posts something tonight that gives Slade grounds to argue the site's integrity has been compromised—"

"Kimi's outside," Dev said.

"I know she's outside."

A pause. The fluorescent panel above the council table hummed at a frequency Tayak had been aware of for two hours without being able to name it.

"I'm not defending what Marcus did," she said. "I'm not calling for his resignation. I'm asking this council to decide what the fight is actually about and then let me go back to work."

Eleanor Runningwater said, "The fight is about the ancestors."

"Yes," Tayak said. "It is. And the ancestors are in that ground. I am trying to bring them out of it carefully, with the right tools, before someone with a backhoe does it the other way." She paused. "That's the same fight. It's the same fight."

Eleanor looked at her for a long moment. Her hands were flat on the table, either side of the printed agenda packet. She did not speak again.

Harold said, "Three weeks."

"Twenty-two days."

"And then what. You give us a report."

"I give you a field report with full documentation, conservation analysis, radiocarbon dates where applicable, and a stratigraphic narrative that a federal acknowledgment reviewer can read as evidence of criterion (a), (b), and (c) continuity. I give you something Darius can put in front of the OFA that isn't testimony. That's physical, stratified, dated evidence of a community on this land from 100 BC to the present." She looked at Darius. "That's what you've been trying to build the petition around for four years. I'm telling you it's in the ground. I need twenty-two more days to get it out."

Darius said nothing. He looked at the table. His pen was still. He had come in this morning with his anger converted to strategy, and now the strategy had run into the same wall the anger had, and he was doing the arithmetic again.

Raymond picked up his pen — the heavy ballpoint with the rubberized grip — and this time he wrote something. Tayak couldn't see what. He set the pen down.

"The motion before us," Marcus said, and his voice was still steady, still the voice of a man running a meeting, which was either discipline or stubbornness and Tayak had stopped trying to tell the difference, "is to table the question of the chair's status pending legal review by Ashby and to authorize the excavation to continue under its current permit conditions for a period not to exceed thirty days." He looked at Delia. "Is that an accurate restatement."

Delia said, "It is not. The motion doesn't authorize anything. The excavation is already authorized. The motion tables your status. The excavation continues because it continues."

"Fine."

"And someone else calls Ashby."

"Paul," Marcus said. "Will you call Ashby."

Paul Swiftwind looked at Marcus for a moment. Then he picked up his phone from the table and said, "I'll call him."

The recorder blinked. Joel's pen moved again, small careful letters at the bottom of the page.

Tayak looked at the clock. Nine fifty-three. Thirty-one days to Wills's equipment. Twenty-two days to the bottom of the mound, if the weather held, if the permit held, if the council held.

Three too many ifs. She had worked in worse.

She put the notebook back in her bag and waited for the vote.

Joel read the motion back from his notepad, his voice a careful monotone: table the question of the chair's status pending legal review, authorize no change to current excavation conditions, reconvene within seventy-two hours upon receipt of Ashby's opinion.

Marcus called the vote.

Delia said no.

Jonah said yes, with the flat tone of a man who had decided the lesser damage was still damage.

Dev said yes. Raymond said yes. Harold said yes, and then looked at the table. Eleanor said no, quietly, without looking up. Paul said yes.

Six to two. The recorder's red light blinked once, twice. Angie typed something. The motion carried.

Nobody said anything for a moment. The fluorescent panel hummed.

Then Delia Swann pushed her chair back. Not fast. She was seventy-one years old and she moved with the deliberate weight of someone who had decided to be noticed. She stood, and she picked up her printed agenda packet, and she held it in both hands in front of her like something she intended to return to the person who had printed it.

"The ancestors," she said, "are watching what we do with what they left us."

She set the packet down on the table in front of Marcus's nameplate. Then she walked out. Her footsteps were even on the tile floor, and the door at the back of the room opened and closed without a sound.

No one called her back. Marcus did not call her back.

The room held the shape of what she had said for a long moment.

Then Harold cleared his throat. Raymond capped his pen. Jonah closed his laptop with a soft click that sounded, in the quiet, like a judgment.

"Twenty-two days," Marcus said. He was looking at the middle distance above the table, at nothing in particular. "Dr. Moyaone."

"Yes."

"You'll send the council a progress report at the ten-day mark."

"I will."

"And you'll notify Joel before you open any new unit."

"I'll notify you directly."

Something moved across Marcus's face — not quite acknowledgment, not quite gratitude. He nodded once.

People began to gather their things. The audience rows behind Tayak shifted and murmured, chairs scraping, someone's phone buzzing against a hard surface. Angie saved her document and closed the laptop. Joel tore the top sheet from his notepad and folded it into thirds with the precision of a man who had been doing this for years, then realized he had left his notepad on the table and came back for it.

Tayak stood and put the field notebook in her bag. She pulled her jacket off the chair back and put it on, working the zipper from the bottom because the pull at the top had been broken since February and she had not replaced it.

She looked up.

Marcus was still at the table. Everyone else was in motion around him — Dev talking to Jonah near the door, Raymond pulling on a coat, Harold speaking quietly to Paul — but Marcus was sitting still, both hands flat on the table in front of him, looking at her.

Not with gratitude. She had been right about that before she'd even finished the thought. It was the look of a man who had just watched someone pull him out of a current he had waded into himself, and who understood, with complete clarity, the size of what he now owed. There was no relief in it. There was only the arithmetic.

She held his gaze for a moment. Then she picked up her bag and walked out.

The parking lot was cold. The temperature had dropped while they were inside — a front moving through, the kind that arrives without announcement and leaves the air tasting of iron and wet leaves. The overhead lights threw yellow circles on the asphalt, and Tayak's breath came out in a thin cloud as she stopped at the edge of the lot and found her phone.

Kimi was leaning against the building wall ten feet away, arms crossed, watching her.

"Well?" Kimi said.

"Twenty-two days."

"And Marcus?"

"Seventy-two-hour hold. Ashby does a legal review of the MOU."

Kimi made a sound that was not quite a laugh. "And Delia?"

"She walked out."

Kimi looked at the sky. The clouds had moved in low and fast, covering the stars. "She'll be back," she said, but she didn't sound certain.

"Maybe." Tayak found Wren's name in her contacts. "Go home, Kimi."

"I'm not posting anything."

"I know you're not. Go home anyway."

Kimi pushed off from the wall and walked toward her car without argument, which was unusual enough that Tayak watched her go for a moment before she pressed call.

Wren picked up on the second ring.

"It's me," Tayak said. "We got the time."

A pause. "How much?"

"Twenty-two days. Possibly less if the MOU review goes badly, but twenty-two days is what we have to plan around."

Another pause, shorter. "That's enough," Wren said. "If the weather holds."

"If the weather holds." Tayak looked at the sky again. The iron smell was stronger now. "Can you be at the site at first light?"

"Yes."

"Bring the extra line level. The one in the equipment locker, not the one in the truck. The bubble's off on the truck one and I keep forgetting to replace it."

"I know," Wren said. "I've been using the locker one for three weeks."

Tayak almost said something about that — about the three weeks of compensation she had been making without noticing, which was exactly the kind of thing Carver had told her to catch — but she let it go. "First light," she said. "We're going deeper."

She ended the call. The parking lot was nearly empty now. Raymond's truck pulled out of the far end. The overhead light nearest her flickered once, steadied. Somewhere beyond the tree line, in the direction of the creek bottom and the field road and the mound itself, a night bird called once and went quiet.

Twenty-two days to the bottom layer. To whatever Nora had been holding for sixty-two years. To the woman whose name had been carried in spoken words from fire to fire, kitchen table to kitchen table, through drought and displacement and the long silences of people who had survived by making themselves hard to find.

Tayak zipped her jacket to the throat — the bottom pull still worked — and walked to her car.
