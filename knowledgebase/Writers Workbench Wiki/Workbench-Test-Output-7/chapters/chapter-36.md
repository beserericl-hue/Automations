The mound rose to Ahanu's shoulder when she stood at its base. She had always measured it this way, not in paces or hand-spans but against her own body, the way a mother measures a child. She pressed her palm flat against the earth of its flank. The soil was hot and dry as fired clay, though the sun had barely cleared the tree line.

She began to count.

"Wekasi's family," she said.

The name went into the still air and stayed there. Across the central space, where Wekasi's house had stood for eleven years, the walls had half-fallen. One side leaned inward at the top, lashings rotted through, and the thatch on the east face had blown loose in the spring wind and not been replaced. Through the gap she could see the pale circle of their hearthstone, ash-cold, already filling with blown leaves.

Her palm stayed on the mound.

"Hota's family."

Hota's house still stood, because Hota had been careful and his walls were good wattle-and-daub, the clay mixed with plenty of fiber, and it would take years to fall. But the door faced her and the door was empty and the storage pit in front of it had been left open, its rim crumbling inward at one edge, a dark mouth saying nothing. A small weed had pushed up through the cracked earth at the pit's edge. Ahanu had pulled weeds in that spot for years, keeping the ground clear for Hota's children to run. She had not pulled that weed. She did not know whose job it was now.

"Panu's family. Panu's mother."

Two hearths, because they had lived side by side and shared a field and were counted as one kin-group but not one household. Both gone in the same week, the old woman first, then Panu's family following her south along the river path. Ahanu had watched them go from this same spot. Panu had not looked back. His mother had, once, and her face had held an expression Ahanu did not want to think about. Not grief exactly, but the look of someone setting down a weight they had carried too long.

She pressed harder against the mound's flank. The earth did not give.

"Sima's family."

Sima had been the first, in the first year, before anyone understood that the drought was not a single bad summer but something longer and deeper. It had settled into the bones of the land the way cold settles into old joints. Sima's maize had failed entirely, not just thin ears but nothing, the stalks yellowing by midsummer, the leaves curling around emptiness. She had left before the harvest that was not a harvest, and Ahanu had thought: *she is being foolish, she is frightened, the rain will come*. The rain had not come.

The mound's soil was fine under her palm, drier than it should have been even at the surface, where the short grass that usually kept moisture in the earth had gone sparse and brown. She could feel small cracks where the sod had split. In the old days, this slope had been soft with damp even in late summer, the earth dark and cool an inch below the surface. She had dug her fingers in once, years ago, just to feel it.

"Ohta's family."

Ohta had argued until the last morning. He had stood in the central space and said the river would rise, the clouds would come, the ancestors would not let the people starve on their own ground. Then he had loaded his household onto two canoes and pushed off from the landing before midday. The landing was farther from the water now than it used to be. A wide band of bleached gravel and cracked mud lay between the old bank and the river's new edge, pale and strange-looking, like skin where a scar has healed wrong.

"Mekwa's family."

She had to think for a moment about Mekwa's house, because Mekwa had taken everything: posts, mats, the ceramic storage jars, even the flat hearthstones, pried up and loaded, the ground beneath them pale and root-threaded where the stones had kept the sun off for years. All that remained was a shallow depression in the earth where the floor had been, and the outline of the post holes, dark circles in the pale dust. Grass was already trying to come back in the depression. Grass did not give up the way people did.

She had not said Mekwa's name in two weeks. She said it now, to the mound.

"Tali's family."

Seven.

Her hand dropped from the mound's flank. The dust clung to her palm, pale and fine, and she brushed it against her skirt. It left a smear the color of old ash. Around the central space, the remaining houses stood in their places, eight of them still occupied, fires still lit each morning, smoke still rising in the slow, motionless way smoke rose in this heat, climbing a little and then spreading flat, going nowhere. She could smell the cooking fires from here, thin woodsmoke and something else: the smell of maize porridge made from last year's stores, the older kernels that were harder to pound, that made a thinner, grainier meal. The children had stopped complaining about it. That was the part that frightened her most. Not the complaint, but the silence after the complaint stopped.

Eight families. Seven gone. The mound was still here.

She turned and looked at it properly, the whole of it, the rounded shape rising above the central space with its cap of dying grass and its worn bare path around the base where feet had gone in ceremony for longer than anyone could say. The path was a ring of fine dust now, pale against the darker earth, and it puffed up in small clouds when she shifted her weight. The mound was the one thing in the settlement that looked as it had always looked, more or less: solid, patient, slightly higher than the surrounding ground. That was why it stayed dry when the floodplain flooded. Whoever had built it had known something about water and high ground and the long patience of the earth.

She pressed her palm to the flank again, lower this time, near the base.

"Seven," she said. "I have told you."

The mound said nothing, which was not the same as not listening.

Behind her, she heard a footstep on the dry path. The particular sound of feet on dust: a soft scuff and then a faint puff, the sound that had replaced the sound of feet on damp earth. She missed that sound. She had not noticed missing it until it was gone. She knew this footstep. She did not turn around yet. She kept her palm on the mound and let Sokan come to stand beside her, and he did, and he was quiet, and his quiet was the worst thing she had heard all morning.

He stood close enough that she could feel the warmth off his arm. Not touching. Sokan never touched without being asked, which she had always thought was a quality, and which now felt like one more thing withheld.

She waited.

The cooking smoke from the nearest house moved between them and the sun, thinning to nothing before it reached any height. Somewhere in the settlement a child was hitting something against the ground, a steady, dull thud, the kind of sound a child makes when there is nothing else to do and no one has told them to stop.

She waited longer.

"The path needs tending," Sokan said finally.

She looked at the worn ring around the mound's base, the fine pale dust, the crumbling edge where the sod had pulled away. He was right. It did.

"That is what you came to say."

He said nothing.

She turned to look at him. He was watching the path the way he watched most things: steadily, without urgency, with the particular stillness of a man who had decided long ago that watching carefully was the same as doing something. His deerskin mantle had a salt-pale line along one shoulder where sweat had dried and dried again all summer. His face was thinner than it had been in the spring. Everyone's face was thinner.

"Sokan." She kept her voice level. "Will the people come tomorrow."

He did not answer immediately. She watched him not answer. He was looking at the far edge of the settlement, where Mekwa's empty floor depression was already going to grass, the pale circle of post holes like a question someone had written in the ground and then walked away from.

"Sokan."

"I heard you."

"Then answer me."

He was quiet for a moment that lasted long enough to have a shape. Then he said, "I will be there."

She turned back to the mound. Her palm was still against its flank, and the clay was warm from the morning sun, warmer than it should have been, warmer than earth had any right to be this early in the day. She pressed harder, as if the mound might push back.

*I will be there.* Not: the others will come. Not: do not worry, the families will not leave again, the ceremony will hold what is left of us together. Only: I will be there. One man, one hearth, one fire still lit.

"How many do you think?" she said.

"Ahanu."

"How many."

He exhaled through his nose, a sound that was not quite a sigh. "Enough," he said. "Enough to do the ceremony properly."

"That is not a number."

"No," he said. "It is not."

She felt something move in her chest. Not grief exactly, not yet, but the space where grief would be when she finally let it settle. She had been keeping it out by staying busy, by counting, by pressing her palm to the mound and saying names. The names were the work. The work kept the grief from having room.

The child somewhere behind her stopped hitting the ground. The silence that followed had weight.

"Ohta said the ancestors would not let the people starve on their own ground," she said.

"Ohta is on the river."

"I know where Ohta is."

Sokan shifted his weight. The dust puffed once under his foot and settled. "Ohta believed what he said. He left because he believed it and the rain still did not come, and he could not stay and watch himself be wrong."

She had not thought of it that way. She did not say so.

"The ceremony is at first light," she said. "I will light the fire myself if I have to. I will walk the path myself." She stopped, because she could hear what she sounded like, and she did not want to sound like that. She pressed her lips together and breathed through her nose and let the thin, ash-tasting air fill her and go out again.

Sokan said, quietly, "I know."

"Do you think it will bring rain."

The question came out before she had decided to ask it. She felt it leave her and hang in the still air between them, exposed, the way a thing is exposed when you have been carrying it inside and then it is suddenly outside and you cannot take it back.

He did not answer right away. She was grateful for that. A fast answer would have been a lie in either direction.

"I think," he said, and stopped. Started again. "I think the ceremony is not only for the rain."

She looked at him.

His eyes were on the mound. "The people who come tomorrow will come because they have not left. The ceremony is how they know they have not left. It is how they know they are still here." He paused. "The rain may come or it may not. But if we do not hold the ceremony, the people who are still here will not know what they are still here for."

She stood with that.

The cooking smoke had thinned to nothing. The child was gone, or had found something quieter to do. The settlement was very still, the way it was still now most mornings. Not the old still of people sleeping late, but the new still of people who had stopped expecting the day to bring something different.

"You could have said that first," she said.

"I was thinking."

"You were standing there saying nothing."

"I was thinking," he said again, with a patience that was almost, but not quite, irritating enough to be a comfort.

She brushed the pale dust from her palm against her skirt. The smear it left was the color of old ash, the same color as the smear from before, and she looked at it for a moment: two pale marks layered on the dark fabric, one from this morning and one from a few minutes ago.

"First light," she said.

"I will be there," he said.

She believed him. She was not sure it was enough. She was not sure it was not., -

The families came before the light had fully separated from the dark. She could hear them before she could see them: the sound of feet on the dry path from the settlement, the soft knock of a water jar against someone's hip, a child's voice cut short by a hand. Ahanu was already at the mound. She had not slept.

She had the fire going at the mound's eastern foot, small but deliberate, built from the dry hickory she had set aside in the seventh month for this purpose. The smoke rose straight up in the still air and then spread flat, a thin sheet that caught the first gray light and held it. She fed it a pinch of dried sweetgrass she kept in a small clay pot stopped with a plug of bark, the last of what she had gathered in the spring, when there had still been enough water in the low ground to find it. The smell opened in the air around her, sweet and faintly green, cutting against the persistent undertaste of ash that had been in everything for weeks.

She counted them as they came.

Sokan first, as he had said. Then Wapi and his wife, the wife carrying the youngest on her back, the child's head lolling with sleep. Then the three women of Hina's household, two of them holding hands the way people hold hands when they are not sure of the ground. Then Danu, who walked with a stick now and had not come to the last ceremony, who had sent word that her knees would not carry her, and who was here now anyway, leaning forward from the hips with her chin lifted, which told Ahanu something she did not have words for yet. Then six more, then three, then a boy of perhaps ten who came alone and stood at the edge of the gathering with his arms at his sides, watching her face.

She counted twenty-three.

Last season there had been thirty-one.

She did not let herself stand still long enough to feel it. She picked up the feathered staff: cedar wood, the feathers tied with cord she had twisted herself from plant fiber. She walked to the east side of the mound to begin.

The path around the mound's base was a ring of fine pale dust, the grass long since worn away by years of feet. In the drought it had dried to something almost powdery, and it puffed up around her ankles with each step, settling on the damp skin above her moccasins and leaving a pale, even line. She felt it. She kept walking.

She went sunwise. She always went sunwise. There was a right way to do this and she knew it and she did it, not because she was certain it would bring rain but because the rightness of it was itself a kind of argument: to the sky, to the ancestors in the mound, to the twenty-three people standing in the early light watching her. *We have not forgotten how.* She carried the staff level, the feathers trailing. Her lips moved with the words of the first address, the old words, the ones Ohta had taught her before Ohta had gone to the river, before the rain had failed to come and he had found he could not stay and watch himself be wrong.

She said the words. She said all of them. She did not shorten them.

At the south side of the mound she stopped and turned to face the sky. The sky was pale, the color of bleached bone, with a thin smear of cloud in the west that had been there for two days and had not thickened. She looked at it. She lifted the staff. She spoke the address to the south direction, the direction of warmth, of growing things, of the season that had refused to come properly for three years. Her voice was steady. She was proud of that.

She moved on.

When she reached the west side she lit the pipe. It was the effigy pipe Mekwa's grandmother had made and Mekwa had left behind when the family went, set on the ground beside the mound as if setting down a debt. She drew the smoke and directed it to each direction in turn, her breath tasting bitter and dry, catching at the back of her throat that was already parched from the morning air. She held the smoke for a moment before releasing it. The smoke went up and spread flat again, the same thin sheet as the fire's smoke. The sky did not answer.

She walked the north side. She said the words for the ancestors in the mound, the names she knew: Heka, Tanu, the woman whose name had gone partially soft in the telling, Ohta's grandfather, the man wrapped in bark at the second depth whose name the chain had not held. She said them all. Her voice did not waver. Behind her she could hear the twenty-three breathing, the shuffle of a foot, the small sound of someone swallowing against a dry throat.

Then she heard the child.

It was not crying, exactly, not at first. It was the sound a child makes before crying: a thin, high note, quickly pressed down by someone's hand, then the muffled sound of the child's face against cloth, then the thin note again, higher. Thirsty. She knew the sound. Every mother knew it. She did not turn. She kept walking, back toward the east, completing the circuit, her feet raising the pale dust that settled on her ankles and did not blow away because there was no wind.

The child's sound stopped. The hush that followed cost something.

She came back to the fire and stood before it. She planted the staff in the ground beside it. She raised both hands, palms up, and spoke the final address to the sky, to the rain that was held somewhere above the bleached clouds, to whatever was listening, if anything was listening. She said the words exactly as she had been taught them, every word, in the right order, nothing omitted, nothing changed.

The sky stayed pale.

The thin smear of cloud in the west did not move. No dark anvil built behind it. No pressure change came through the still air, no smell of wet earth or distant lightning. The insects in the dry grass went on buzzing. The hickory fire crackled once and settled. Somewhere in the settlement, a jar was set down on hard ground and rang with that high, sharp sound that empty vessels make.

She lowered her hands.

She stood with her back to the mound and looked at the twenty-three people in the early light. They were looking at her. Sokan was in the middle of them, not at the front, not at the back, in the middle, where she could find him without searching. Danu had her eyes closed, her stick planted in the dust, her lips still moving with something. The boy who had come alone was watching Ahanu's face with an expression she recognized: he was trying to learn from it what he was supposed to feel.

She did not know what to give him.

She kept her face still. She kept her hands at her sides. She looked at each of them in turn, not quickly, not in a sweep, but one by one, the way you look at people when you want them to know they have been seen. Wapi's wife, the child still asleep on her back, unbothered, innocent of the ceremony and the pale sky both. The three women of Hina's household, the two who had held hands now standing apart, each holding herself. Sokan, watching her with the particular quality of attention he had when he was not going to speak unless she needed him to.

She said: "The ancestors have heard us."

Her voice came out even. She had not been certain it would.

She said: "We have spoken the names. We have walked the path. We have given the smoke to the four directions. The ceremony is complete."

No one spoke. The insects went on.

"We will come back tomorrow," she said.

A small sound moved through the group. Not protest, not agreement, something between: the sound of people absorbing a thing they had half expected and had hoped not to hear confirmed. Danu opened her eyes. The boy's expression did not change.

Sokan said nothing. He had told her the night before what the ceremony was for, and he had been right, and he did not need to say it again. The clay was warm. Already warm, this early, the sun barely up.

She pressed her hand harder against it.

Behind her, the last footsteps faded. She was alone with the fire and the mound and the pale sky and the sound of the insects. She thought about the storage pit she had opened two days ago, the low heap of cobwebbed cobs at the bottom, the faint sour smell that had come up when she lifted the bark cover. Then she thought about tomorrow.

She would light the fire again. She would walk the path again. She would say all the words, in the right order, nothing omitted.

She did not know if it would bring rain.

She had expected him to say something. She had been braced for it, some version of *you should have woken me* or *you should have slept* or even the silence that meant he was worried and did not know how to say so. But this was a different kind of silence. He was not withholding. He was simply there, the way the mound was there, the way the river was there even when you could not see it.

She did not ask how he had known to come.

The sky lightened another degree. The tree line separated itself from the sky, became a jagged edge of individual shapes. And then she heard more footsteps.

She did not turn at first. She waited until she was sure, until the sound was unmistakable: not one person but several, moving at different paces, a shuffle and a quicker step and then the slower walk of someone older. She turned.

Wapi's wife came first, with the youngest on her hip, the child still half-asleep with its head against her shoulder. Behind her, Daya and his wife, Daya carrying a tied bundle that she recognized as dried fish wrapped in bark, their stores, or part of them. Then Reva, who walked with a stick now and had walked with a stick since the winter before last, his feet raising small puffs of dust with every step on the dry path. And behind Reva, Yona.

Yona walked with her eyes on the ground in front of her. She did not look up when she reached the mound. She sat down on the western side, away from Ahanu, and put her hands on her knees, and looked at the mound the same way Ahanu was looking at it.

No one spoke about the rain.

Wapi's wife sat and settled the child in her lap. The child slept. Daya set his bundle down and opened it: the dried fish, and beneath it a small pouch of parched corn, the kernels hard and pale, the kind you pounded into meal when the fresh corn was gone. He set these things at the mound's base, not ceremonially, not with words, just set them down the way you set something down when you have brought it and this is where it belongs.

Reva lowered himself to the ground with the slow, deliberate care of a man who has learned that his body will do what he asks of it but requires time. He sat, arranged his stick across his knees, and closed his eyes. He might have been sleeping. He might have been praying. With Reva it was sometimes impossible to tell, and Ahanu had come to think this was not an accident.

The sky was pale now, the horizon going from gray to the color of old ash, and the mound's shape was clear: the rounded top, the worn path around its base, the places where the grass had given up and the packed clay showed through, cracked in long lines that she had been watching open wider all summer, the earth splitting along seams she had not known were there.

She looked at the people sitting around it.

Eleven. She counted without meaning to: Sokan beside her, Wapi's wife with the child, Daya and his wife, Reva, Yona, and four others who had come quietly while she was watching the sky, arriving the way people arrive when they have decided something without talking about it. Eleven people sitting in the early light around a mound that had held this community's dead for longer than anyone here could trace, eating nothing, saying nothing, watching the pale sky above the tree line for clouds that were not there.

She felt something move through her that was not hope and was not despair. It was smaller than hope and more durable: the recognition of a fact. They had come back. No one had told them to. She had not sent word. Yona had not organized it. They had simply come, the way you go back to a fire that is going out, not because you are certain you can save it but because you cannot make yourself leave it.

The bowl Sokan had brought was between them. She lifted the bark cover. Porridge, thin, made from the older stored corn, the kind that had been in the pit since last harvest and had that faint sour smell she had learned to stop noticing. He had cooked it before dawn. He had risen before her, or had not slept either, and had cooked it and brought it to her here.

She looked at him.

He was watching the mound. His profile in the pale light was the face she had been watching for twenty years, the lines of it familiar the way the path was familiar, worn into her in a way she could not have described but would have felt immediately if it changed.

She did not say anything. She put the bark cover back.

The sun came up behind the trees: not dramatically, not with color, just a brightening that turned the pale sky white and then yellow-white, the flat, hard light of a drought morning that would be hot by midday and hotter by afternoon. The shadows around the mound shortened and sharpened. The dust on the path around its base went from gray to the color of dry bone.

Wapi's child woke and looked around with the wide, assessing eyes of a child who has woken in an unfamiliar place and is deciding whether to cry. It looked at the mound. It looked at Ahanu. It did not cry.

Daya's wife began to hum, not a ceremony song, just a low, tuneless sound, the kind of sound you make when you are doing something with your hands and your mind is somewhere else. She was smoothing the dust in front of her with her palm, back and forth, the way you smooth a mat before sitting. The sound was very quiet. The insects had started again in the dry grass beyond the path.

Ahanu looked at the mound's cracked surface, at the long lines in the clay where the earth had split, and she thought about what Yona had said in the dark: *perhaps the ancestors are not listening*. She thought about what she had said back to her, and she thought about whether she had believed it.

She believed it enough.

But she could also see, in the clear flat light of morning, what she had not let herself see clearly before. Eleven people. The path worn to dust. The storage pits going low. Daya had brought his fish and his corn and set them at the mound's base, and that was a true and generous thing, but it was also nearly everything he had. You could not eat ceremony. You could not drink the names of the dead.

She did not know yet what she would have to give up to hold this. She knew it would be something. The center held this morning, and it had held yesterday, and it would likely hold tomorrow, but she could feel the weight of what was pulling at it: the hunger, the dry ground, the families already gone north, the families who had not come this morning, the slow loosening of a thing that had once seemed fixed.

The mound sat in the early light and said nothing, the way it always said nothing, the way it had been saying nothing for longer than she had been alive. The people sat around it and said nothing either, and the sun climbed, and the shadows shrank, and the dust on the path stayed still because there was no wind.

She would have to find something to give them. Not rain. She could not give them rain. But something. Something that was not only *stay because the dead are here* and not only *the words are enough.* Something she did not have yet.

She looked at Sokan. He was still watching the mound. His hands were on his knees, the knuckles dry and cracked from the work of the summer, the skin at his wrists pale where the sun had not reached. She had not told him what Yona had said. She had not told him what she had said back. She would tell him, or she would not, and either way he would already know the shape of it, the way he knew the shape of most things that moved through her.

The child in Wapi's wife's lap reached out and put one small hand flat on the ground. It pressed its palm against the packed earth and looked at it, as though checking something. Then it looked up at Ahanu with dark, direct eyes, a little older than the rest of its face.

She held its gaze for a moment. Then she looked back at the mound.

*From whatever we leave behind,* she had told Yona.

She did not know yet what that would be. But she was here, and the people were here, and the mound was here, and the morning was already burning toward the day's full heat, and there was work still to be done: water to fetch, the far end of the maize field to check, the cracked wall on Reva's house that needed new clay before the rains came, if the rains came, when the rains came.

She put her hands on the ground and pushed herself up.

"Come," she said. Not loudly. Not as a command. Just the word, the way you say a word when it is simply true. She picked up the bowl Sokan had brought and held it out to Wapi's wife, who took it and drank what was left and passed it to Daya's wife, who drank and passed it to Reva, who drank and set it on the ground with a small hollow sound, the empty clay ringing briefly in the dry air.

They rose, one by one, in the way people rise when they have been sitting a long time: slowly, with attention to knees and backs, the small adjustments of bodies that have been still. Yona rose last. She did not look at Ahanu. She picked up her walking stick, a straight piece of ash she had brought that Ahanu had not noticed until now, and turned toward the path back to the settlement.

Ahanu walked the path around the mound's base before she followed them.

Sunwise. Feet in the dust, the fine pale powder rising around her ankles with each step, settling on the dark skin of her feet, coating the cracks in her heels. The path was clear and worn and hers, and her mother's, and older than that. She walked it once, slowly, feeling the packed earth under her feet and the sun on the back of her neck and the dry air in her throat.

Then she walked back to the settlement, and the day began.
