The building smelled of floor wax and recycled air. All government buildings smelled this way, as if someone had decided long ago that the air inside should have no memory of outside.

Nora Moyaone walked through the double doors at seven minutes past nine, the quilt folded under her left arm. She had folded it herself that morning before Kimi arrived, the way Ida had taught her: thirds lengthwise, then in half, so the center panel faced inward and the back panel faced out, the stitched notation pressed against her ribs where no one could read it until she chose.

Her right hand found the door frame as she entered. A brief, light touch, the kind of steadying she had learned not to disguise. Eighty-two years old, and the body had its opinions. She did not hurry.

The commission chamber was a long rectangular room with fluorescent lights that hummed at a frequency just below the threshold of annoyance. Three folding tables had been pushed together at the front to form the commission's bench, covered with a length of dark green cloth that did not quite reach the floor on either side. Six commissioners sat behind name placards printed on card stock. A stenographer, a young woman with a narrow face, sat to the left with a keyboard device on a stand in front of her, earbuds trailing to a recording unit. A water pitcher and two glasses stood at the center of the table, the water already gone slightly flat-looking in the artificial light.

Nora took this in without stopping.

The gallery held perhaps forty people. Tribal members she knew by face and name, a few she did not, two reporters she identified by their posture and the small notebooks angled on their knees, and, near the back, a man in a gray suit whose stillness had the quality of someone paid to watch rather than listen. She had seen that quality before. She did not look at him a second time.

Tayak was seated to the left of the witness table, in the second chair of the first row. She was wearing her field jacket, Nora noticed. Not a suit, not a dress. The same dark canvas jacket she wore to the site, as if she had come straight from the ground and was not going to pretend otherwise. Her hands were flat on her thighs. She was watching Nora the way she watched the trench when something was coming up, with a stillness that cost her something.

Nora did not let her eyes stay on Tayak. Not yet.

She set the quilt on the witness chair while she hung her coat on the hook beside the door. The coat was dark wool, brushed that morning, the collar turned down properly. She had worn it to Ida's funeral, to her husband's funeral, to the OFA consultation in 1997 when the man from Washington had spoken for forty minutes about evidentiary thresholds and then asked, with genuine puzzlement, why the tribe had no contemporaneous written records from the eighteenth century. She had not answered him that day. She had not been the one testifying. She was the one testifying today.

She picked up the quilt and walked to the witness chair and sat down.

The commission chair was a broad man in his late fifties named Alderman, with the look of someone who had spent years in rooms like this one and had made a reasonable peace with fluorescent light. He had a thick neck and reading glasses pushed up on his forehead and a way of holding his pen that suggested he used it more as a pointer than a writing instrument. He waited until she was settled, which she respected.

"For the record," he said, his voice carrying the flat, careful enunciation of someone who knew a transcript was being made, "we are resuming the hearing on the cultural and historical designation of Site 18CH-447, Charles County, Maryland. The time is nine-twelve a.m." He looked down at the paper in front of him, then up. "The commission calls Nora Moyaone."

He said her name correctly. Both syllables of the surname, the stress in the right place. Someone had told him, or he had asked. She noted this.

"Present," she said. Her voice came out steady, which she had not been entirely certain it would. The room was cold, the way rooms with too much air conditioning were always cold, and cold had been doing things to her voice since October.

"Mrs. Moyaone, you are here today to provide testimony regarding the oral history and cultural significance of Site 18CH-447 as it pertains to the Piscataway people's continuous presence in Charles County, Maryland. You have submitted an affidavit, which has been entered into the record as Exhibit Fourteen." Alderman glanced at the stenographer, who gave a small nod. "You are now asked to speak to that affidavit and to any additional testimony you wish to offer. You are under oath as of your swearing-in this morning. Do you understand?"

"I understand."

"You may proceed."

Nora set the folded quilt on the table in front of her, her hands resting on top of it. The cotton was warm from being held against her side. She could feel the slight ridge of the back panel's stitching beneath her palm, the notation system Ida's grandmother had made, the one Nora had spent three winters learning to read before she understood it was not a code but a map.

She looked at the six commissioners. Two of them were already writing. One was watching her with the careful, neutral expression of someone trying not to have an expression. One was looking at the quilt.

Good, she thought. Look at it.

"My name is Nora Moyaone," she said. "I am eighty-two years old. I was born in Charles County, Maryland, on Billingsley Road, in the same house where my mother was born, and her mother before her. I have lived within twelve miles of this site my entire life."

She paused. Not for effect. To let the stenographer catch up.

"I am going to tell you what I know and how I know it. I am going to tell you who told me, and who told them, and who told them before that. I am going to tell you the names. All of them that I have."

One of the commissioners, a woman with short gray hair and a yellow legal pad, looked up from her writing.

"The commission has forty minutes scheduled for your testimony," Alderman said. Not unkindly. A statement of fact, the way a man reads a tide table.

Nora looked at him.

"Then I will use forty minutes," she said.

The woman with the yellow legal pad set down her pen. The room, which had been making the small shuffling sounds of a room settling into itself, went quiet.

Nora's hands did not move from the quilt.

She had been afraid, driving here with Kimi, that the room would hear an old woman telling family stories. That they would listen with the particular patience people reserved for things they had already decided did not count as evidence. She had been afraid of the fluorescent light and the card-stock name placards and the water pitcher and all the apparatus of official procedure, which had a way of making everything that did not fit its categories sound like something else. Like sentiment. Like memory. Like the kind of thing you put in a footnote and did not cite.

She looked at Alderman. She looked at the woman with the yellow legal pad. She looked at the man in the gray suit at the back of the room, who was watching her with the same quality of paid attention he had brought to everything else.

She had forty minutes.

She had been carrying this for sixty-two years.

She began.

She began with Saya, because Saya was the name she had been given first, the name Ida's grandmother had placed at the front of the chain like a door.

"Saya," she said. "Born approximately 1660, Charles County, Maryland. Daughter of a Piscataway man who worked as a copyist for the Jesuit mission on the Patuxent River. Her father taught her to read colonial documents. She used that knowledge to draft a petition to the Provincial Court in 1697, arguing that the high ground near Mattawoman Creek held her people's dead and could not be taken by patent. The petition was entered into the court record by a clerk named Thomas Ware during the winter session of that year. I have read that petition. My granddaughter excavated two oilskin-wrapped copies of it from the mound site last spring. They are in evidence."

She heard a pen moving. Good.

"Saya carried two things forward. She carried the written petition, the paper record, the one courts could read. And she carried the names. The chain of names going back from her to the people already in the ground. She knew that one form of keeping was not enough. That is why there are two."

The man in the gray suit at the back had stopped writing.

"Before Saya," Nora said, "there was Okwi."

She said it the way she had practiced in the kitchen at five in the morning with the house dark and the coffee going cold on the counter. Not performed, not announced. Placed. The way you set a stone.

"Okwi. Saya's husband. He understood the same thing Saya understood: that the paper record and the spoken record had to run together or one of them would fail. He is the one who buried the copies. He knew that burying them in the mound was not hiding them. It was keeping them in the place that already held the proof."

"Waya wore a shell gorget for twenty years. She gave it away with the names attached, Sokan, Ahanu, Suki, because she understood that the names were the point, not the object. "Each of these people," Nora said, "made a choice. Not one of them was required to carry this. There was no institution to compel them. There was no enforcement mechanism. There was no record that would have noticed if they had set it down. They carried it because they understood that the ground holds the dead and the living hold the names, and that one without the other is incomplete."

The same part where she kept the cold in the room and the tightness in her left knee and the fact that she had not eaten since five-thirty that morning.

"I have been carrying the name of the first woman," she said, "for sixty-two years. The woman at the bottom of the chain. The one who started it. I have not said it on a telephone. I have not written it in a document. I have said it in the places where it belongs to be said, and I have said it to the people who needed to receive it, and I have waited for the moment when saying it in a room like this one would mean something that could not be undone." "The mound is not a landmark," Nora said. "It is not a site. It is not a designation. It is a record. The back was unbleached muslin, and it was covered in stitching.

Not decorative stitching. Not quilting in the ordinary sense, the running stitch that holds the layers together. The woman with the yellow legal pad had not picked up her pen.

The fluorescent lights went on humming. The water pitchers stood in their places. The room smelled of old coffee from the urn on the side table and the faint dry cedar-and-dust smell of the quilt, which had been folded in its cedar chest for eleven months and now lay open on the commission table in the fluorescent light of a government building in Charles County, Maryland, in the autumn of 2024.

Nora lifted her hands from the back panel. She straightened. Her lower back ached from standing, but she did not shift her weight. Not yet.

She said, "That is the name of the woman who placed the copper pendant in the ground. The woman my granddaughter found at the bottom of the mound. She is the first name in the chain. Every name I have spoken today goes back to her."

Alderman said, "It is in the record."

"Yes," Nora said. "Now it is."

She looked at Tayak again.

Tayak had not moved. Her hands were still flat on the table. But her eyes had changed. They were wet, not spilling, just wet, the way eyes go when something has arrived that a person has been waiting for without knowing they were waiting. The arrival was not a relief exactly. It was something older than relief, something that sits beneath relief the way the deepest stratum sits beneath all the layers above it.

Nora thought: *sixty-two years.*

She thought: *Ida, I gave it to her. I gave it to her in the right place.*

She thought: *your granddaughter found the bottom of the mound, and I have just told her who is there.*

She did not allow herself to think the next thought. The next thought had her daughter in it, and she could not go there, not in this room, not in front of these people, not while she was still standing.

She folded her hands in front of her on the back panel of the quilt.

"I have nothing further," she said, "unless the commission has questions."

The chair said the commission would take a fifteen-minute recess.

The room moved. Chairs scraped. The man with the blue lanyard stood and spoke quietly to the woman with the yellow legal pad. The stenographer set her hands in her lap and rolled her shoulders back, the slow rotation of someone releasing a long tension. Two of the commission members stepped out through the side door. The digital recorder's red light went dark.

Nora stood at the table and looked at the quilt.

It had been folded in its cedar chest since last November, when she had finished the back panel's final notation mark and understood that the work was done. Eleven months. Before that it had lived in the chest for years at a stretch, taken out only when she was adding to it or when she needed to look at something she was afraid of forgetting. The chest sat at the foot of her bed on Billingsley Road and she had walked past it every morning for sixty-two years. She had never once considered getting rid of it. She had never once found it easy to open.

Now it lay on a government table under fluorescent lights and it looked, she thought, exactly like what it was. Not a document. Not an artifact. A thing made by hands, repaired by hands, carried by hands. The central panel had a brown water stain along the lower left edge from the summer of 1972, when Hurricane Agnes had come up through the floor of the old house and touched the bottom of the chest before she got it onto the bed. The stitching on the figure's left shoulder had been redone twice, once by Ida, once by Nora herself, with thread that was close but not exact, so if you knew where to look you could see the repair as a seam of slightly darker cotton running through the original. The back panel's fold lines were deep and permanent, the cotton stiffened along each crease into something almost papery, so that when she had unrolled it on the table the panel had not lain flat but had held its folded geometry for a moment before settling, the way a person holds their shape when they first stand after sitting a long time.

She began to refold it. Back panel first. She brought the lower edge up to the center fold, pressing the crease flat with both palms the way Ida had shown her, using the weight of her hands rather than her fingers because fingers left ridge marks in aged cotton.

"Here."

Tayak was beside her. She had not heard her cross the room. She was on the other side of the quilt, and she took the upper edge without being asked and brought it down to meet Nora's fold, and for a moment they both had their hands on the same crease, pressing it flat, and neither of them spoke.

They worked in silence through the next fold. The quilt was large, made for a full bed in an era when beds were wider than they are now, and folding it properly required both of them to move in coordination, stepping back, bringing the edges together, pressing. The dry cedar-and-dust smell rose as the layers compressed. Nora had been smelling that smell her whole adult life and it no longer carried anything she could name except *the chest*, and the chest meant everything the chest contained, which was everything she had been asked to hold.

They got the quilt to its final folded size, a rectangle about the width of a large book and the depth of a man's hand. Tayak held it while Nora smoothed the top surface once, checking the alignment of the edges. Then Nora took it back and held it against her chest with both arms, the way she had carried it into the room that morning.

She looked up.

Tayak was watching her. The professional composure was gone. Not collapsed, not broken, but set aside the way you set aside a tool you no longer need for this particular work. Her eyes were still wet. She had not wiped them.

She said, "How long have you known her name?"

Nora said, "Since I was twenty."

Tayak was quiet. The fluorescent lights hummed. From the corridor outside came the sound of voices, the commission members talking in the low, careful way of people who are not ready to say what they are thinking.

"Ida told you," Tayak said. It was not quite a question.

"In her kitchen. I was twenty years old and she was seventy-one and she said she needed to tell me something before she lost the words for it. She had her hands flat on the table." Nora paused. "She died four years later. I had four years with her."

Tayak looked at the quilt in Nora's arms. Then she looked at Nora's face.

"Sixty-two years," she said.

"Yes."

"You carried it that long and you didn't, " She stopped. She pressed her lips together and looked away toward the window, where the grey October light came through the blinds in flat, even bars. Her jaw was tight. She was deciding something, or trying to.

Nora waited. She had learned, over eighty-two years, that the most important thing a person could do in a room was sometimes simply to remain in it.

Tayak turned back. Her voice was controlled but only just. "Why didn't you tell me sooner? I've been in that ground for eleven months. I've been, " She stopped again.

"I know what you've been doing," Nora said.

"Then why?"

Nora shifted the quilt in her arms. Her lower back still ached and she was tired in the way she was always tired after something that had required the full use of herself. Not the tiredness of age, which was different and always present, but the particular emptiness of having given something away that you had been holding for a very long time.

She said, "Because names don't belong in the air. They belong in the right place. Ida made me understand that. The name is not information. It is not a fact you can hand to someone across a room. It has to be received in the place where it means what it means." She looked at Tayak steadily. "You needed to be standing at the bottom of the mound. Not in my kitchen. Not on the telephone. Here."

Tayak said nothing for a moment. Then: "And if the commission had ruled against us before I got there?"

"I would have found another way to get you there."

"That's not an answer."

"It is the only answer I have."

Tayak looked at her for a long time. The wet had not left her eyes. She was not weeping and she was not going to weep. Nora recognized this because it was the same thing Nora's own face did when it was working to hold something that was larger than the moment allowed for.

The side door opened and two commission members came back in, talking in low voices, pulling out chairs. The stenographer returned and set her hands over the keyboard. The man with the blue lanyard came back to his seat and glanced at the table where the quilt had been, registering its absence with a small, almost imperceptible adjustment of his expression.

Alderman came in last. He moved to his chair at the head of the table and set his hands on the back of it and looked at the room.

"We'll reconvene in two minutes," he said. "Ms. Moyaone, thank you. You're free to go, or you're welcome to stay for the public comment portion if you'd like to observe."

"I'll stay," Nora said.

Tayak had not moved. She was still standing beside Nora, close enough that Nora could feel the warmth of her through the sleeve of her good coat.

Nora said, quietly enough that only Tayak could hear: "The name is in the record now. Whatever happens in this room, whatever they decide, she is in the record. She was in the ground for two thousand years and now she is in the record. That is not nothing."

Tayak looked at the table where the quilt had lain. The fold lines were still visible in the slight compression of the tablecloth, the ghost of the quilt's shape pressed into the fabric beneath it, the way a thing leaves its mark on what it rested on.

She said, still quiet, "I know it's not nothing."

"But?" Nora said.

Tayak turned and looked at her directly. What was in her face was not anger exactly, and not grief exactly, and not accusation. It was something more complicated than any of those, something that had been sitting in her for a long time waiting for the moment it could be named. The moment had arrived and she was not yet sure what to do with it.

She said, "I'll ask you the rest later."

"Yes," Nora said. "You will."

She meant it as a promise, not a warning. She thought Tayak understood this. She was not certain.

Alderman said, "All right. We're back on the record."

The stenographer's fingers moved to the keys. The red light on the digital recorder came back on. Nora carried the quilt to the chair against the wall and sat down with it in her lap, her hands resting on the top fold, and she watched her granddaughter walk back to her seat at the table and sit down and open her field notebook and pick up her pen. She thought: *sixty-two years is a long time to carry a name alone, and now I am not carrying it alone, and she is angry, and she has the right to be angry, and she will ask me the rest, and I will answer.*

She thought: *that is what comes next.*

The chair called the next item. The room moved forward. Outside, the October wind pressed against the government building's windows with a sound like breath held and slowly, carefully, released.
