The mound rose out of the field's edge without announcement, the ground thickening and lifting until Tessa was standing on something built by hands. She had known this mound her whole life. She knew the particular give of the soil at its southern face, where the earth was darker and held moisture longer than the surrounding field. She knew the oaks at its base had grown their roots around it rather than through it, as if even the trees had learned to keep a respectful distance.

She stood at the eastern edge now, where the slope began, and the gorget lay in her open hands.

The light was going. October light went fast here. One moment the sky held a thin orange line above the treeline and then it was simply gone, and the world became shadow and the smell of turned earth and the last of the year's corn drying in the adjacent field. She could hear the creek somewhere below, running low after the dry summer. Somewhere across the water, far enough to be almost nothing, an axe was working through wood. That sound had been in the world her whole life. She no longer heard it as anything except time passing.

The gorget was heavier than it looked. It always was. She had thought that for years, lifting it from its cloth before each ceremony, feeling the weight settle in her palms. The shell was white and worn smooth at its edges, the suspension hole drilled clean through, the cord of tanned deerskin threaded through and knotted where Matta's fingers had knotted it in those last months when he knew he was counting his actions. He had placed it around her neck himself, his hands slow and deliberate. She had been perhaps nine years old, standing in his workroom with the smell of pipe clay in the air, and he had not explained why.

*You know what this is*, he had said. Not a question.

She had known. She had watched him wear it for as long as she could remember.

The face of it caught the last of the light. The engraved coil at its center, a serpent she had always thought, though Matta said it was older than any name they had for it, picked up the angle of the dusk and for a moment the lines seemed to move. She had seen that happen before, in firelight, at ceremonies. She was a grown woman now, not a child. The mound's soil was still dark under her nails from the digging she had done at first light, her back aching from too long crouching. She knew better than to call the movement alive. Knowing better did not make the movement stop.

She had worn the gorget at every ceremony since Matta placed it around her neck. He had not told her outright that she would lead those ceremonies. He had told her to watch, to learn the order of the words and the direction of the offerings and the way you held the pipe when you spoke to the fire and held it differently when you spoke to the water. He had been teaching her for years and he had not slowed down, and she understood now, standing here with the gorget in her hands and the pit open at her feet, that he had been teaching her because he had known how little time was left.

He had not said so. That was not his way. He had simply kept teaching, pressing the knowledge into her hands the way you press clay into a mold. Firmly, without gaps, without asking whether the mold was ready.

She thought about what it meant to be given something you had not asked for and could not put down.

The gorget was that. It was the visible form of everything Matta had pressed into her: the pipe-making, the name-list, the oral map of the ground, the knowledge of what lay in the mound and where and why. She had received all of it. She had held it carefully. She was here now to do what Matta had told her must be done when the time came.

She had not told anyone she was coming here tonight. Her neighbor Paka's fire was visible from the western slope, a warm orange square in the darkening field, and she did not turn to look. She did not want Paka to come out and ask her what she was doing. The explaining would require her to say her brother's name, one of them anyway, and she was not ready for that yet.

Two brothers. Two different kinds of leaving.

Nawan had gone north in the spring, six months ago, toward the British posts with three other men from the settlement, following the rumors that ran along the trade routes like water finding its level. She did not know if he had reached wherever he was going. She had not heard from him since the mud dried on the road after the thaw, and she had stopped expecting to hear. Stopping the expectation was not the same as stopping the hope. He had made his position clear before he left, in the flat, tired voice he used when he had already argued something to exhaustion and was only repeating the conclusion. *The mound is old thinking.* He had not said it cruelly. He had said it the way you state a fact about weather. *The ground doesn't protect anyone. The names don't protect anyone. We need other kinds of protection now.*

Thomas had made the same calculation by a different road. He had not left the county. He had simply left everything else. He cut his hair short at the sides, put on a dark wool coat with pewter buttons, attended the Anglican chapel at Port Tobacco, went by an English name among the English and said nothing when they used it. She had passed him on the road three weeks ago and he had looked through her the way you look through glass. She stopped walking and he did not. The space between them opened and widened and then he was gone around the bend, and she stood there with her hands at her sides, understanding that this was what he had chosen and that he had chosen it completely.

He had warned her before the choice hardened into habit. *Stop the ceremonies*, he had said. *Crow's Foot kept them and he is dead in the county jail. I will not watch you follow him.* He had not been wrong about Crow's Foot. He had not been wrong about the risk. She thought about that now, standing at the mound's edge. Thomas's fear was reasonable. The English constable who had appeared at the settlement three days ago and read his order aloud from horseback, his horse shifting its weight in the mud while he read, had been the visible form of a power that did not require her agreement to act.

She had buried the sacred objects the morning after the constable's visit. The pipe bowls, the ceremony bundle, the things that could be named in a court order. She had not buried the gorget then. She had not been ready.

She was ready now.

The mound was silent the way things are silent when they are full rather than empty. The earth here held things. Matta's pipe bowl, three layers down, the one he had placed himself when she was a child and he had brought her here at dawn and made her stand at the edge and watch him dig with the iron-bladed hatchet he kept wrapped in cloth and never used for anything else. *Watch*, he had said. *You need to know where it is.* And below that, older still. She did not know exactly what, only that Matta had told her there were things here that went back further than his grandfather's grandfather, that the mound was not their making but had been given into their keeping, and that the keeping was the most important thing a family could do.

She had believed him. She had always believed him. That was not the source of her difficulty.

Her difficulty was this: she was the last one who knew all of it. Not the oldest person in the community, but the only living person who held the full name-list and the full map of what lay in the ground here. Matta had been the last one before her. He had carried it from his grandmother, who had carried it from hers, and the chain had come down to Tessa because there was no one else it could have gone to. Nawan had refused it, not with cruelty, but with a flat, final clarity she had understood even as it hurt her. *I will not carry a thing I cannot put down.* Thomas had set it down without saying so, which was somehow worse. He had once known the burial names. She had heard him recite them as a boy, following Matta's lead. Somewhere between then and the wool coat with the pewter buttons, he had chosen to forget.

She closed her hands around the gorget.

The deerskin cord was soft from years of Matta's handling, the knot at the suspension hole worn almost flat by his fingers before hers. Along the shell's edge, where it had rested against Matta's chest for twenty years, the surface held a faint shine that was not the shell's natural color. A body-oil sheen, worked in over years, that she had noticed once in good light and had not been able to stop noticing since. Matta was in the gorget now, in that shine. That was what she was putting in the ground.

The axe sound across the water had stopped. The creek ran on. The last light was fully gone and the sky above the treeline held the deep blue that comes just before dark, the blue that has no name in English and that she had heard Matta call by a word she had never learned to say correctly.

She stepped onto the slope.

She did not go straight to the opening. She stopped at the flat stone Matta had used as a threshold marker, a piece of gray sandstone no bigger than a man's chest, half-buried in the slope, its upper face worn smooth by hands and weather and time. She had no idea how old it was. Matta had not known either. He had only said: *step over it, not on it.* She stepped over it.

The smell of the earth hit her before she reached the pit. Damp and close and faintly mineral, with something underneath that was older than damp, a darkness in the smell the way a root cellar smells different from a field, carrying the cool of depths that the sun has never touched. She crouched at the edge and set the gorget down on the flat ground beside her, cord coiled, and looked at it in the last of the sky's light.

The shell was pale. Not white. The years had given it a warmth, a faint honey color in the thicker parts, a near-translucence at the thinned edges where the artisan had cut close. She did not know who had made it. Matta had not known either, or had known and not told her. What he had told her was that it had come from a shell the size of a man's fist, a whelk from the coast, traded inland through hands she could not count, and that by the time it reached the family it had already been old. The carving on the face was a coiled figure, not quite a serpent, not quite a circle, something between the two, with a small eye near the center that caught light differently from the rest of the surface. She had traced it so many times that her fingertip knew the groove before it arrived there.

She picked the gorget up and turned it over.

The back was plain except for the suspension hole and the faint parallel scratches where the cord had worn against the shell over years of hanging. She ran her thumb along those scratches. They were Matta's, all of them, the marks of his ceremonies, his years of wearing, added to whatever marks the gorget had carried before it came to him. She had not yet added herself to the shell. She had worn it at ceremonies, standing beside Matta while he taught her the order of things, but she had been too careful to press against it the way a person does when they forget to be careful. The shine on the edge was his. She was putting his shine in the ground.

She turned the gorget over again, face up.

*What are you*, she said aloud. Not a question she expected answered. More the kind of thing you say to something you have known long enough that speaking to it is just another form of attention.

The creek ran below. A frog started and stopped.

She thought about what she was surrendering. Not the shell. She had made her peace with that. Those things lived in her memory now, and her memory would be the vessel for them until she could pass them to someone else, and that was the nature of things carried in memory: they were not transferable by object alone, they required the telling. Matta had told her that. He had said it plainly. *I cannot give you what I know. I can only give you time to learn it yourself.* He had given her years. She had used them as well as she could.

What she was surrendering was the object's ability to teach.

She had not thought of it that way until this moment. The thought arrived with the particular clarity of things understood too late to be useful. The gorget, worn at ceremony, said something to everyone who saw it. It said: *this person stands in a line that goes back further than you can see, and the line is unbroken, and the line is here.* Without the gorget she could still say those words. But words were thinner than objects. Objects were evidence in a way that words were not. She had learned that from Matta too, though he had not said it in those terms. He had only said, *show them the work*, meaning the pipes, meaning the clay, meaning the thing made by hands that could be held and examined and could not be argued with the way a claim could be argued with.

She was putting that evidence in the ground.

She was doing it anyway.

Because Matta had been right about the other thing too, the harder thing: houses burned. She had seen one. A cooking fire in a dry August, nothing left by morning but foundation stones and a smell that clung to the settlement for a week. The mound had been here longer than any house she could name, longer than any living person's memory of houses, longer probably than the language she was thinking in. The ground keeps what you give it. Not the meaning. Not the story. But the object. The evidence. The fact of the shell, carved and worn and placed by someone who knew what she was doing and why.

She would have to trust that.

She would have to trust that the object and the story could survive separately, the object in the ground, the story above it, passed from mouth to mouth, and that someday, if the story thinned or broke, the object would still be there, waiting to be found by someone who would know how to ask what it meant.

She picked up the gorget and held it in both hands, cord hanging, and began to speak the names.

She started with the oldest ones she knew, the names Matta had given her in order, the ones that went back past his grandfather's grandfather to the edge of what the family's memory held. Some of them she knew only as sounds, names whose bearers had no stories attached anymore, only the names themselves, worn smooth by repetition into something almost abstract, like stones in a streambed. She said them anyway. The saying was the point. The saying kept the chain.

She moved forward through the generations, through names that had stories, through the ones she knew well. The woman who had carried the family south. The man who had kept the pipe-making craft alive through a winter when the clay froze and the fuel ran short. The woman whose name Matta had always said with a pause before it, a half-breath of particular respect he had never explained and she had never asked about. She said each name once, clearly, into the cooling air above the pit.

She came to Matta.

She stopped.

She said his name twice. Once for the record, once because she wanted to. She had watched him die and she had held the gorget in her hands at his burial and she had not cried then because there had been too much to do, too many people who needed the ceremony to go correctly. She had done the ceremony correctly. Now she was here and there was no one watching and she said his name twice and the second time her voice was smaller than the first.

She waited until her voice was steady again.

Then she said her own name. *Tessa.* It felt strange in her mouth in this context, the way your own face looks strange in still water. Familiar and not, at the same time. She said it clearly. She was part of the chain. She was not going to leave herself out of it out of some misplaced humility. Matta had told her: *you are not keeping this for yourself. You are keeping it for the ones who come after. That means you have to be in it.*

Then she stopped, because she had come to the hard part. Two brothers, two refusals, and she had to decide what to do with each of them.

She thought about Thomas first, because Thomas was the one she had to think about carefully. He had informed on the ceremonies. She did not know this for certain. She had no proof, only the timing of the constable's visit and the fact that Thomas had known the ceremony schedule and had chosen survival by a particular method. He had warned her four days before the constable came. She had thought the warning was love. Now she thought it was both love and the other thing, and that Thomas himself probably could not have separated them.

She said his name.

*Thomas.* She said it in the old way, not the English way, the way Matta had said it when Thomas was a boy and had not yet cut his hair. It felt like reaching for something that had moved. He was her brother and he was part of the chain. She placed his name there not as forgiveness, she was not ready for forgiveness and might never be, but as a fact. He had been taught the names. He had sat at Matta's knee and heard the same list she had heard. He carried the knowledge whether he used it or not, the way you carry the shape of a door in your hands after years of opening it, even after the door is gone. The mound would hold his name. It did not require his consent.

Then Nawan.

She thought about his face the morning he left, the flat set of his jaw, the way he had not looked back at the settlement when the road bent north. She thought about what he had said. *The mound is old thinking.* She thought about how he had not said it cruelly.

She said his name.

*Nawan.* She said it once, firmly, into the air above the pit, the way you set something down on a table and step back from it. Not a prayer. Not a plea. A placement. He was part of the chain whether he wanted to be or not, and the mound would hold his name whether he was here to hear it or not, and she was not doing this for him. She was doing it for the chain, for the thing that had to be kept intact even when one of its links had walked away and might not come back.

The frog started again below, found its rhythm, kept it.

She lifted the gorget and held it for a moment at the level of her chest, where she had seen it hang for as long as she could remember, and then she lowered it into the pit.

The gorget touched the earth.

She did not let go immediately. She kept her hands around it, the shell cool now in the evening air, the cord coiled beside it on the dark soil, and she felt the cold of the pit come up through her fingers. The compacted earth of the lower layers pressed back against her knuckles, firm and slightly greasy, different from the topsoil above it. Matta had told her about that. *The old earth holds itself differently*, he had said. *You can feel where the work has been done.* He had been right. She could feel it. The soil here had been carried and placed and carried again, basket-load by basket-load, by people whose names she had just spoken into the air, and the density of it was not only physical.

She opened her hands.

The gorget lay in the basin she had cut, a shallow oval just large enough, the walls clean. She had done that part carefully, using the iron hoe for the first several inches and then her hands for the last, the way Matta had taught her to work near anything that mattered. Rough tools to the depth of indifference, then hands only, then patience. The shell's face caught a last angle of the failing light through the trees. The engraved lines were worn smooth at the center from Matta's years of touching. The suspension hole he had drilled himself before she was born. The edge that had rubbed against his sternum until the shell had taken on the faint oil-shine of his skin. It looked small in the pit. It had never looked small when he was wearing it.

She sat back on her heels and looked at it for a moment without speaking.

Below it, she knew, were the other layers. Matta's pipe bowl, wrapped in its bark cloth, placed by his hands in the years when his hands were still good. And below that, the oilskin bundle, Okwi's document, the petition, the thing that had been written down and buried because the people who wrote it had understood, as she understood now, that paper trusted to a house was paper waiting to burn. Okwi was a name on the list, not a face she had ever seen, one of the names Matta had said with a particular stillness, the kind of stillness that meant the name carried something heavier than ordinary memory. She had never asked what the document said. She had only been told that it mattered and that it was there and that someday, if the right person found it, it would speak for itself.

She was adding a third layer. Below Matta. Below Okwi. *Tessa.*

The frog in the wet ground below the mound had found its rhythm again after her digging disturbed it. It called and waited and called. Somewhere north, far enough to be a sound without direction, an axe was working at timber. Steady, unhurried. She had stopped noticing it the way you stop noticing a sore tooth when you have other things to attend to.

She took the small tobacco pouch from her belt. Trade wool, the color of dried blood, with a brass tinkler on the drawstring that Matta had tied there and she had not wanted to undo. She shook a pinch of tobacco into her palm and held it over the pit and let it fall. It scattered across the gorget's face and caught in the engraved lines.

Then she spoke the names again.

Not all of them this time. Only the ones that mattered most to the act she had just performed, the ones whose objects were already in the ground below her, the ones whose hands had touched the same soil. She said Okwi's name, which she knew only as a name, no face attached, no story beyond the document and the decision to bury it. She said the name of the woman who had carried the family south, whose name Matta had always placed at the beginning of the second part of the list, the hinge between the names-as-sounds and the names-as-stories. She said Matta's name again, once. She had already given him the double-saying. This was a completion, a closing of the circle she had opened when she first began.

Then she said her own name.

*Tessa.*

It still felt strange. She thought it probably always would, and that this was correct. You should feel the strangeness of placing yourself in a chain that went back past the edge of memory. It should not feel easy or natural. It should feel like what it was: a claim, and a responsibility, and an acknowledgment that she would someday be only a name herself, a sound worn smooth, passed forward by someone whose face she would never see.

She stayed with that for a moment.

Then she said Thomas's name again. The second saying, the one below the ceremony's structure.

*Thomas.*

She said it the old way again. She said it to the gorget, to the tobacco scattered across its face, to the layers below it and the sky above it. She said it to the man who had passed her on the road without stopping, who had warned her and then used the warning as a door to walk through. She did not know if she would ever be able to separate the love from the other thing. She thought perhaps she was not meant to. The mound did not ask you to arrive with clean hands. It asked only that you arrive.

Then Nawan's name. The second saying.

*Nawan.*

She said it clearly. She said it to the gorget, to the tobacco scattered across its face, to the layers below it and the sky above it and the frog still calling in the wet ground and the axe still working in the north. She said it to the mound itself, which had been holding names longer than she had been alive and would hold them longer than she would be alive and did not require his consent to hold his.

He had walked north on a road that bent away from everything she was doing here. He had said *the mound is old thinking* and he had not said it cruelly, which was almost worse, because cruelty would have given her something to push against. He had said it the way you state a fact about weather. He had chosen the world that was coming instead of the world that was here, and she did not think he was entirely wrong about which one was larger. She thought he might even survive better in it than she would.

But he was part of the chain.

He had been taught the names. He had sat at Matta's knee and heard the same list she had heard, in the same order, with the same pauses at the difficult parts. He carried the knowledge whether he used it or not, the way you carry the shape of a door in your hands after years of opening it, even after the door is gone. She could not take that from him by leaving his name out. She could only refuse to pretend the chain had a gap where he was.

The mound would hold his name now. Below the gorget, below Matta's pipe bowl, below Okwi's document, in the dark and the compacted earth and the faint charcoal flecks of old burning that she had felt under her fingernails when she dug. It would hold his name alongside hers, alongside all the others, without judgment, without asking whether he had earned it or wanted it or would have refused it if he had been standing here.

She had not done it for him.

She had done it because the chain had to be kept intact, and because she was the one keeping it, and because she had decided, sitting here with the gorget in the ground and the tobacco scattered and the cooling air coming down through the oaks, that the chain did not end with the living. It included the ones who had walked away. It included the ones who had chosen erasure. It included Nawan, who had looked north on a bending road and not looked back, and Thomas, who had looked south toward Port Tobacco and its English chapel and chosen that horizon instead. Neither of them would know she had said their names here. Neither of them would have wanted her to.

She reached for the first basket of earth.

The earth in the basket was still faintly warm from where she had carried it against her side on the walk up. She took a handful first, not to fill the depression, but to feel what she was working with. Compacted, slightly greasy between her fingers, darker than the topsoil she had set aside. Older soil. The kind that had been moved before, carried in someone else's basket, pressed down by someone else's hands. She could feel the faint grit of old charcoal in it, small as pepper.

She set the cedar bark down first.

Matta had cut it before his hands had gotten bad, a strip as long as her forearm, still faintly resinous, curled at the edges where it had dried. He had not asked what it was for. He had handed it to her and gone back to his clay, and she had folded it into the basket under her sleeping mat and not spoken of it until now. She laid it over the gorget's face, not covering it entirely, just bridging it, the way Matta had shown her you bridge a pipe bowl's air chamber before the final sealing so the heat draws evenly. That was the motion he had drilled into her hands: not a slam, not a press, but a setting-down. Weight distributed. No gaps.

The cedar bark settled against the gorget's carved face and she held it there for a moment with two fingers, feeling the shell's slight coolness through the bark.

She let go.

Then she began to fill.

She worked with both hands, the way Matta had taught her to seal a pipe bowl's air chamber, not packing from the top but building from the sides, pressing inward and then down, so the fill supported itself rather than collapsing toward the center. Her fingers found the rhythm quickly. The soil was more granular than clay, less responsive, but it had its own logic: it wanted to compact along the grain of what was already there, to follow the layers that had been laid down before hers. She let it. She did not fight the ground's memory of itself.

She did not hurry.

The first basket emptied. She reached for the second without looking up, her eyes on the depression as it rose, the cedar bark disappearing under the fill, the gorget gone now, gone from sight, gone from reach, given over entirely to the dark and the weight of the earth above it. She pressed the fill down in small sections, working her way around the perimeter the way you work around a pot's rim, checking for softness, for any place where the earth had not settled. She found one spot near the eastern edge where the fill had bridged without packing. She worked her fingers into it and filled the gap and pressed again.

The second basket emptied.

She smoothed the surface with her palm, moving in slow circles, feeling for high spots and low. The soil had a slightly different color from the surrounding mound, darker, damper, but that would fade. In a season it would be indistinguishable. In ten seasons it would be nothing. The mound would absorb it the way it had absorbed everything else: the pipe bowl below, the document below that, the older things below those, all the layers she had felt under her nails when she dug, each one a decision someone had made and then covered over and then left to the ground's keeping.

She set the empty baskets aside.

Then she placed both palms flat against the surface.

The earth was cool now, cooler than the air, the way ground gets in late afternoon when the light has gone low and the shadows have been sitting on the soil for hours. She felt the slight give of the topmost fill under her hands, not soft, not unstable, just not yet fully settled, still adjusting to its new arrangement. She could feel the faint roughness of the soil against her palms, the occasional small stone, the compressed grain of the older layers beneath the new.

She waited.

She was not sure what she was waiting for. Not a sign. Not a response. She thought she believed something adjacent to what Matta had believed, that the act of pressing your hands against the ground and waiting was itself the thing, the ceremony complete in the waiting, the way a pipe bowl's seal is complete not when you press it but when you hold the pressure long enough for the clay to remember the shape.

She held the pressure.

Her pulse was in her hands. She could feel it, the small, steady knock of it against the packed earth, the way it moved through her palms and into the soil and disappeared. She stayed with that. The frog was still calling somewhere below the mound's eastern face, the same two-note call, patient and unhurried, indifferent to what she was doing. The axe in the north had stopped. The light through the oaks was amber now, almost gone.

She thought about the gorget below her hands. Not the shell itself. She had already let go of the shell. That was done. She thought about the weight of it, Matta's years of wearing it, the way it had moved when he moved, the way she had watched it catch the fire at every ceremony she had attended since she was old enough to stand still through one. She had held it in her hands through years of apprenticeship. She would not hold it again. Her hands would remember the shape of it, the smooth edges, the slight roughness of the engraved lines, the cool of the shell in winter and the warmth of it after it had rested against skin. The remembering would take a moment each time, and the moment would be its own small ceremony, repeated until it wasn't.

She could no longer feel her pulse against the ground.

She was not sure when it had stopped, whether the earth had absorbed it or her hands had gone numb or the pulse had simply quieted below the threshold of feeling. That was the marker Matta had given her, years ago, when she had asked how you know when the seal is finished. *When you can no longer feel yourself in it.* She had thought he was talking about the clay. She had been young then, her first year of watching him work, and she had thought he was talking about the clay.

She lifted her hands.

The surface of the mound held the faint impressions of her palms for a moment, two shallow shapes in the soil, fingers slightly spread, the heel of each hand pressed deeper than the fingers. Then the earth settled, the impressions softened, and they were gone.

She sat back on her heels and looked at the place where the gorget was.

There was nothing to see. A slightly darker patch of soil, a little smoother than the surrounding surface, but already the light was too low to read the color clearly. By morning it would look like the rest of the mound. By the end of the week she would have to remember exactly where she had dug to find the spot again, and she was not certain she would try.

She picked up the iron hoe she had used to open the ground and set it in the empty basket. She picked up the tobacco pouch, lighter now, nearly empty, and tucked it inside her wool sash. She picked up the baskets, one nested inside the other.

She did not look back at the mound as she walked down.

She told herself this was the same discipline Matta had taught her for finishing any piece of work: you set it down, you step away, you do not keep handling it after it is done. The handling after was how you ruined things. The handling after was how you introduced doubt into a seal that had been good.

She told herself this, and she walked down through the oaks toward the creek, and the frog was still calling, and the light was almost gone, and the mound held everything she had given it, and her name was in the ground now alongside all the others, and she did not look back.

She reached the bottom of the slope where the oaks thinned and the ground leveled into the creek bottom. The last light was horizontal here, coming through the gaps between the trees in long, flat bars that lit the grass without warming it. She stopped.

Her hands were dirty. She had known they would be dirty, she had dug with them, pressed with them, worked the soil back with her fingers, but she looked at them now as though she had forgotten. The knuckles were dark with the mound's earth, that particular color, almost greasy, the older fill from below the surface layer. Matta had told her once that the color came from the generations of ceremony performed on the mound's face, the fires, the tobacco smoke, the oils from hands and bodies laid against it over time, all of it worked down into the soil until the soil itself held a record of having been touched.

She did not think that was a story told to make the work feel larger than it was. Not anymore.

She set the baskets down and crouched at the creek's edge and put her hands in the water. It was cold, colder than the air, which had already dropped since the light began to fail. The current was slow here, backing up slightly behind a root mass on the far bank, and she could feel the fine silt of the bottom moving against her fingertips. She worked the mound's earth from her knuckles, from the creases of her palms, from under her nails. The water ran dark for a moment, then clear.

She looked at her clean hands.

She was the only person alive who knew all of it. Not the oldest person in the community, but the only living person who held the full name-list and the full map of what lay in the ground here. Matta had been the last one before her. The chain had come down to Tessa because there was no one else it could have gone to. Nawan had refused it with words. Thomas had refused it with silence, which was its own kind of answer. She had not been asked whether she wanted to be the last one standing with it. She had simply been the one still standing.

She dried her hands on the inside of her sash and stood.

The creek made its sound. The frog was still calling somewhere behind her, up the slope, patient as weather.

She thought about her brothers.

Not with anger. What she had was simpler and harder: she missed them both. She missed Nawan the way you miss a tooth that has been pulled, with a sudden sharp awareness at unexpected moments, when she reached for something that was no longer there. She had expected him to be part of this. She had imagined, in the years before he left, that when the time came for the gorget to go into the ground, he would be standing at the mound's edge beside her. They would say the names together, and the weight of it would be divided between two pairs of hands.

She missed Thomas differently. Thomas was three miles south and might as well have gone north with Nawan for all the use of his nearness. She missed the brother he had been before the choice calcified, the boy who had broken their grandfather's clay pipe by accident and cried about it for an hour, who had known the burial names and recited them with Matta on winter evenings, who had understood what the mound was and had not yet decided that understanding was a liability.

Both their names were in the ground now.

She had spoken them deliberately, with intention, in the place where they would be held by the oldest witnesses she knew. She had placed them there because they were her brothers and because the mound was safer than any house, any body, any living memory, and she had wanted them to be somewhere safe even if they did not want it themselves.

They would not have wanted it.

She knew that. She had known it when she spoke their names and she had spoken them anyway, and that was the part she would have to carry. Not the speaking itself, but the fact that she had weighed their refusals against her love for them and her love had been heavier, and she had let it be heavier, and now their names were in the earth of this place and they would never know it and she could not take it back.

She picked up the baskets.

The light was gone from the oaks now. The sky above the treeline held a thin strip of pale color, the last of it, and below that the trees were already dark. She could find her way without light. She had walked this ground since she was small enough to hold Matta's hand on the path, had learned the slope of it and the roots and the soft place near the second oak where the ground was unreliable after rain. She did not need the light.

She thought: *will I tell them?*

Not whether she should. She understood this much: telling Nawan would require finding him first, which might not be possible. Telling Thomas would require him to stop and look at her, which he had decided not to do. There might be no telling. There might be only this, the names in the ground, the knowledge held in her alone, the chain intact and invisible, running through her the way water runs through rock.

She walked into the trees. The creek sound fell behind her. The basket handles were smooth against her palm, worn to the shape of Matta's grip over years of use, and she held them loosely, the way he had taught her to hold finished work. Present but not gripping. Not trying to keep what was already done.

The questions stayed with her as she walked. Not comfortable questions. Not ones that would resolve themselves by morning or by the week's end or perhaps ever. It was possible she would never tell either of them. It was possible that their names in the ground would be the only record of what she felt for them, the only place she had been able to say it without the words getting in the way, without their refusals standing between her and the saying. The mound would hold it. The mound held everything given to it. And in a hundred years, or two hundred, or five, when the names above ground had thinned and the houses had rotted and the language had changed past recognition, the mound would still hold their names alongside hers, and none of them would need to have agreed to it.

She thought: *that may have to be enough.*

The tree line closed behind her. The mound was behind her in the dark, and the gorget was below the mound's surface, and her brothers' names were below that, and all the names were below that, going down through the strata to the oldest ones, the ones she could barely pronounce, the ones that were more sound than meaning now. She carried the empty baskets and the nearly empty tobacco pouch and the iron hoe, and she walked toward the lights of the settlement through the dark, and the questions of her brothers walked beside her, and she did not have answers for them, and she kept walking.
