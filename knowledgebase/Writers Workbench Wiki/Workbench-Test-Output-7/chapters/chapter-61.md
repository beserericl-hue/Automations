The river gave back the sky before the sky knew what color it wanted to be.

Yanu stood at the water's edge with her feet in the cold mud and her arms at her sides and watched the light come. The reeds on the far bank were black sticks against gray. A muskrat crossed the shallows without hurrying, its wake a slow V that reached the bank and dissolved. She had been awake since the birds first moved in the trees above the house, which was before first light, which was when she always woke, which her husband Ranu said was because she had made a bargain with the morning that the rest of them had not been told about.

She had not corrected him. Ranu's explanations were usually better than the truth.

The pendant lay against her collarbone, cool copper on warm skin, the weight of it so familiar she felt it only when something else made her feel it. She did not reach up to touch it. She watched the river.

A pair of herons stood in the shallows forty paces upstream, grey and still as smoke that had forgotten to rise. She had been watching herons on this stretch of water for as long as she could remember and she knew their stillness was not patience — it was hunger made motionless, which was a different thing. One of them struck. Came up with a small fish that caught the first light and threw it back silver before disappearing down the long throat. The other heron did not move.

South wind coming. She could feel it in the way the reeds held — not stiff, not slack, but with a faint lean to them, just enough. The sky above the eastern tree line had gone from grey to a pale color she had no word for, the color between grey and yellow that only existed for a few breaths each morning. The clouds that had sat on the horizon since yesterday were already breaking apart from the south, which meant the warmth would come by midday and the wind with it. Clear. Warm. South wind by midday.

She was right about weather the way other people were right about their own names — not because she had thought it out but because she simply knew, the way you know the shape of your own hand in the dark.

The kingfisher arrived without announcement, dropping from somewhere above the bank to take its branch over the water. It sat with its beak pointed downstream and its whole body forward, a small blue-green fire that the morning had not quite lit yet. It did not move. It was waiting for something the river had not yet offered.

She watched it.

The heron upstream called once — a flat, tearing sound, nothing like a song — and lifted from the shallows on wings that seemed too large for the body they belonged to. It crossed directly overhead, close enough that she could hear the slow pull of air through feathers, and banked south over the tree line and was gone.

Taya.

The thought arrived the way the heron's call had: without warning, without invitation, simply there. She had not been thinking about Taya. She had been thinking about the wind and the clouds and the kingfisher and the color of the sky. And then she was thinking about nothing else.

Taya had liked the herons. That was the whole of it — a small fact, a child's preference, the kind of thing that does not seem worth remembering until the child is gone and then becomes the thing you cannot stop remembering. She had been two years old. She had pointed at a heron on this same stretch of bank and said a word that was not quite the word for bird and not quite the word for big and was somehow both. Yanu had laughed. She could still hear herself laughing.

She pressed her hand flat against her sternum, against the pendant, without meaning to.

The copper was warm now from her skin. It was a small thing, the pendant — a disc no wider than two fingers, with a hole punched near the top where the cord passed through. The trader who had made it had come through the settlement in the second summer after Taya died. He had come from the north, following the river corridors the way traders did, carrying objects in a wrapped bundle that he opened at the fire in the way of a man who knows what he has and is not in a hurry. He had spoken a language she did not know well, something from farther north, and the word he used for the copper was a word she had never heard before and could not now remember, though she had tried.

Ranu had traded for the pendant — two deer hides and a pot of rendered fat — because Taya had reached for it. That was his explanation. Their daughter had reached for the bright thing and so he had given what the trader asked. Yanu had said he was foolish to spend two hides on a child's whim. Ranu had said the child was right to want beautiful things. They had both been right, which was how most of their arguments ended.

She stopped herself. That was not right. Taya had been gone more than a year by the time the trader came. She had not reached for anything. That was a story Ranu told, the way he smoothed things — he had bought it because it was beautiful and because Taya had been the kind of child who reached for beautiful things, and somewhere in the retelling the memory had become the fact. She had let him tell it. It was a better story than the truth, which was that he had stood at the trader's mat and looked at the copper pendant and thought of their daughter and spent the hides before he had decided to.

She had worn the pendant every day since. Not for herself — she had understood this only gradually, the way you understand a word you have been using wrongly, with a small shock of correction. She wore it for Taya. The pendant was Taya's, made in the season of Taya's absence, traded into the family in the shape of what Taya had been. That it now hung around Yanu's neck was a kind of custody, not ownership. She was keeping it for someone who could no longer keep it herself.

The kingfisher dove. Hit the water and came up with something small and silver and returned to its branch in a single motion, the whole sequence so fast it seemed impossible, like a trick of the light. It sat with the fish crosswise in its beak and did not move.

The river moved. The reeds leaned south. The pale color between grey and yellow was gone now, replaced by something warmer, and the first real light was coming through the trees on the eastern bank, laying itself across the water in long strips that shifted as the current shifted. A dog barked once somewhere in the settlement behind her. She heard a child's voice, high and brief, then nothing.

Her children were waking.

She had four of them now — the three boys and the girl who had come after Taya, the girl who was four years old and who reached for things the way Taya had reached for things, and who Yanu sometimes watched with a fear she could not name and a love so large it frightened her. She did not compare them. She tried not to compare them. But the reaching — the way a child reaches for something bright with both hands and absolute certainty — that was the same, and sometimes it stopped her breath.

She turned from the river.

The settlement was coming awake behind her, smoke beginning to rise from the nearest house, the smell of it — woodsmoke and something charred, last night's maize left too long in the pot — reaching her on the still air. The mud under her feet was cold and held the shapes of her footprints precisely, the way river mud does in the early morning before the sun reaches it.

She looked back once at the kingfisher. It was still on its branch, still holding the fish crosswise, still waiting for something she could not see.

She thought: the day will be clear. Warm. South wind by midday.

She thought: Taya.

She walked back toward the smoke.

The fire had died to coals while she was gone. She fed it — dry sticks first, then a split piece of hickory, then another — and it came back without argument, the way a good fire does when the bed is right. She blew once at the base and the coals brightened orange and the flame caught and she sat back on her heels and let it build.

The clay pot was where she had left it, set to one side of the hearth on flat stones. She filled it from the water jar — two trips to the river that morning if she wanted enough for the meal and for washing — and set it over the fire. The maize had been soaking since last night, cracked and softened. She added it to the pot with a piece of dried fish she took from the rack above the smoke, and a handful of late hickory nuts she had shelled two days before and kept in a folded piece of bark. It was a good pot. She had made it herself, the clay mixed with crushed shell, the walls thin enough to heat fast. She had pressed cord around the rim when the clay was still soft, more from habit than intention, the way her hands knew the pattern before her mind asked for it.

The oldest boy came out first. He was twelve, and he came out the way twelve-year-olds do — not quite awake, one hand pressing his hair flat on one side where it had been against the mat, blinking at the fire with an expression of mild grievance at the morning. His name was Sena. He sat down across from her without speaking, which she appreciated.

Then the second boy, Kato, who was nine and who was never not talking. He came out at a half-run and sat down too close to the fire and she moved him back with one hand without looking at him and he scooted back and began telling her about a dream involving a very large fish and a canoe that had a hole in it, and she said yes and mm and is that right while she stirred the pot.

The third boy was seven. His name was Piri. He came out slowly, still carrying his sleeping mat, and set it down beside the fire and lay down on it again with his eyes closed. She left him. He would be up by the time the food was ready. He had always been this way — not lazy, just requiring a longer negotiation with the morning than other people.

Then the girl.

She was four, and her name was Wasi — one of Yanu's grandchildren by blood, given into the household when her own mother died of fever the previous spring, though she had been with them long enough now that she had stopped being a guest and become simply theirs — and she came out of the house at a dead run and threw herself at Yanu's back with both arms and no warning, which was also how she had always been, even before the fever took her mother. Yanu caught her weight without spilling the pot, which was a skill she had developed over the past year through necessity. She settled Wasi onto her hip and let her look at the fire and Wasi said, immediately and with great urgency: "I'm hungry."

"I know," Yanu said.

"When is it ready?"

"When it's ready."

Wasi accepted this. She leaned her head against Yanu's shoulder and looked at the fire with the focused, proprietary interest of a child who has decided the fire is hers personally. Her hair smelled of sleep and woodsmoke and something sweet that Yanu could not account for. She was solid and warm and her weight on Yanu's hip was a specific and familiar weight, and Yanu held it.

Ranu came out last. He always did. He was not a man who hurried toward mornings. He came out with his hair loose and his eyes clear — he was one of those people who woke completely, no gradual return, just absent and then present — and he looked at the four children arranged around the fire in their various states of consciousness and he said: "The river was high last night. I heard it."

"It wasn't," Yanu said.

"I heard it moving fast."

"You heard Kato talking in his sleep."

Kato looked up from his fish-dream recitation, offended. "I don't talk in my sleep."

"You do," said Sena, without opening his eyes.

Ranu sat down on the log beside the fire and accepted a piece of bark with dried berries that Yanu handed him, because the food was not ready yet and he would eat the whole pot before it was if she did not give him something to work on first. He ate a berry and looked at the sky, which was lightening now to the east, the color of the inside of a clamshell, pale and faintly iridescent.

"There was a man," Ranu said, "who lived upriver, in the time of my father's father's father, who could tell when the river would rise by watching the herons."

"The herons don't tell you when the river rises," Sena said. "They move when it's already rising."

"He watched them before," Ranu said. "He watched the way they stood. There's a particular way a heron stands when it knows water is coming."

"How?" said Kato.

"It leans forward a little. Just the head. Like it's listening to something underground."

Yanu stirred the pot. She had heard this story before. She had heard most of Ranu's stories before, which was not his fault — he told them the way other people breathed, continuously and without apparent effort, and the supply did not seem to diminish. She was fond of this about him. She was also, at this particular moment of the morning, somewhat tired of it. Both things were true. They were usually both true.

"The man's name," Ranu said, "was—"

"Piri," Yanu said. "Sit up. The food is nearly ready."

Piri sat up from his mat with the slow, dignified movement of a person who has decided to rejoin the world on his own terms. He rubbed his eyes. He looked at the pot. He looked at the sky. He said: "Is it going to rain?"

"No," Yanu said. "South wind by midday. It'll be warm."

"How do you know?"

"The kingfisher was hunting from the low branch. It hunts from the high branch before rain."

Piri considered this. "What if it just wanted the low branch?"

"It didn't."

He accepted this too. Her children had learned, at various ages and through various small defeats, that when she said a thing about the weather she was right, and that arguing about it was a way of being wrong twice — once now and once when the weather came.

She ladled the maize into bowls — flat-bottomed clay bowls, mismatched, three of her own making and one that had belonged to her mother — and handed them around. Wasi took hers with both hands and the focused seriousness of someone receiving something precious. Kato immediately spilled a small amount on his knee and wiped it on the ground. Sena ate without looking at the bowl, watching the treeline to the east where the light was now coming through properly, the long strips of it she had seen on the water now reaching the settlement, laying themselves across the house walls and the packed earth of the yard.

Ranu was telling the heron story again. He had found his way back to it around the fish and the berries. The man upriver. The herons. The particular angle of the head. Yanu half-listened. She ate her own maize standing, because she always did, because sitting down to eat was something that happened after the children were settled and the fire was banked and the water jar was full, and she had not yet refilled the water jar.

The pendant swung forward as she leaned to add a stick to the fire. The copper caught the firelight — a brief, warm flash, amber and orange at once — and Wasi, who missed nothing, reached out one small hand and touched it.

"What is that?"

Yanu looked down at her. Wasi had her father's eyes — very dark, very direct — and she was looking at the pendant with the same expression she used on things she intended to understand completely.

"A pendant," Yanu said.

"I know it's a pendant." Wasi was four and had no patience for answers that restated the question. "What is it? Where did it come from?"

"From far away."

"How far?"

"Far enough that the man who brought it spoke a language I didn't know."

Wasi turned this over. "What language?"

"I don't know. That's the point of not knowing it."

Wasi reached for the pendant again, both hands this time, and Yanu let her hold it — the cord still around her own neck, Wasi's small hands cupped under the disc, tilting it toward the firelight. The copper was warm from Yanu's skin. It was a small thing, no wider than two fingers, with a punched hole at the top where the cord passed through, and in the firelight it held the color of late afternoon in high summer, the color of the river when the sun was on it directly.

"It's pretty," Wasi said, with the authority of a final judgment.

"Yes," Yanu said.

"Who did it belong to?"

Yanu looked at her daughter's hands — her grandchild's hands, she corrected herself, though the correction had grown harder to make with each passing month — small and certain, holding the pendant up to the light. The same reaching, the same certainty. She had watched these hands for a year and she still could not look at them without the other hands appearing alongside them, the ones that had held this same copper once and given it back.

"Your sister," she said. "It belonged to your sister."

Wasi went still. She was four and she knew about Taya the way children know about things that happened before they could understand them — as a shape, a weight in the air, a name that made the adults quiet. She looked at the pendant in her hands and then up at Yanu.

"Where is she?"

"In the ground," Yanu said. "On the high ground above the river. With the others."

Wasi looked at the pendant again. She turned it once in the firelight, watching the copper catch and release the warmth. Then she placed it carefully back against Yanu's chest, both hands settling it there with a deliberateness that was not like four years old at all, and went back to her maize.

Yanu stood very still for a moment.

Then she went to fill the water jar.

The morning was already warm. She had been right about the wind.

The trader had come in the second summer after Taya died.

Yanu remembered the season precisely because she had been counting them — not deliberately, not with marks on a post, but in the way you count things you cannot stop counting. One winter. One spring. One summer. She was still carrying the grief loose inside her, the way you carry water in a basket, knowing it will drain through and leave you carrying the wet weight of the basket itself.

He came from upriver, from the northwest, with two other men who did not speak either. They moved through the settlement in the unhurried way of people who had learned that arriving quietly was safer than arriving with noise. They had packs of folded hide, heavy enough to make them walk with a forward lean. They set up at the edge of the yard where the path met the open ground, and by midmorning half the settlement had drifted over to look.

Yanu had not drifted. She had work to do. She was splitting hickory for the smoking rack, and she kept splitting it, and she watched from where she was.

The trader was not a tall man. He was broad across the chest and moved with the deliberate economy of someone who had walked very far and intended to walk very far again. His clothing was different — the cut of it, the way it sat on his shoulders — and he wore two small copper discs at his ears that caught the light when he turned his head. He spoke to people through gestures and the occasional word that Yanu could not place in any language she knew. When someone showed him something they wanted to trade, he would hold it for a long time, turning it over, and his face gave nothing away.

He had copper. Not much — four or five pieces in a flat pouch of soft hide — but the kind of copper that had been worked by someone who knew what they were doing. Flat discs, a narrow strip beaten into a curve, a small figure that might have been a bird or a man with his arms out. The settlement had seen copper before, but not often, and not like this. Ranu had gone over immediately. Of course he had.

Yanu kept splitting hickory.

On the second day she went to look.

She told herself she was going because she was going anyway. She had been thinking about the copper discs at his ears. She had been thinking about how he had made them, or who had, and how far they had traveled to get here.

He was sitting cross-legged on a mat he had unrolled from his pack, and he had a small iron-hard tool in his hand — a punch, she thought, though she had never seen one shaped quite like it — and he was working a piece of copper flat against a smooth stone. The sound was a small, precise tapping. Not loud. Patient.

She watched from the edge of the gathered people.

The man looked up. He had a broad face, deeply weathered, with lines that cut deep at the corners of his eyes. He looked at the cord around her neck.

She was wearing the cord already, though there was nothing on it yet. She had made it from twisted plant fiber in the weeks after Taya died, thinking she would put something on it, some object that would be Taya's object, but she had not found the right thing and the cord had stayed empty. She wore it anyway. Perhaps because Taya had liked to reach for things at her neck.

The trader looked at the empty cord. Then he looked back at the copper in his hands.

He set down the piece he had been working and reached into the flat hide pouch and took out a small disc of raw copper — raw in the sense of unworked, smooth on one face, rough on the other, no larger than what fit in his palm. He held it up briefly, showing it to her. Not offering. Showing.

She nodded.

He began to work.

She did not leave. She should have — there was water to carry, and the smoking rack was not finished — but she stood and watched him work the copper against the stone, the small precise tapping, the way he turned the disc by fractions and tapped again, and she watched the shape emerge from it the way a fish emerges from deep water, slowly, and then all at once.

He was making it round. Properly round, not the rough circle you got from hammering without a guide. He had something — a thin rod of bone or antler, she could not tell — that he used to press a line around the edge, and the line was even, and the disc became a pendant.

He punched the hole at the top with a single clean strike. She flinched at the sound. He did not.

He held it up. The copper caught the afternoon light — it was late afternoon by then, she had lost the middle of the day entirely — and it was the color of the river when the sun was on it directly, amber and orange at once, warm in a way that seemed to come from inside the metal rather than from the light falling on it.

He offered it to her across the mat.

She took it. It was lighter than she expected. It was warm from his hands and from the work of making it, and it sat in her palm with a kind of settled weight, as if it had always been there and she had simply not been holding it yet.

She looked at him. She wanted to ask him something — she was not sure what — but they had no language in common, and the question was not one she could have shaped in words anyway. She wanted to ask him how he had known. He had not asked her anything. She had not told him about Taya. He had simply looked at the empty cord and made the pendant, the way you do something that is obvious once you see it needs doing.

She understood, holding it, that it was not made for her. It was made for the cord. For what the cord was waiting to hold. He had seen the empty cord and understood its purpose better than she had been able to say.

She threaded the cord through the hole herself, there in front of him, and put it on.

He watched. He made no particular expression. He picked up the piece he had been working before and went back to the small, patient tapping.

She took herself home and refilled the water jar and finished the smoking rack before dark, and the pendant swung against her chest the whole time, warm from her skin, settling into the hollow below her collarbone as if the hollow had been made for it.

He left on the third morning with his two quiet companions, back upriver, northwest, toward whatever country had produced the copper and the tool and the knowledge of how to use them. She watched him go from the yard. He did not look back. She had not expected him to.

She had thought, in the weeks after, that she would understand eventually what he had understood — what it was he had seen when he looked at the empty cord that made him reach for the copper without being asked. She had thought it was something learnable, some knowledge about grief that she did not yet have, and that time would give it to her.

Two summers on, she still did not know. She had stopped expecting to. The pendant was warm and it was Taya's and she wore it every day, and that was the whole of what she understood about it. The trader had known something she did not, and he had gone back upriver with the knowledge, and what he had left behind was not the knowledge but the copper, which was enough. Which was, she thought, perhaps more than enough.

The water jar was full. She set it by the door and looked at the sky. The south wind was coming in off the river now, carrying the smell of warm mud and something green, and the light was moving through the settlement in long strips, the way it did in the first real heat of the day.

The mound was not tall. That was the thing she always forgot until she was standing at it — that it was only a low rise above the river terrace, barely a man's height at its crown, the grass growing over it the same color as the rest of the hillside. You had to know what it was before you could see it. She had known since she was a child, when her mother had brought her here and named the people in it, and now she knew it the way she knew the river's voice at night, without having to attend to it.

She was watching the swallows.

They were working the air above the water in long, tilted arcs, dropping low over the surface and lifting again without apparent effort, and she was reading the pattern of them the way her mother had taught her — not the individual bird but the shape the flock made, the height at which they chose to fly. High meant dry air, the kind that preceded several days of clear weather. Low, skimming the river, meant moisture coming, pressure dropping, rain within a day or two. These were flying low. Not panicked-low, not the frantic dropping you got ahead of a serious storm, but easy-low, unhurried, the way you walked when you knew rain was coming but were not afraid of it.

She filed this away without words.

She heard Ranu before she saw him — the particular sound of his step on the dry grass, the slight catch in his breath from the slope. He came up beside her and stood for a moment without speaking, which meant he was reading her mood, which he was good at, and then he said, "What are you looking at?"

"Birds."

He looked at the river. The swallows were still working their low arcs. "What are they saying?"

"Rain. Tomorrow, maybe the day after."

He nodded. He did not question this. In eleven years he had never questioned her readings, which was one of the things she loved about him and also, occasionally, one of the things that made her tired, because there was no one to argue with her when she was wrong.

He stood beside her with his hands loose at his sides, and she could feel him deciding something. He had the stillness of a man who was about to tell a story, which was a particular stillness, different from his ordinary quiet — a gathering in, like a breath before speaking.

"My mother," he said, "when my brother died."

She waited.

"He was four years old. Fever. You know how fast it goes at that age." He paused. "She kept his sleeping mat rolled up in the corner of the house for three years. She would not let anyone move it. My father asked her once — just once — and she looked at him in a way that made him not ask again."

The swallows turned in a long sweep and came back lower over the water.

"In the third year," Ranu said, "there was a flood. Not a bad one, but enough. The mat got wet. She unrolled it to dry it out, and she found — I don't know what she found. A smell, maybe. The way things hold a smell. She sat down in the yard with the wet mat across her knees and she cried for the first time. Three years, and she had not cried, and then the flood came and ruined the mat and she cried."

He stopped.

"What happened?" Yanu asked, because she could hear there was more.

"She burned it. That afternoon. She built the fire herself and put it in." He turned his head slightly toward the pendant at her chest, then back to the river. "After that she was — not better. But different. Like something had shifted in her, the way a stone shifts in a riverbed. Still there. Just not in the same place."

Yanu listened to this. She understood that he meant well. She understood that the story was a kind of gift, the only kind he knew how to give, and that he had chosen it carefully — the mat, the flood, the burning, the shift. He was telling her that the weight moved. That it did not disappear but it moved, and that the moving was a kind of relief.

She reached up and touched the pendant.

It was warm from her skin, as it always was. The edge of it was smooth where her thumb had worn it over two years of this same gesture, this same reaching. She had done it so many times that she no longer noticed doing it until she was already doing it.

She did not know what she was waiting for.

That was the truth of it, the thing she could not say to Ranu because she did not have the words for it yet: she was waiting for something to tell her what to do with Taya's pendant, and nothing had told her. Not the copper itself. Not the mound. Not the birds, who told her everything about weather and nothing about this. She had worn it every day for two years and it was Taya's and it was warm and it was the right weight and she did not know what came next. She had thought, in the first months, that she would know. That grief had a shape like a river, with a current that carried you somewhere. But she was still in the same place.

She was not like Ranu's mother. She did not have a mat to burn.

"I know what you're doing," she said.

"Do you."

"You're telling me a story because you think it will help."

"Does it?"

She considered lying. She was capable of it — she had done it before, the small merciful lies that kept a conversation from going somewhere painful. But she was standing at the mound in the late morning with the swallows low over the river and the south wind carrying the smell of rain coming, and she did not have the energy for it.

"Not yet," she said.

He accepted this without protest. He was good at that too — at receiving the truth without flinching from it, without immediately trying to repair it. He stood beside her and they both looked at the river, and the swallows kept their low arcs, and the pendant rested against her chest the way it always did, warm and familiar and not yet finished with her.

"She kept his name," Ranu said after a while. "My mother. She said it every morning. Just the name. Nothing else."

Yanu looked at him.

His face was turned toward the water. He was not looking at her. He had said the thing sideways, the way you offered something you were not sure would be taken.

She looked back at the mound.

The grass on its crown was dry at the tips and green at the base, and a small brown bird — a wren, she thought, though it was gone before she could be certain — had landed at the edge and then lifted again immediately, as if it had remembered somewhere else it needed to be.

She said nothing. She touched the pendant again. The copper was warm and the weight of it was the same weight it had always been.

But she thought about the name.

She thought about what it would mean to say it out loud, here, at the mound, with Ranu beside her and the rain coming and the swallows still working the air above the water in their long, unhurried arcs.

She did not say it. Not yet.

The answer was not here yet. But something had shifted, the way a stone shifts in a riverbed — still there, just not in the same place — and she stood at the mound's edge in the warm smell of the coming rain and held the weight of it, and waited.

The south wind came exactly when she had known it would.

She had read it in the swallows three hours ago — the tightening of their arcs, the way they dropped lower over the water as the pressure changed — and she had said nothing about it to Ranu because there was no need to say it. The wind would come or it would not. Saying so changed nothing.

It came.

She felt it first at the back of her neck, where the pendant's cord lay against her skin, and then across her shoulders, and then the whole air shifted and the smell of rain moved through the clearing like something with weight. The river surface changed. The reeds along the near bank bent and recovered, bent and recovered. A heron that had been standing at the shallows for the better part of the morning lifted without urgency and crossed to the far side.

Ranu had gone back to the house. She had not asked him to go; he had simply understood that she needed to stand here a little longer, and he had touched her arm once — his hand warm and brief against her elbow — and then she had heard his footsteps through the grass and then nothing. He was good at reading her in the way she read weather. Not always right. But he tried, and the trying was its own kind of faithfulness.

She was alone at the mound.

She looked at it the way she looked at a line of cloud building over the western ridge — not searching for something, just receiving what was already there. The mound was not tall. It rose from the terrace like a held breath, grassed over, its crown level with her shoulder when she stood at its base. She had seen it her whole life. She had watched her mother's mother's people add to it, had watched the earth packed down with hands and feet, had watched the grass take hold again each spring as if the ground were simply healing a small wound. She knew who was in it. She knew the names. She had said them herself at ceremonies, her voice one thread in the larger cord of voices, and the names had gone into the air and the air had taken them.

She had not thought, until now, about what it meant to put something in.

The wind moved through the clearing again and she closed her eyes for a moment. The smell of rain was stronger now — wet stone, turned earth, the particular sharpness of river water pushed ahead of itself. She could hear the maize in the field behind the house, the dry rustle of stalks that needed the rain and knew it was coming. She could hear, very faintly, Ranu's voice from inside — he was already talking, already beginning something, the low rise and fall of it carrying through the bark walls without words. He was probably talking to Wasi, who would be awake by now and demanding to be carried.

She opened her eyes and looked at the mound.

She had thought, in the first weeks after Taya died, that the pendant was hers to keep. That was what keeping meant — you held the thing close, you wore it, you carried the weight of it against your skin every day until the weight became ordinary. That was how you did not forget. She had done it. She had worn it every day for two years and she had not forgotten. She would never forget. That was not the problem.

The problem was that she did not know what came after not forgetting.

She moved to the base of the mound and put her hand against the grass. It was warm on top from the morning sun, cool underneath where the roots held the moisture. She pressed her palm flat against it the way she had seen Ranu's youngest do once — the child had simply walked to the mound and pressed his hand to it, no ceremony, just curiosity, the way children touched everything to understand what it was. She had watched him do it and thought nothing of it at the time.

She thought of it now.

The grass was dry at the tips and green at the base and the earth beneath it was solid and dark and had been there longer than she had. The people in it had been there longer than she had. They were not gone in the way a fire went out — they were here, held, kept by the ground the way the ground kept everything it was given.

She had been wearing the pendant for two years and it was warm from her skin and it was the right weight and she had not let it go.

She had not let it go because she had not known how to let it go without losing Taya again. That was the truth she had not said to Ranu, not because she was hiding it but because she had not found the shape of it until this moment, standing at the mound with the south wind in her hair and the rain smell coming on strong now, the sky to the west gone the color of pewter behind the tree line.

She looked at the mound. She looked at it the way she looked at a bird she had not seen before — carefully, without deciding what it was until she had looked long enough to be wrong twice.

The people in the mound were kept. They were not lost. They were here, in the earth, on the high ground above the river, with the grass growing over them and the rain coming every season to green the grass again. They were held by something larger than memory, larger than the effort of any single person to remember. The ground did not forget.

She had been trying to hold Taya with her own hands. With her own skin. With the weight of copper against her chest every morning when she woke and every night when she lay down. She had been holding on as if the moment she stopped holding, Taya would be gone. As if the pendant were the only thing keeping her daughter in the world.

But the pendant was not Taya's because Yanu wore it. It was Taya's because Taya had been the kind of child who reached for bright things with both hands and absolute certainty, and the pendant had been made in the shape of that reaching, in the season of her absence, by a man who understood without being told what an empty cord was for. That moment did not need Yanu's neck to survive. It needed somewhere it would not be lost.

She stood very still. The wind moved the grass on the mound's crown. A wren — she was certain this time — landed at the edge, turned its head once with that quick decisive motion wrens had, and was gone.

The ground kept what it was given.

She did not make a decision. That was not how it happened. It was not a decision the way choosing a river crossing was a decision, with two paths and a moment of choosing. It was more like the moment when the clouds confirmed what the birds had already told her — not new information, just the world catching up to what she already knew.

She lifted the cord from her neck.

The pendant swung free, catching the flat light of the overcast sky — no warmth in it now, just the true color of the copper, a dull reddish-brown that was somehow more honest than the firelit amber she was used to. She held it by the cord and looked at it. It was a small thing. A disc no wider than two fingers, with a punched hole at the top. A man she would never see again had made it in an afternoon without being asked, because he had looked at an empty cord and understood what it was for.

She crouched at the mound's southern face.

The earth was firm but not hard. She worked a small hollow with her fingers, pressing in and turning, the way she worked clay — not forcing, just persuading. The soil was dark and smelled of root and rain-to-come, and it gave without much argument. She made it deep enough. She placed the pendant in, face up, the cord coiled around it. She looked at it for a moment in the small dark hollow she had made.

Then she said the name.

"Taya."

Just the name. Nothing else, the way Ranu's mother had done it. The wind took it immediately, the way the wind took everything, and the word was gone before she could second-guess it, and she was glad.

She covered the hollow with both hands, pressing the earth back the way you close something that should stay closed — not roughly, not with finality, but with the care you use for things you are trusting to something larger than yourself. The ground settled under her palms. The grass at the edges was undisturbed. In a season it would be as if nothing had changed, and that was right. That was exactly right.

She pressed her palms flat against the mound one last time.

The earth was cool and solid and had been there longer than she had.

She stood up.

The rain was very close now. She could hear it in the oak forest to the west, the first drops striking leaf after leaf in a sound like something being said very quietly and very clearly. The swallows had gone from the river. The reeds were bending steadily now, not recovering between gusts, just leaning south and staying there.

She turned and walked back toward the house.

From inside she could hear Ranu's voice, rising now, taking on the particular shape it had when he was at the good part of something — she could not hear the words but she could hear the pleasure in them, the way he leaned into a story the way she leaned into a wind she was reading. She could hear Wasi's voice too, a high delighted sound, and then another voice, her eldest, asking something, and then Ranu's voice again, lower and slower, settling into the answer.

She stopped at the door.

She put her hand to her throat. The cord was gone. The hollow below her collarbone was empty and she felt the emptiness the way you feel a tooth that is no longer there — not as loss exactly, but as the shape of where something was.

She stood in the doorway and listened to Ranu tell the story, and the first rain reached the clearing behind her and touched her shoulders and the back of her neck, and she let it, and she breathed, and the hollow at her throat was empty and the mound was behind her and Taya's name was in the air and in the ground both, and she did not feel better — not yet, not today — but she felt, for the first time in two years, that she had done the right thing with her hands.

She went inside.
