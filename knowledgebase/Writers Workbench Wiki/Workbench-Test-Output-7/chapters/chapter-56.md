The mound was knee-high.

Pell stood at its eastern base and looked at it the way Kessik had taught him to look at it — with patience, and with something close to love, and with the clear knowledge that what it was now was not what it would become.

The quarry ditch curved behind him, a shallow arc cut into the ridge's shoulder, the raw earth still dark from yesterday's work. Three of the women had gone down into it at first light with long-handled cutters, slicing turf in neat rectangles the length of a forearm, and the smell of it rose now in the cold air — wet clay and broken root and the faint iron smell of the blade's edge, which always reminded him of the smell just before rain.

Kessik was watching him.

She stood at the mound's south side with her hands at her sides, her heavy wool mantle pinned at the left shoulder with a bronze penannular brooch — the ring worn smooth at the pin-joint, the terminals rubbed bright from years of handling. She had not worn it to work in before. She had put it on this morning without explaining why.

"Come here," she said.

He came around the base to her side, stepping over the quarry lip with the ease of someone who had been doing it since the ground broke last spring. He stood beside her and looked at the mound's low rise.

"Tell me what you see," she said.

He looked for a moment. "Earth. Turf. The center's higher than the south edge."

"Yes."

"Is that wrong?"

"It will be, if we fill the south edge the same way we filled the center." She crouched and pressed her palm flat against the mound's face, where yesterday's turf layer met the older fill below. The surface was cold and firm, the grass-side laid down, soil-side up, the way she had shown them. "Feel this."

He crouched beside her and pressed his hand where hers had been.

"What does it tell you?"

He pressed harder. "It's settled."

"The center is settled. The south edge is not. You can feel the difference if you know what you're looking for — there's give, just there." She moved his hand two finger-widths south. "You feel it?"

He was still for a moment. "Yes."

"That's where we begin today. Not the center. Not the top." She stood. "The mound will pull toward its own weight if you let it. You have to build against that. You have to think about what it will be in fifty years, not what it looks like now."

Pell looked at the south edge. "And the orientation?"

"River-facing." She pointed east, down the ridge's long slope to where the river showed between the trees as a flat grey brightness, not yet touched by the sun. "Every layer faces the river. The earth goes down with the grass-side toward the water. Not because the water will see it. Because the people who come after will dig it up one day and read it, and they should be able to tell which way we were looking."

He was quiet for a moment, turning this over.

"That's a long time to think ahead," he said.

"That's the point of building it."

She walked the south edge, pressing the surface with her foot at intervals, reading the give through the thin leather of her shoe. The ridge was cold and wet from last night's frost, and the grass at the mound's base was still silver-edged where the sun had not reached.

"We pack by hand today," she said. "No baskets on the face. Baskets on the quarry side, to carry. But the laying is done by hand."

Pell looked at his hands, already rimed with dried clay from the morning's work.

"All of us?" he said.

"All of us."

He nodded once, the way his father used to nod, and went back for his basket.

The others came up the ridge as the sun cleared the treeline — six of them, carrying baskets and stretchers, their breath making brief white shapes in the cold air. Kessik watched them come and pointed to the south edge without greeting them. They had been doing this long enough to understand a pointed finger.

The work was slow and deliberate. Each rectangle of turf came off the stretcher and went down into the south edge's shallow gap, grass-side down, soil-side up, pressed flat with open palms before the next piece was laid. She moved along the line of workers, watching the angle of each placement, correcting with a word or a gesture. When one of the men laid a piece with a slight tilt toward the center she stopped him with a hand on his arm and showed him how to read the slope of the existing surface before placing the next piece — not against the slope, not with it, but level, always level, building the correction into the fill rather than plastering over it.

"You're not filling a hole," she said. "You're building a floor."

He looked at her. He was perhaps thirty, with a long face and patient eyes. "What's the difference?"

"Filling a hole, you're thinking about what's missing. Building a floor, you're thinking about what will stand on it."

He considered this, then laid the next piece with more care.

The sun climbed. The frost burned off the grass and the ridge dried slightly, and the smell of the earth changed from cold and iron to something older and darker, the smell of deep soil opened to the air. Kessik worked beside them for a time, her hands in the clay, pressing each piece flat with the heel of her palm, feeling the mound receive it. There was a satisfaction in this that she had not expected when she first conceived the project — not the satisfaction of completion, which was still years away, but the satisfaction of correctness, of a thing being done the right way, each layer being what it was supposed to be and nothing else.

She stood back and watched Pell lay a long piece of turf across the south edge's outer margin, pressing it down with both hands, leaning his weight into it, getting the contact right. He was learning to read the surface the way she had shown him. He was a quick learner, which was important, because there were things she needed him to know before she was ready to tell him she needed him to know them.

Not yet. Not this morning.

This morning there was only the mound, and the cold air, and the smell of opened earth, and the flat grey river below them catching the first of the day's real light.

"River-facing," she said again, to no one in particular, to all of them, to the mound itself.

The work continued.

---

Kessik had conceived the mound. That was the word Pell used, later, when he tried to explain it to others — not *built*, not *planned*, but *conceived*, the way a woman conceives a child, as something that begins inside and must be brought outward at cost. She had come to him three years before the ground broke and said she needed a builder and a keeper, and that she had chosen him for both, and that he should think on it and give her his answer before the next new moon.

He had given her his answer in four days. She had not seemed surprised.

What she had not told him, in those first years, was that the mound was also a chain — that the earth and the names were the same thing, that you could not lay one without the other, that the whole structure would be hollow without the speaking. She taught him this slowly, the way she taught him everything: not explaining first and demonstrating second, but placing him in the middle of the doing and letting understanding arrive through his hands.

He was not always grateful for this method.

"Why didn't you tell me about the names from the beginning?" he asked her once, in the fourth year, when she had begun teaching him the order.

She looked at him with the precise, unhurried attention that was her characteristic expression. "Would you have understood them before you understood the mound?"

He thought about it. "No."

"Then I told you at the right time."

This was how she spoke: not unkindly, but without the softening he had grown up expecting from teachers. She had precise, unhurried authority. She could still climb to the mound's top to check his work in those years, and she did it without ceremony — arriving at the summit, looking at what he had done, saying what needed to be said, descending. She had been doing this for longer than he had been alive. The knowledge sat in her body the way long practice sits — not as thought but as reflex, as the body's own understanding.

He learned to trust that knowledge before he could articulate why.

---

Each autumn, when the harvest was in and the stores were sealed, Kessik called the workers to the ridge for the naming.

She stood at the top of each season's new layer and spoke the names aloud — the dead of the settlement, going back as far as she could hold them, and then further, to the names she had learned from her own teacher, names that had no faces attached to them anymore, names that were only sounds and the weight of being kept. She spoke them in order, oldest first, the way you count from the beginning rather than the end, because the end was not yet known.

The workers stood below her on the slope and listened. She did not ask them to repeat the names. Not yet. That would come later, when Pell was ready to stand where she stood.

In the fourth year, she began teaching him the order.

"You're not memorizing," she told him. "You're learning the shape. Names have a shape the way a path has a shape. You don't remember every stone. You remember where the path goes."

"What if I lose one?"

"You won't lose one if you know what comes before it and what comes after."

"What if I lose the one at the beginning?"

She looked at him. He was serious, not testing her.

"Then you say the ones you have," she said. "A chain with a link missing is still a chain. What you cannot do is pretend the link is there when it isn't. You say: *here is where I do not know*. And you keep going."

He was quiet for a moment. The wind came up from the river, carrying the smell of cold water and woodsmoke from the settlement below.

"And the one at the end?" he said.

"The one at the end is always the most recent dead. When I die, my name goes in."

He said nothing.

"That's not a request," she said. "That's how it works."

He understood, then, that she was not only the mound's architect. She was its first named keeper. And she was making him the second.

---

The mound rose through the years the way a child grows — in the watching you could not see it, but in the stepping back you could. By the seventh year it stood above the quarry ditch's rim by the height of two men, and the ridge had changed shape in a way that was visible from the river path below. People from the southern settlements began to come to look at it. They stood at a distance, not approaching, the way you stand at a distance from something you do not have permission to understand yet.

Kessik let them look.

In the eighth year, a man from the eastern band came to the ridge and asked what the mound was for.

"For remembering," she said.

"Remembering what?"

"Everyone."

He looked at it for a long time. He had a careful, angular face and the deliberate posture of someone who had learned to think before speaking.

"My people have a mound," he said. "Older than anyone living. We don't know who built it."

"Now you know how mounds get built," she said.

He looked at her. She thought he might be offended. He wasn't.

"May I bring my mother?" he said. "She knows the names further back than I do."

"Yes."

---

The tenth year was a good year for the mound. Four households sent workers through the full harvest season; the quarry ditch was extended to the north to give them fresh turf, and the north face rose faster than it had in any previous year. Pell organized the work that season without being asked, and Kessik watched him do it from the ridge's edge, noting the way he moved between workers — not commanding exactly, but orienting, the way a post orients a fence without being the fence itself.

He had learned the names by the ninth year. All of them, in order, without error. She had tested him three times, the last time in the dark, with no prompting, and he had gone from the beginning to the end without stopping.

She had not told him what this meant. She did not need to. He knew.

In the third year, Pell had brought his wife to the work. She was a woman from the eastern band who laughed easily and moved with an efficiency that announced nothing about itself. She learned the laying method in half a morning. By afternoon she was correcting the angle of another worker's placement with a gesture so light he barely noticed he'd been corrected.

"She's good," Pell said, watching her.

"Yes," Kessik said.

"You could have told me that."

"You already knew it. That's why you married her."

He looked at her sideways, the way he had looked at her since he was a boy and suspected she was saying two things at once.

---

In the twelfth year, Kessik began to tire more easily.

She noticed it first on the climb to the ridge. The path was not steep — she had walked it so many times that her feet knew every root and stone — but in the twelfth year she found herself stopping at the second bend to rest, her hand on the trunk of the ash tree that grew there, her breath coming shorter than it should. She said nothing.

The workers came and went. Pell directed them. She moved along the face as she always had, watching the placement, correcting with a word or a gesture. If she moved more slowly, no one said so. If she rested more often, leaning against the mound's face with her hand pressed flat against the turf, it could have been contemplation. She let it look like contemplation.

In the thirteenth year, she stopped carrying earth herself.

This required a rearrangement she made without announcing. She had always carried a share — not the heaviest loads, but a share, because it was not right to direct work you would not do yourself. Now she found reasons to be occupied elsewhere when the baskets were being filled: checking the angle of the south face, measuring the quarry ditch's depth, speaking to the eastern band's elder about the placement of the next season's layer. The work she did was real work. She simply arranged it so that none of it required lifting.

Pell watched her.

She knew he watched her. She knew the quality of his watching — the same careful attention he brought to the mound's surface, reading the angle before placing the turf, never forcing a correction, always working with what was already there. He watched her the same way and said nothing, and she was grateful for his silence and furious at it in equal measure, because his silence meant he had seen, and seeing without speaking was its own kind of acknowledgment.

She did not want to be acknowledged.

---

In the fourteenth year, she stopped climbing to the top.

She told herself it was because the naming could be done from the south face, which was true. She stood at the mound's mid-slope for the autumn naming that year, her hand on the turf face for balance, and spoke the names from there, and the sound carried well enough, and no one asked why she was not standing at the summit as she always had.

Except that she felt the absence of it. She felt the ridge above her like a debt, and the names she spoke felt slightly wrong without the height, without the wind that came unimpeded from the river when you stood at the top, without the view of the valley below that made you understand, each time, what the mound was for — not the workers on the slope, not the quarry ditch, not the careful layering, but the valley, the river, the settlement's smoke, the living people going about their lives in the ground the dead had held for them.

She said the names anyway. She said them in order, oldest first, all the way to the most recent dead, and her voice did not waver.

Afterward, Pell came to stand beside her. He did not offer his arm. He had learned not to offer what she had not asked for.

"The south face is settling well," he said.

"Yes."

"The north face needs another layer before the cold comes."

"I know. We'll do it next week if the weather holds."

They stood together looking at the valley. A thin line of smoke rose from the settlement, straight up in the still air, then bending east at some height she could not see.

"The names were good this year," Pell said.

She looked at him. He was not looking at her. He was looking at the valley, his face quiet and unrevealing, the way his father's face had been quiet when he was deciding something.

"They're always the same names," she said.

"Yes," he said. "But this year they were good."

She understood what he meant. She did not answer it. She turned back to the mound's face and pressed her hand flat against the turf, feeling the cold of it, the slight give of the new season's layer not yet fully compacted, the earth still breathing under her palm.

*Not yet*, she thought. *There is still the sixth layer. There is still the figure. There is still the top.*

She did not say this aloud. She took her hand from the mound and walked back down the ridge path, slowly, her feet finding each root and stone from memory, the bronze brooch at her shoulder catching the last of the afternoon light.

The bark she had chosen was birch, stripped in late summer when it still bent without cracking, kept rolled and weighted through the autumn so it would lie flat. She had cut it herself, in July, before the worst of the tiredness came on. She had not told anyone why she wanted it.

She carried it up the south face on the morning of the sixth layer's completion, the roll tucked under her left arm, the antler figure wrapped inside it. The figure was small enough to hold in both hands — a deer, or something deer-shaped, the tines worn smooth where she had worked them with a river stone over three winters, the eyes two shallow cups rubbed with red ochre that had long since faded to a rust-brown stain. She had made it in the second year of the mound's building, before she knew it would take fifteen. She had known, even then, that it belonged in the sixth layer. She had not known why the sixth. She had stopped asking herself that kind of question.

Pell was already at the top of the prepared face when she came up. He had been directing the final placement of the turf — the clean outer skin of the layer, the grass-side out, tamped down with the flat of a wooden paddle. The workers were finishing the north quadrant. The hollow she had asked for was cut in the center of the layer, a hand's-depth scoop in the dark subsoil, waiting.

He saw her coming and did not come down to meet her.

She was grateful for that.

The slope was steep enough in the upper third that she had to lean forward, her free hand finding the turf face, her feet searching for the flat places where previous workers had compacted the earth. She breathed through her mouth on the steeper sections. She did not stop.

When she reached the prepared face, Pell was standing three paces back from the hollow, his arms at his sides, watching the workers on the north quadrant. He had arranged himself so that he was clearly occupied elsewhere. She recognized the arrangement. She had done the same thing herself, in the thirteenth year, when she stopped carrying earth.

She set the bark roll down on the turf and knelt beside the hollow.

The kneeling cost her. She kept her face still.

The bark unrolled easily — she had weighted it well. The antler figure lay in the center of it, the tines pointing up, the rust-brown eyes catching the flat winter light. She looked at it for a moment. She had looked at it many times. It did not look like much. It looked like what it was: a piece of antler worked by a woman who was not a carver, over three winters, until it held the shape she needed it to hold.

She picked it up with both hands.

She spoke the names then, quietly, not loudly enough for the workers to hear. She said the oldest names first, the ones that were nearly sounds rather than words now, worn smooth by repetition the way the river wears a stone. She said the name of the first woman buried here, the pause before the second syllable that changed its meaning, the way her own teacher had taught her. She said the names in order, all the way forward to the most recent dead, the ones whose faces she still held clearly. She said her own name last, because she was placing this and the one who places a thing must be named in the placing, or the thing is placed by no one.

Her voice did not waver.

She set the figure into the hollow with both hands, pressing it down until it seated firmly in the dark soil, tines pointing up toward the layer that would cover it. She pressed the bark around it, folding the edges down, tucking them under so the figure was wrapped on all sides. Her fingers were stiff in the cold. The bark crackled once and then held.

She pressed her palms flat against the wrapped figure for a moment, feeling the slight give of the soil beneath.

Then she rose.

It took longer than it should have. She had to shift her weight twice, find a place to brace her left hand, push upward through the resistance in her hip. She was upright. She was breathing hard — not the ordinary hard of a climb, but the other kind, the kind that did not resolve quickly, that required standing still and waiting for the body to decide it was finished.

She stood still and waited.

Pell had turned. He was looking at her now, not at the workers. His face was quiet. He did not move.

The breathing slowed. She looked at the hollow, now filled with the wrapped figure, the dark soil around it. One of the workers would come and lay the final turf over it, and then the layer would be done, and then there was only the last layer, and then the top.

"I can finish the last layer," Pell said.

"Not yet."

He looked at her.

"Not yet," she said again, and her voice was even, and she held his gaze until he understood that she was not refusing help. She was refusing to be absent. She was refusing the version of this where the last layer went up without her hands on it, her feet on the slope, her name spoken from the summit when the work was done.

He understood. She saw it move through his face the way weather moves across open ground — arriving, settling, passing into stillness.

"Next week," he said. "If the weather holds."

"Next week."

She turned back to the hollow and nodded once to the nearest worker, who came forward with a cut of turf, grass-side down, and laid it over the figure. The man tamped it with the flat of his hand, careful, thorough. He did not know what was underneath. He did not need to.

She watched the turf settle into place, the layer closing over the figure the way water closes over a stone.

The sixth layer was finished.

She stood on the mound's face in the thin winter light and looked out at the valley below — the river, the settlement's smoke, the fields stripped bare for the season — and thought: *one more*.

The spring came late and cold, the ground holding its frost into the fourth month, and when it finally broke the mud was deep enough to pull a boot from your foot if you stepped wrong. Kessik had the mat brought out and set at the mound's southern base, on the driest ground, where she could see the whole face of the slope. She sat on it with her legs folded and her hands in her lap and watched Pell work.

He had sent the others away. She had not asked him to. He had simply done it — come to her the evening before and said the final layer was his to build, and she had looked at him for a long moment and then said nothing, which was the same as yes.

He worked with a bark tray, wide and flat, the edges curled up by soaking and drying so it held its shape. He had made three of them. He filled each one at the quarry cut on the north side — she could not see the cut from where she sat, only the moment he came back around the mound's shoulder with the loaded tray held level in both hands, walking carefully, placing each foot before trusting it with weight. The mud pulled at him. He did not hurry.

He was broad now. He had his father's shoulders and his father's stillness and something of his own that she could not name because it had grown up in front of her and she had never had the distance to see it whole. His hair was going gray at the temples. She had not noticed when that started.

He climbed the slope with the loaded tray and set it down on the mound's crown and began laying the earth in the method she had taught him: working outward from the center, pressing each handful flat before adding the next, building the layer in shallow courses the way you build a wall — not piling, but placing, so the whole thing would hold its shape through rain and freeze and the long weight of years. He had watched her do it. He had done it beside her. He knew the method.

He was doing it wrong.

"Too fast on the outside edge," she called up.

He stopped. Looked down at her.

"You're building the edge before the center is fully seated. Start again from the middle."

He looked at the work he had done. He did not argue. He broke the outer edge apart with his palms and worked back toward the center, starting the course over.

She watched him rebuild it. The morning light was flat and white, the kind that came before rain. A crow settled on the bare oak at the mound's north side, watched them for a time, and left. She breathed steadily, the way you breathe when there is nothing left to do but witness.

He came down for more earth. She heard him at the quarry cut, the sound of the tray scraping against the cut face, the brief silence while he loaded it. He came back around the shoulder of the mound. His boots were caked to the ankle.

"The tray is too full," she said.

He looked at it. She was right — the earth was heaped past the curled edges, and the tray flexed slightly in the middle under the weight. He set it down and removed a double handful, set the excess on the ground, and lifted the tray again.

"Better."

He climbed.

She watched him work through the morning. He came down seven times. Each time he passed her she said something — *press from the heel of your hand, not the fingers; that northeast section is sitting high; the center still wants another course before you move out.* He listened. He corrected. He did not tell her he knew, even when he did. She recognized this as a form of love, the kind that does not announce itself.

By midmorning the layer was three-quarters built, the new earth dark against the old, the surface still rough and unfinished at the edges. He came down for the seventh load and when he came back around the mound's shoulder she saw that the tray was loaded differently — the earth packed more firmly, the weight distributed toward the back so the tray rode level. He had worked it out himself. She said nothing about it.

He climbed. She watched his hands. He laid the earth in the right order this time, center out, pressing each course flat before starting the next, his palms working in slow circles the way she had shown him — not tamping down but seating, persuading the earth into its place rather than forcing it. The layer was taking shape. It had the right profile: low and broad, not peaked, a gentle dome that would shed water without eroding.

She felt something in her chest that was not the tiredness. She let herself feel it.

He came down for the last load. She heard him at the quarry cut for longer than usual — the cut was nearly empty now, the last of the good soil requiring more work to collect. She heard the tray scrape. Then silence. Then the sound of him coming around the shoulder of the mound with the tray held level, his arms steady, his face concentrated and quiet.

He passed her without speaking and climbed.

She watched him lay the last earth. He worked slowly, placing each handful with the care you give to a thing you know you will not do again. The northeast edge, which had sat high all morning, he built up with three careful courses until it matched the rest. He smoothed the whole surface with his palms, working outward from the center in widening circles, and when he reached the edge he pressed the new earth firmly against the old, sealing the join.

He sat back on his heels and looked at what he had built.

She looked at it too, from the base. The mound's crown was finished. The final layer was done.

She shifted her weight to rise. She tried once, bracing her left hand against the mat, pushing upward slowly, and this time she made it — unsteady for a moment, but she was standing. She stood in the mud at the mound's southern base and looked up at the finished crown.

Pell came down the slope. His boots were dark with mud to the knee. He stopped in front of her and looked at her face, reading it the way she had taught him to read the mound — not for what was on the surface but for what the surface told you about what was underneath.

"Help me up," she said.

He offered his arm without comment. She took it — not the hand but the forearm, her fingers wrapping around the bone and muscle of it — and they started up the slope together.

The climb was slow. The mud was soft in the spring thaw and her feet slipped twice, and both times Pell steadied her without pulling, without hurrying her, without making anything of it. The slope was not steep. She had climbed it a hundred times. She climbed it now.

They reached the top.

She stood on the finished crown of the mound and looked out.

The valley opened below her — the river running high with snowmelt, brown and wide, catching the flat white light; the settlement's smoke rising from four fires, straight up in the still air; the fields dark with turned earth, the barley not yet showing; the oak wood on the far ridge, bare still, the branches fine as scratches against the sky. She could see the quarry cut on the north side, the raw scar of it, the earth they had taken out over fifteen years to build the thing she was standing on. She could see the path worn into the grass by the workers' feet, the same path season after season, the ground knowing the weight of them.

She stood there for a long time. Pell stood beside her and said nothing.

The wind came up from the river, cold and smelling of mud and new water, the smell of the season turning. She felt it against her face. She felt the finished earth under her feet — firm, settled, the last layer still dark with moisture but already beginning to knit to the layer below it.

"Now it will remember us," she said.

Pell looked at her. "You built it."

She shook her head. "We built it. That's the point."

He was quiet for a moment. She watched his face. He was looking out at the valley, at the smoke from the settlement's fires, at the river running high.

"All of us," he said. Not a question.

"All of us. Everyone who carried a basket. Everyone who cut turf in the cold. Everyone whose name I spoke into the layers." She paused. "Everyone who will speak the names after."

He understood what she was telling him. She saw it settle into him, the way the earth had settled under his palms all morning — not forced, but placed, finding its own level.

Below them, one of the settlement's dogs crossed the open ground between the houses, trotting purposefully toward something she could not see. A child ran after it, arms out. The smoke from the nearest fire bent slightly as the wind shifted, then straightened again.

She looked at the mound's crown under her feet. Fifteen years. The antler figure in the sixth layer. The names in all the layers below it, going down to the first woman, the one whose name required the pause before the second syllable, the one who had started this with a copper pendant and a grief she could not put anywhere else.

All of it under her feet. All of it holding.

"Next winter," she said.

Pell turned to look at her.

"Tell me the names again, next winter. All of them, in order." She met his eyes. "I want to hear you say them without stopping."

---

The descent was harder than the climb.

She had known it would be. The slope pulled at her differently going down, the weight of her shifting forward, her left leg slow to catch. Pell stayed at her left side and she took his forearm again and they went down together, one step at a time, her feet finding the soft places in the mud and pressing through them to the firmer ground below.

She did not speak on the way down. She needed what breath she had.

At the bottom she stopped and let go of his arm and stood for a moment with her hands at her sides, waiting. She did not fight it. She had learned not to fight it toward the end of things, only to wait.

"Go home," she told him. "Eat something."

"I'll walk with you."

"I know the way."

He looked at her the way he always looked at her when she said something like that — not arguing, not agreeing, just holding the space between them open a moment longer. Then he nodded, and she walked back to the settlement alone, her shoes dark with mud, the cold coming up through the leather soles with each step.

---

She lived through the spring planting and the summer and the harvest. She moved more slowly than she had the year before, and the year before that, and she did not pretend otherwise. She kept working — not at the mound, which was finished, but at the settlement's other work: the clay pots, the dried fish, the winter stores counted and recounted in the low-ceilinged storehouse that smelled of grain dust and old smoke. She sat with the children in the evenings and told them what they needed to know. She told Pell's wife the names of the plants that grew at the creek bend and which ones the fever would not touch. She told Pell's eldest daughter, who was seven and had her father's serious eyes, that the mound was not a grave — or not only a grave — and that when she was old enough she would understand the difference.

She did not tell Pell everything.

She meant to. She had meant to for three years, since the illness first showed itself as a tightening that would not release, since she had understood what it was and what it meant. There was a name she had not yet given him — not a name from the chain, which he knew, or nearly knew, but a name from before the chain, a name she had been told in the last winter of her own teacher's life, a name that required a particular silence before it could be spoken, a silence she had not yet found the right moment to make.

She kept thinking there was more time.

---

The first snow came in the month after the harvest feast, light and early, gone by midday. She stood at the door of her house and watched it fall and thought: *this is the winter*.

She was not afraid. She had had a long time to work through the fear, and she had worked through most of it, and what remained was something quieter — not peace exactly, but the thing on the other side of fighting. She had built the mound. She had spoken the names into the layers. She had given Pell what she could give him. The thing she had not given him was still there, waiting, and she needed to give it to him before the cold took her capacity for speech, which would come before it took anything else — she had watched her own teacher go the same way and she knew the order of it.

She sent word to Pell. Come tonight.

He came at dusk, alone, as she had asked. He sat across the fire from her in the way he had learned to sit — not leaning forward, not filling the silence with questions, just present, his broad hands resting on his knees. The fire was small. She had not built it large on purpose; she did not want to be warm and comfortable for this, she wanted to be clear.

"There is a name," she said, "that I have not told you."

He waited.

"It is not in the chain you know. It is before the chain. It is the name of the woman who told the first woman — the one at the bottom — what a mound was for. Where the idea came from. She is not buried there. She is from somewhere else, and her name does not belong to this ground, but it belongs to the story of this ground, and if you do not have it the chain is shorter than it should be."

Pell said nothing. She could see him holding it, turning it over.

"Say it after me," she said.

She said the name. It had two syllables, and the pause before the second one was not a hesitation but a kind of weight, the way you pause before setting something heavy down to make sure the ground beneath you will hold. She said it once. She said it again. She watched him say it back to her, his mouth finding the shape of it, the pause in the right place, almost right, not quite.

"Again," she said.

He said it again. Closer.

"Again."

This time it was right. She heard it — the pause in the right place, the second syllable landing where it needed to land. She nodded.

"That is the name before the first name," she said. "When you teach the chain, you teach it last. After everything else. When the person you are teaching has said all the other names without stopping, you say: there is one more. And you tell them this one."

"Why last?"

"Because it is hardest to hold. Because it does not belong to the ground and so the ground will not help you remember it. You have to hold it yourself."

He was quiet for a long time. The fire shifted, a log settling, and the light moved across his face. He looked like his father in that moment — the same stillness, the same quality of attention that did not need to announce itself.

"Say it again," she said. "The whole chain, from the beginning. All the names, in order. End with that one."

He began. She listened.

He knew most of it. He knew the names she had spoken into the layers, the names she had taught him over fifteen years of building, the names that went down through the mound's depths to the copper pendant and the woman who had placed it there. He said them in the right order, with the right pauses. He stumbled once, near the middle, and recovered without prompting. He reached the end of the chain she had taught him and then he paused, and then he said the new name — the pause in the right place, the second syllable where it needed to be.

She let out a breath.

"Good," she said.

She did not tell him that there was one more thing she had not told him — not a name but a place, a small thing, a detail about the sixth layer that she had meant to explain and had not, because she had thought there was more time. She did not tell him because she was tired now, deeply tired in a way that had nothing to do with the day's work, and because she thought he might find it himself when the time came, and because some things were perhaps meant to be found rather than given.

She was wrong about that. She knew she was wrong about it even as she let the moment pass. But the tiredness was very great, and the fire was warm, and Pell was sitting across from her saying the names quietly to himself, committing them, and she let herself close her eyes and listen to him say them.

---

She died in the deep of winter, before the first snow melted. The cold had come into her chest by then and her speech had gone the way she had known it would go — not all at once but in stages, each morning a little less of it, until in the last days she communicated mostly by the direction of her eyes and the pressure of her hand. Pell sat with her. His wife sat with her. The children came and went. She could hear the settlement's sounds outside the walls — the animals, the wind, someone splitting wood in the cold — and she found she was glad of them, the ordinary sounds, the world going on.

She died on a morning when the light came in low and flat through the door and lay across the floor like a pale cloth. Pell was holding her hand. She was aware of him, and of the light, and of the cold that was not painful anymore, only very present, and then she was not aware of anything.

---

He stood at the mound's base the next morning in the grey light before dawn, alone.

The snow had not come yet. The ground was frozen hard, the mud from the spring thaw long since locked solid, and his breath came out in short white clouds and dissolved. The mound rose above him — dark against the pale sky, its crown visible against the ridge, the shape of fifteen years' work and all the years before that, the shape of everything she had built and everything she had taught him and everything she had carried that he had not yet understood the weight of.

He stood there for a long time.

He said the names. All of them, in order, from the first to the last, the name before the first name at the end with its pause in the right place. He said them quietly, not performing them, just saying them, the way you say a thing you need to make sure you still have.

He had them. He was almost certain he had them.

But there was something in her face, in the last weeks — a thought she had started and not finished, a direction her eyes had moved toward and then away from. He had not asked. He had thought there was more time.

He looked up at the mound's crown in the grey light and understood, for the first time fully, that the mound was his now. Not his to own — she had never owned it, no one owned it — but his to carry. His to know. His to pass on.

And he did not know all of it.

He stood in the cold and looked at what they had built, and felt the shape of what he did not know the way you feel a missing tooth with your tongue — the absence specific, the outline of it clear, the thing itself gone.

The light grew. Somewhere in the settlement a fire was being fed, smoke rising straight up in the still air. He watched it rise and thin and disappear.

He would need to go back into the mound. Not to dig — not yet, perhaps not ever — but to understand what she had placed in the sixth layer and why she had placed it there and what the placing meant. She had told him some of it. He was no longer certain she had told him all of it.

He turned and walked back toward the settlement, his boots loud on the frozen ground.

Behind him the mound stood against the sky, holding everything it held, patient as ground is patient, waiting for the next person who would know how to ask it the right question.
