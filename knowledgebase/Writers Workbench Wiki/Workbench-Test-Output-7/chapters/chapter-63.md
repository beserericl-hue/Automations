The fire had burned down to coals by the time the child came.

Yanu remembered that first. Not the pain, which had been ordinary, the same pain she had carried for hours through the long afternoon while Ranu sat outside the house talking to anyone who would listen, telling them she was doing well, telling them the child was nearly here, telling them a story about his own mother's labor that no one had asked to hear. She had heard his voice through the wattle wall, rising and falling, and she had thought: even now. Even now he cannot be quiet.

The pain she had expected. The quiet she had not.

The child came out silent. Not the held silence of a child gathering breath before its first cry, but a genuine, settled quiet, as if she had decided the world outside her mother's body was worth looking at before she announced herself to it. The woman who helped, broad Mara, whose hands knew exactly what to do and did it without speaking, had lifted the child and checked her face. Yanu saw the small flicker of concern cross Mara's eyes before the child blinked, once, and turned her head toward the ember-light.

That was all. No cry. Just the turning of the head.

Mara laughed, a short surprised sound, and said: "She wants to see it first."

Yanu reached for her, and Mara laid the child on her chest, still slick, still attached by the cord that pulsed its last few beats between them. Yanu felt the impossible weight of her. Not heavy, a small bird's weight, but dense with presence, with thereness, with the particular gravity of a new person who had not existed yesterday and existed completely now.

She did not cry. She did not know what to do with her face.

Ranu came in when the cord was cut. He came in with his mouth already open, already forming words, and he stopped in the doorway for one moment with his hand on the door frame and his eyes on the child. His face did something Yanu had never seen it do before. It went still. Ranu's face, which was always moving, always shaping the next sentence, went as still as standing water.

Then the moment passed and he crossed the floor and knelt beside them both, and he said: "She came in the spring. She came when the cranes came back. I heard them this morning, I told Mara I heard them, I said the child would come today and here she is, here she is, she came with the cranes."

Yanu said: "The cranes came back three days ago."

"Today was different," Ranu said. "Today they called from the south field. I heard it. Ask Mara."

Mara, gathering the soiled cloths, did not look up. "I heard cranes," she said, which was not the same thing, and Ranu chose to hear it as confirmation.

He reached out and touched the child's hand with one finger, the back of her hand, lightly, the way you touch something you are not sure you are allowed to touch, and the child's fingers opened and closed around his finger without waking. He looked at Yanu over the child's head with an expression she had no word for. She did not try to find one. She only looked back at him, and the fire settled in the hearth, and outside the cranes called from somewhere she could not see.

That was the morning., -

The first winters passed the way early years pass when the season is generous. Steadily, without drama. Taya grew. She put on weight through the cold months as if the cold itself were a kind of nourishment. She learned to walk in her second summer, then to run, then to stop running long enough to watch things: the cranes crossing the sky, the swallows cutting low over the river, a beetle making its way across the packed earth of the floor. She did not point at what she watched. She simply watched, with the slow deliberate tracking of a person taking inventory of the world, and Yanu watched her watching and felt the thing she could not name settle deeper into her chest.

The cranes came back each spring and Ranu told the story of Taya's birth again, as he told it every spring, as he would tell it every spring for as long as anyone stood still long enough to hear it. Taya, by her fourth or fifth year, had stopped looking at her father while he told the story. She watched the birds instead.

She was seven years old when the fever came., -

Yanu returned to the spring the way you return to a fixed point when you are lost. Not because it helps, but because it is the last place you were certain of your position. The child on her chest. The cord still warm between them. Ranu's finger caught in the small fist. The ember-light orange against the wattle wall, and outside the sound of cranes, and the whole world still ahead of them, every morning of it, every spring.

She had not known, then, how Taya would grow. The birds especially. The way she tracked them before weather changed, the way a sandhill crane calling from the east meant something different than one calling from overhead. Yanu herself had learned to read birds from her own mother, who had learned it from hers, and she had thought: here is someone I can teach. She had thought that while the child was still new enough that her eyes could not yet focus, while she was still a warm weight against Yanu's side in the sleeping hours, still learning what the world was made of.

She had thought: here is someone I can teach, and she had taught her, and the teaching had taken years, and the years had been good years, and she had not known how few of them there would be.

The early seasons had been generous. The cranes came back, and then the swallows, and the maygrass came up clean without the black rot that had taken half the stored seed the year before. Taya had watched the swallows from the doorway, her head turning to follow each bird across the sky with the slow deliberate tracking of someone taking notes. She had not laughed at the birds. She had watched them. Ranu had laughed at everything. He laughed at the way she watched, laughed at the way she turned her head, told the story of her watching birds to anyone who stood still long enough to hear it. But Taya herself had only watched, and Yanu had understood something in that watching she could not have named.

She had understood that this child was going to be quiet in the way that mattered. Not silent. She had cried when she was hungry, cried when she was cold, argued with Kato over the last of the autumn berries with a ferocity that made Yanu hide her face to keep from laughing. But quiet in her attention, in the way she gathered the world in before she responded to it.

Yanu had been proud of that. She had not said so to Ranu, who would have made a story of it, but she had been proud.

She was proud of it still. She held it the way she held the pendant now, turning it in her fingers in the dark of the house. The metal was cold. The thong was stiff with years of her own sweat and smoke. She thought: she watched the cranes. She watched the swallows. She turned her head toward the ember-light before she ever cried. She was here for seven years and she watched everything.

She had been here. She had been entirely, completely here.

Yanu pressed the pendant against her sternum and held it there.

Outside, the wind moved through the thatch, and the coals in the hearth breathed their dim orange breath, and the house held its cold winter smell of damp reed-mat and old smoke, and Yanu sat in the dark and returned to the spring, to the cord still warm, to the finger caught in the small fist, to the cranes calling from the south field, and she stayed there as long as she could., -

The fever came in the night between the third and fourth week of the cold season, in the seventh winter of Taya's life, when the stored seed was already down by a third and the deer hides stacked beneath the sleeping platform had begun to smell of the damp that had crept in through the base of the wall. She had been trying not to hear that smell as an omen.

She woke to Taya's breathing. Not to a cry. Taya did not cry. She woke to the sound of breathing that was wrong, too fast, the way a hide bellows sounds when someone works it in a hurry, and she put her hand on the child's chest before she was fully awake and felt the heat coming off her like a stone pulled from a fire.

She did not wake Ranu.

She lifted Taya and carried her to the hearth and sat down with her back against the clay-plastered stone, the child held upright against her chest, and she began to count the breaths. Thirty in the time she could say the names of all the families she knew. Too many. She wet a cloth in the water jar and wiped the child's face, her neck, the creases of her elbows, and Taya turned her face into the cloth without waking, and Yanu counted again.

By morning the count had not changed.

Ranu woke and saw her face before he saw the child's and went quiet in a way that was unlike him. He boiled water and steeped the dried herbs Mara had left after the birth. The ones she said were for fever, boneset and yarrow bound together with a strip of bark, though she had said it the way people say *if you ever need it* and mean *you probably won't*. Yanu held the cup to Taya's lips and coaxed a little of it in, and the child swallowed without opening her eyes.

"She'll turn," Ranu said.

Yanu said nothing.

"Children run hot. You know this. You've seen it."

"I know," Yanu said.

She had seen it. She had seen children burn for three days and come back laughing. She had also seen the other kind, and she knew already, in the place below knowing, which kind this was. She did not say it. She held the child and counted the breaths and wet the cloth again when it dried, and Ranu told her about a boy from his mother's household who had burned for a full week and recovered so completely that he grew to be the largest man in the settlement, and Yanu listened to the story and counted breaths and said nothing.

The second day the breathing slowed. She had thought slower would mean better and then she counted it and understood it did not mean better. Slower and shallower both. Taya's skin had gone from hot and dry to hot and damp, a fine sweat along her hairline that Yanu kept wiping away, and when Yanu pressed her lips to the child's forehead the heat was a living thing, something with its own intention.

She tried the tea again. Most of it ran back out.

Mara came and looked and did not say the thing Yanu could see her not saying. She brought more of the dried herbs and a different preparation, something that smelled of pine resin and something else Yanu could not name, and she showed Yanu how to rub it into the soles of the child's feet. Yanu did it. The child's feet were small. She had known that, had known it every time she helped Taya into her moccasins, but she had not let herself think about the size of the child's feet until she was holding them in her hands, and then she had to stop and breathe for a moment before she could continue.

"Keep her warm," Mara said. "But not too warm."

It was not useful advice. Yanu understood that Mara knew it was not useful and had said it anyway because there was nothing useful to say.

The third day was the same as the second. The breathing neither worsened nor improved. Yanu slept in intervals of an hour or less, waking each time to put her hand on the child's chest and count. Ranu kept the fire. He did not tell stories. He sat across the hearth from her and fed the fire and watched her face, and she understood that he was doing the only thing he could think to do, which was to keep the light steady, and she was grateful for it in a way she could not have said aloud.

On the fourth day Taya opened her eyes.

They were glassy and did not track the way they usually tracked, that slow deliberate following of movement that Yanu had come to know as the child's particular way of attending to the world. But they were open, and Yanu put her face close and said the child's name, and the eyes moved toward her voice.

Ranu began a new story. Something about the cranes, about what it meant that the child had come with the cranes and so the cranes would look after her, and Yanu let him tell it. She understood that he needed to tell it. She held Taya and listened to the story and counted the breaths and watched the eyes.

Then she reached up and lifted the pendant from her neck.

She had not planned to. The thong caught at her hair and she worked it free and held the pendant where Taya could see it, held it in the light above the child's face, turning it slowly so the firelight moved across the copper. Taya's eyes tracked it. Not perfectly, not with the full deliberate attention of her ordinary watching, but the eyes moved, following the slow turning of the metal, and Yanu felt something release in her chest.

She lowered the pendant until it rested against the child's palm. Taya's fingers did not close around it, but the hand was warm against Yanu's fingers, and the eyes were still open, and the breathing was still there, and Yanu sat with the pendant between her palm and her daughter's palm and counted the breaths.

By evening the eyes had closed again and the breathing had gone shallower still.

Yanu put the pendant back on. She did not sleep.

The fifth day she could not get anything into her. Not water. Not the tea. The child's lips were dry and slightly cracked at the corners, and Yanu wet them with her finger and held the child and rocked without knowing she was rocking.

The sixth day the breathing changed again. There were pauses now. Not long, a beat, two beats, and then the breath came back, but the pauses were there, and Yanu counted them the way she had counted everything else. She counted them because counting was the only thing she had left that felt like doing something.

Ranu sat across the hearth from her and had stopped talking. He watched her face more than the child's. She knew he was reading her the way she read the birds, looking for the sign that would tell him what was coming, and she kept her face still because she did not want to be the thing that told him.

She did not sleep on the sixth night.

She sat with her back against the hearthstone and held Taya against her chest and felt each breath go in and out, and the pauses between them grew longer and she counted the pauses too. One count, two, three, and then the breath came back, and she let herself breathe again with it.

The house was very quiet. Outside the wind had dropped and there was only the occasional creak of the roof timbers, settling in the cold, and the faint sound of Ranu's breathing from the sleeping place where he had finally gone at her insistence, because one of them needed to sleep and it was not going to be her.

The fire had burned down to coals. The light was dim and orange and steady, the way fire is when there is nothing left to consume but the deepest heart of the wood. Yanu did not add more fuel. She sat in the diminishing light and held her daughter and counted.

The pauses grew longer.

She counted to five. To seven. And then the breath came back, shallower than before, barely enough to feel against her palm, but there.

She counted to nine.

The breath came back.

She counted to twelve.

She waited.

The seventh day came in the dark, the way days come in winter. Not with light, not with any announcement, but with the slow alteration of the cold, the hour before first gray when the frost deepens and the timbers contract and the house makes its small sounds of adjustment. Yanu had counted through the night and she knew by the quality of the dark that the night was ending, and she sat with Taya against her chest and counted, and the pauses between the breaths were longer now than the breaths themselves.

She counted to fifteen, to twenty, and the house was very still, and the coals breathed their dim orange breath, and Yanu sat with her hand flat on the child's chest and felt the warmth there, the warmth that was still there, and she waited, and she counted, and the breath did not come back.

She did not call out.

She sat for a long time with her hand on the child's chest, feeling the warmth that was still there and would be there for a while yet, and she thought: *she is still warm*. She thought this the way you think a thing when you are trying to stay in the last moment before the next one arrives. She held it. She held the child and she held the last moment and she did not let the next one come yet.

The coals settled. A small sound, barely anything.

Yanu pressed her lips to the top of Taya's head. The child's hair smelled of woodsmoke and the pine-resin preparation and underneath both of those things something else, something that was only Taya, that Yanu had known from the first morning, from the cord still warm between them, from the finger caught in the small fist.

She breathed it in.

She sat in the dark house with the coals going gray at the edges and her daughter's weight against her chest, and she did not call out, and Ranu slept, and the world outside was silent and frozen and did not know yet what had happened inside this house, and Yanu sat with the fact of it alone, the way you sit with something before you have to share it, before it becomes a thing that belongs to everyone's grief instead of only yours.

She held the pendant in her free hand. She had not known she had reached for it.

The metal was cold. The thong was stiff. She turned it in her fingers in the dark and felt the place where the loop had worn smooth from years of her own touching, and she held her daughter, and she did not call out, and the house held its cold winter smell of damp reed-mat and old smoke, and outside nothing moved, and inside nothing moved, and Yanu sat in the last quiet moment before the world changed and held it for as long as she could., -

The coals were ash-gray by the time Ranu woke.

She heard him shift in the sleeping place, heard the small sounds of a man coming back to himself, the intake of breath, the rustle of hide, the pause that meant he had remembered. Then silence. Then his feet on the packed earth floor.

He came around the hearth and stopped.

She did not look up. She felt him standing there the way you feel a change in the air.

He made a sound. Not a word. The beginning of one, a syllable that had no ending, that broke off before it could become anything. She heard him try to draw breath around it and fail. He tried again. The second time was worse than the first. The sound came up and hit something and collapsed back into him, and then there was only his breathing, which was not steady.

Yanu looked at Taya's face.

The child's features had smoothed in the night. That was the only way she could think of it. Smoothed, the way clay smooths when the water leaves it and it begins to dry. The small crease between the brows was gone. The mouth was slightly open, the way it had been in sleep, and the lips were pale now at the center where they had been pink. Yanu had closed the eyes herself, hours ago, in the dark. She had used two fingers and pressed gently, the way her own mother had shown her once, the way you do it so they stay.

Ranu sat down on the edge of the sleeping place. He put his hands on his knees. He was looking at Taya and his mouth was moving without sound, which was the most frightening thing Yanu had ever seen from him, because Ranu's mouth was never without sound. He had words for everything. He had words for the way light moved on water and words for the particular silence before snow and words for the feeling of a task finished and a task not yet begun. He told stories the way other people breathed, not as a choice but as a function, as the thing that kept him upright. She had been tired of it sometimes. She had sat across the fire from him and felt the stories washing over her and thought: *be quiet, just once, be quiet*. She had not known until this moment that she had been wrong to think it. She had not known that his silence would feel like this. Like the house itself had stopped.

He tried a third time. This time she heard what he was reaching for, the first sound of Taya's name, and it did not come. His throat worked. His hands pressed harder against his knees. He looked at the floor.

She did not reach for him.

She understood that she should. She understood that he was her husband and that he was drowning and that the right thing, the human thing, was to put her hand on his arm or his shoulder or to say something that would give him a rope to hold. She understood all of this and she sat with Taya against her chest and she did not move. She was not able to. Whatever she had that would have reached for him was being used for something else entirely, and she could not spare it. She thought: *I am sorry*, and she thought it toward him the way you think a thing you cannot say aloud, and she held the child and she did not move.

Outside, she heard the first sound.

Footsteps on frozen ground. More than one person. The crunch of frost under feet, then voices, low, not yet at the door, and she knew that someone had seen the smoke had not risen this morning, or had heard something in the night, or had simply woken with the knowledge that sits in the body before the mind names it. They would come. They always came. This was what community meant: they came, and they stood outside the door, and they waited, and then they came in, and the grief became shared, which was supposed to be a relief.

She did not want relief.

She wanted her daughter.

She wanted the specific weight of Taya against her chest, the particular warmth that was already changing. Still there, still present, but different now, the warmth of a stone that has been in the sun rather than the warmth of something that makes its own heat. She pressed her lips again to the top of the child's head. The hair was soft and fine and smelled of woodsmoke and underneath the woodsmoke something that was Taya alone, some particular living smell that was already beginning to be past tense, and Yanu pressed her face into it and breathed.

The footsteps stopped outside the door.

A voice, she thought it was Mara, the broad woman whose hands knew what to do with grief, said something low and careful. An acknowledgment. A question with no question in it. *We are here.*

Ranu stood. He moved to the door with his hands at his sides and his face doing something she could not see from where she sat. He pushed the hide aside and went out, and the cold air came in for a moment, sharp and clean, before the hide fell back.

She heard him outside. She heard the voices of the others, the murmur of people arranging themselves around a new fact, the particular sound of a gathering that knows its purpose and does not yet know what to do with its hands. She heard Mara's voice again, steadier now, asking something. She heard Ranu try to answer.

The sound he made carried through the hide and through the cold air and into the house, and it was nothing. It was the shape of speech with no substance in it. A man trying to say a thing and finding the thing would not be said.

The gathering went quiet.

That silence, the silence of people outside waiting for words that did not come, was worse than weeping would have been. Weeping she could have borne. Weeping was sound and sound was the world still moving. This was the world stopped, the world holding its breath the same way she had held her breath through the night, waiting for the next breath that did not come.

She pulled the pendant from her neck.

The thong caught at her hair and she worked it free without hurrying. The metal lay in her palm, small, cold, green-dark along the edges where the patina had built up over years, brighter where it had rubbed against her skin. She had worn it so long she had stopped feeling it. She felt it now. The weight of it. The specific weight of a thing that has been held by more hands than you can name, that carries in its metal the warmth of every neck it has hung against, every fire it has been near, every winter it has survived.

She turned it in her fingers.

She did not know yet what she would do with it. She knew only that she could not put it back on. That was the one clear thing. She could not put it back on and go on wearing it as if the world were the same world, as if the pendant were the same pendant, as if she were the same woman who had put it on that morning. She was not. The woman who had put it on that morning had a daughter. The woman sitting here now had the weight of a daughter and the cooling fact of her and the smell of her hair and the memory of her hand around one finger, and that was not the same thing, and the pendant could not go back on her neck as if it were.

Outside, someone had begun to speak quietly. Not Ranu. Another voice, older, a woman's, the measured sound of someone filling a silence because silence had gone on long enough. The gathering shifted. She heard feet on frozen ground, the careful sound of people moving without purpose, waiting.

Ranu did not come back in.

She understood that he was standing out there in the cold with the others and that his mouth was still not working and that the others were standing close to him because that was what you did, and that all of this was correct and right and the way things went, and she sat inside the house with her daughter and the pendant cold in her palm and she did not go out.

The pendant had a small loop at the top, worn bright where the thong had passed through it for years. She put her thumb against the bright place and felt the smoothness there, the smoothness of long use, the smoothness of a thing that has been worn and touched and worn again until the metal has a kind of memory in it, a record of all the touching.

She thought: *I cannot keep it.*

She thought: *I cannot give it away.*

She sat with both thoughts at once, the way you sit with two things that cannot both be true and are.

The cold air pressed under the door-hide. The coals breathed their last gray breath. Outside, the gathering waited, and Ranu stood among them with his silence, and Yanu sat in the house with her daughter and the pendant and the two thoughts that could not both be true, and she did not move, and the morning went on without her., -

The pendant was small enough to cover with one hand. She had not known that. She had worn it for so many years that she had stopped seeing it as a thing with dimensions, a thing that existed in space. It was simply part of her, the slight drag of it at her throat, the way it shifted when she bent, the faint tick of metal against her collarbone when she moved quickly. She had stopped noticing it the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat.

She noticed it now.

She held it over Taya.

Not touching. Held above. The way you hold a lamp over something you need to see clearly before you decide what to do with it.

Taya's face had gone still in the way that faces go still when the thing that makes them a face has left. The eyes were closed. Yanu had done that herself, in the dark, with her fingers, because the eyes had been partly open and she could not bear it. The small mouth was slightly parted. The color had gone out of the lips, not gray, not yet, but the particular pale of something no longer being fed from within, the way a fire goes to ash not all at once but by degrees, the orange leaving first, then the red, then the last faint heat.

She had been beautiful. She was still beautiful. That was the part that made no sense. The beauty had not left with the rest of it. It was still there in the shape of her, in the line of her jaw and the curl of her ear and the way her hair lay across her forehead, fine and dark, and Yanu looked at all of it and held the pendant above it and felt the weight of the pendant in her hands and felt the weight of her daughter below it and could not make the two weights into one thought.

Her mother had worn this pendant. Her mother's mother before that, though Yanu held that knowledge the way you hold a story told in firelight, true in the telling, harder to see in the morning. The metal had come from somewhere north and far. Her mother had said: *from the place where the water is so large you cannot see the other side.* She had asked as a child asks about a thing they cannot picture, *is it larger than the river?*, and her mother had said *larger than everything you know*, and that had not helped her picture it but had helped her understand that the pendant was old in a way that most things were not old, that it had traveled a distance most things did not travel, and that it was hers now to keep and to pass on.

She had kept it. She had worn it every day of her adult life, through two pregnancies, through the summer the river ran low and the fish did not come, through the winter three years ago when the cold lasted so long that the bark on the young birches split and bled and they burned the split pieces because there was nothing else. She had worn it through all of that. She had worn it this morning when she woke and found the breathing had stopped.

She could not put it back on.

She turned it in her palms. The loop at the top was worn to a high shine, brighter than the rest, because the thong had passed through it every day for years and the friction had polished the metal down to its truest color. The edges held the patina, green-dark, almost black in the deepest grooves, the color of old copper left to the air. Where it had lain against her skin, the metal was warmer in tone, almost brown, the patina worn away by warmth and oil and the slow press of living. She had never looked at it this carefully. You do not look carefully at the things you wear every day. You look at them only when you are deciding what to do with them.

She was deciding.

She did not know yet what she was deciding. That was the truth of it. She sat with the pendant in both hands above her daughter's still body and she did not know, and she understood that not knowing was not the same as having no answer. The answer was somewhere in her already. It had been forming since the moment the breathing stopped, maybe before that, maybe in the long night when she had held Taya against her chest and felt the heat of the fever burning and burning and then, at some hour she could not name, simply not burning anymore.

She had thought, in those first years after Taya was born, that she would give the pendant to her when she was old enough. She had watched the girl grow into herself, the watchfulness, the patience, the way she held still when other children fidgeted, and she had thought: when she is a woman, I will sit with her and give her the full account. She had planned to wait until Taya could sit still for the length of a story, until she could repeat it back, until she had the patience for the kind of listening that a pendant like this required. Seven years was not enough. She had always thought there would be more.

She had waited too long. She had planned to wait a little longer and she had waited too long and now there was no one to give it to.

That was the fact. She turned the pendant in her fingers and that was the fact beneath all the other facts, the one that made the others make sense in the way that a stone at the bottom of a pile makes sense of the pile's shape. There was no one to give it to. Taya was the one she had been keeping it for, the one she had been wearing it toward, and Taya was here and not here, present and gone, her face still her face and her body already beginning to be something other than her body, and the pendant had nowhere to go.

She could not keep it. She had known that from the moment she pulled it from her neck. Every time she looked at it she would see this room. This light, the thin gray of early morning pressing through the gaps in the thatch, the fire gone to coals, the cold that had crept in from the ground up through the packed earth of the floor and into her legs and her back and her hands. This child. The specific weight of this child against her chest through the night, growing lighter as the hours went on, not because Taya was lighter but because Yanu's arms had learned the weight and stopped registering it, the way you stop hearing a sound that does not stop.

She would see all of it. Every time. The pendant would carry this morning in its metal the way metal carries heat, invisibly, completely, giving it back to you the moment you touch it.

She could not give it to a living person. She understood this too, though she could not have said why in words that would have satisfied Ranu, who would have wanted reasons, who would have turned the question over and looked at it from all sides the way he turned everything over. She could not have explained it except to say that the pendant was now Taya's in a way it had never been anyone else's, that the giving-to had already happened in the fourth-day firelight when she had held it above the child's face and the glassy eyes had tracked the turning metal, and that to give it now to someone living would be to give away something that no longer belonged to her to give.

She held it in both hands. She brought her hands together until the pendant rested in the cup of her palms, both thumbs touching the metal, and she held it that way over her daughter's body and she did not move.

It required this. This sitting. This not-knowing. This holding of the pendant in both hands above the child and the slow understanding that the answer was already there, had been there since the night, was forming itself in the dark the way ice forms on still water. Not all at once, not in a single moment you can point to, but by degrees, each degree invisible, until you look and it is done.

She looked at the pendant. She looked at her daughter.

The pendant had come from far away. It had traveled to reach her hands. It had passed through hands she could name and hands she could not name and it had arrived at her neck and she had worn it and now it was here, in this room, above this child, and Yanu understood, not all of it, not yet, but the beginning of it, that the traveling was not finished.

That it had somewhere still to go.

She did not know where. She did not know the form the going would take. She knew only that the pendant was not done, that it had not reached the end of its traveling, and that she was the one who would send it on, and that this knowledge was both a relief and a weight she had not expected, and that she would sit with it until she understood what it asked of her.

She sat.

The coals breathed. The smoke from outside came faint through the thatch. Her daughter lay still below her hands, and the pendant lay still in her palms, and Yanu sat between the two stillnesses and waited for the knowledge to finish forming., -

The women came when she called them.

She did not call with words. She stood and lifted the door-hide and they were there. Mara first, then two others whose names she knew and whose faces she could not have described to you in that moment, and they came in without speaking and they set to work with the quiet efficiency of people who have done this before and know that speech costs something the room cannot afford.

Yanu stepped outside.

The cold hit her face like a flat hand. The fire someone had built was burning well now, past the green-wood smoke, settled into itself, and a man she did not look at was feeding it from a stack of split birch. The birch bark caught with a thin yellow flame before the wood beneath took hold. She could smell it, the clean, slightly sweet smell of birch burning, the smell of winter mornings and of gatherings, and she stood with her back to the door-hide and breathed it and let the cold take the warmth from her face and her hands.

Ranu was at the fire.

He looked at her when she came out and she saw him try to speak and she watched the words fail him. His mouth opened and what came out was not sound. He closed it. He tried again. His throat worked and his eyes were red and the silence that came from him was the loudest thing she had heard all morning. Ranu, who told stories the way other people breathed. Ranu, who had a word for everything and a story behind the word and another story behind that one, who could talk from first light to dark and never once repeat himself. He stood at the fire with his mouth working and nothing coming out of it, and Yanu looked at him and understood that she loved him completely and that she could not help him and that there was nothing to say to him that would not cost her something she needed to keep.

She crossed the yard to where he stood and she put her hand on his arm. That was all. He put his hand over hers and they stood at the fire and did not speak.

People came. They came the way people come to these things, quietly, in ones and twos, from the direction of their own fires, their breath visible in the cold air, their faces already arranged into the expression that this kind of morning calls for. She knew them all. She had known them all her life. She watched them come and she felt the gathering form itself around her the way a river forms around a stone, not with intention, not with plan, just with the logic of water finding the shape of what is there.

She did not want them.

She understood that this was wrong of her, or not wrong exactly, but ungrateful. They were here because they loved Taya, or because they loved Yanu, or because they understood that this was what you did, that you came, that the coming was itself the thing. But she felt their presence as a weight and she wanted to put it down. She wanted the yard empty. She wanted the fire and the cold air and Ranu's hand over hers and nothing else.

She kept her hand on Ranu's arm and she let the gathering happen around her.

At some point Mara came to the door and said something to the two women who had gone in with her and they nodded and went back inside. Yanu did not ask what had been said. She knew what was being done. The body was being washed and dressed and arranged. Taya was being made ready. The pendant was in Yanu's hand. She had not put it down, had carried it out with her when she stepped outside, had held it through all of this, her fist closed around the metal, and she felt its weight there, the slight warmth it had taken from her palm, the edge of it against the heel of her hand.

She did not know how long she stood there.

At some point Ranu found his voice. He said her name, just her name, nothing else, and she heard in it everything he could not say, the whole weight of the morning compressed into two syllables, and she turned and looked at him and he looked at her and there was nothing to do with that look except receive it and hold it.

"I know," she said.

He nodded. His eyes were wet. He did not wipe them.

"I know," she said again, though she was not sure what she was answering., -

They carried Taya to the mound in the middle of the morning, when the light had lifted enough to see clearly but the cold had not yet softened. Six people carried her. Yanu walked beside them. She had asked to carry her herself and Mara had looked at her with the steady, unsentimental look of a woman who has seen what it costs and said, quietly, that she should let others carry the weight of it, that her arms would need to be empty for what came after. Yanu had not argued. She walked beside the six and she watched Taya's face. They had left her face uncovered. The cold had changed it, had smoothed it in a way that the fever had not, so that it looked more like sleep than the fever had ever looked, more like the ordinary sleep of an ordinary night, which was the worst thing about it.

The mound was a low rise above the river terrace, no taller than a man, older than anyone's memory. The ground around it was frozen, the frost-stiffened grass pale and brittle, crackling under their feet. The earth of the mound itself was darker than the surrounding ground, darker and slightly damp where the frost had not quite reached, and when Yanu stepped onto it she felt the difference through the soles of her feet. Not softer, exactly, but more yielding, as though the ground here remembered being turned.

The others who had been digging stepped back. There was a shallow trench in the southern face, the dark earth piled to one side, still faintly steaming in the cold air. A wooden digging stick, its fire-hardened tip dark with soil, stood propped against the pile.

Yanu stopped at the edge of the mound.

She let the six who carried Taya go ahead. She stood at the edge and she looked at the ground and she felt the pendant in her closed fist and she did not move.

Behind her, the gathering had followed. She could hear them, the sound of breath, the crunch of frost under feet, the small sounds of people standing still in cold air. She did not turn around. She kept her eyes on the mound, on the dark line of the trench, on the pale winter grass at the edge of the rise.

She opened her hand.

The pendant lay in her palm. The copper was dark, green-dark along the edges where it had sat against her skin for years, brighter in the center where her thumb had rubbed it through the night. It was small. She had always known it was small and yet it surprised her now, how small it was, how much it had come to occupy in her thinking, how much space a thing this size could take up in a life.

Taya had held it once. One time, in the early days of the fever, on the fourth day when the eyes had opened and tracked the turning metal, Yanu had lowered the pendant until it rested against the child's palm. Taya's fingers had not closed around it, but the hand had been warm, and the eyes had moved, and Yanu had sat with the pendant between her palm and her daughter's palm and counted the breaths. That was the only time.

Yanu closed her hand again. She stood at the edge of the mound with her fist closed around the pendant and she looked at the place where Taya would be put into the ground and she felt the question settle into her the way cold settles into a body. Not all at once, but completely, until it is part of you and you cannot say where you end and it begins.

What does it mean to keep something.

What does it mean to let it go.

She did not have the answer. She had the question, and the pendant in her hand, and her daughter in the ground ahead of her, and the gathering behind her breathing in the cold air, and Ranu somewhere at her back with his ruined voice and his red eyes and his stories that had all gone quiet. She had all of this and she had the mound under her feet, the old dark earth that had been turned before and would be turned again, the ground that held the ones who had come before and would hold the ones who came after, and she stood in the middle of all of it and she did not move.

Then she raised her closed fist.

She raised it to the level of her face, her arm bent at the elbow, her fist at the height of her own mouth, and she opened her mouth and she said her daughter's name.

Not as ceremony. Not as the beginning of a ritual or the close of one. She said it the way you say a name when the person is in the next room and you want them to know you are thinking of them. She said it once, into the cold air above the mound, into the pale winter light, into the silence of the gathering behind her and the silence of the ground ahead.

Taya.

The sound left her mouth and traveled out over the mound and was gone.

She stood for a moment longer with her fist raised and her mouth still open slightly, as though she might say something else. She did not. There was nothing else. There was only the name, and the mound, and the pendant in her hand, and the question she had been sitting with since the night, and the cold, and the long work of the morning still ahead of her, and Ranu, who would try to find his voice again, who would try to make a story out of this because it was the only thing he knew how to do with the things that broke him, and who would fail, and try again, and fail, and try.

She lowered her arm.

She looked at the trench. She looked at the dark earth piled to one side, still faintly steaming. She looked at Taya's face, uncovered in the cold air, smoothed and pale and still entirely her.

The answer had finished forming.

She walked to the edge of the trench and she knelt in the frozen grass and she pressed the pendant face-up into the dark earth at the bottom of the shallow cut. She pressed it with both thumbs until the soil closed around its edges, until only the face of it was visible, a small bright circle in the dark ground. She held her hands there for a moment. The earth was cold. The pendant was cold. She felt both against her palms and she did not move.

Then she took her hands away.

She stood. She stepped back from the trench. She nodded to the six, who were waiting, who had been waiting with the patience of people who understood that this moment had needed to happen before the next one could, and they brought Taya forward, and they laid her in the ground above the pendant, and the dark earth received them both.

Yanu stood at the edge and watched.

Behind her, the gathering was very still. She heard Ranu's breathing, still unsteady, still catching, and she heard the small sounds of the six working, and she heard the frost-stiff grass creak under someone's shifting weight, and she heard nothing else. The world had gone quiet in the way it goes quiet when something that cannot be undone is being done, when the moment is too large for ordinary sound.

She watched the earth go back.

She watched it cover the pendant and cover her daughter and she did not look away. She had thought she would not be able to watch and she found that she could not look away, that looking was the thing she had left to give, that bearing witness was the last form of keeping.

When it was done she stood for a long time with her hands at her sides and the mound at her feet, the dark earth newly turned, slightly raised where it had been filled in, the frost-grass pushed back along the edges of the trench. In a season it would settle. In a year the grass would grow back over it and the line of the trench would be invisible and the mound would look as it had always looked, a low rise above the river terrace, older than anyone's memory, holding what it held.

She put her hand on the earth.

The cold came through her palm immediately, the deep cold of ground that the sun had not reached in weeks. She pressed her hand flat and felt the cold and felt the earth and felt, underneath the cold and the earth, nothing. Nothing she could name or point to, no warmth, no presence, no sign that anything below her hand was anything other than soil and the deep dark of a winter mound. She had not expected to feel anything. She had not come here looking for comfort of that kind. She had come here because this was where Taya was, and this was where the pendant was, and she was the one who had put them both here, and she needed to put her hand on the ground and feel what was true.

What was true was cold earth and her own palm and the sound of the gathering behind her beginning, slowly, to breathe again.

She took her hand away.

She stood up.

She turned and looked at Ranu, who was looking at her with an expression she had no word for. Not the expression of a man who had found his story yet, but the expression of a man who was beginning, somewhere very deep, to understand that he would need one. She held his look for a moment. Then she turned back to the mound.

The earth was dark and still and cold and it held what it held.

She had no more to give it.

She turned and walked back through the gathering, and the people made way for her, and the cold air moved around her, and the birch fire crackled at the edge of the yard, and somewhere above the river the sandhill cranes called from a direction she could not see.

She walked toward the sound.
