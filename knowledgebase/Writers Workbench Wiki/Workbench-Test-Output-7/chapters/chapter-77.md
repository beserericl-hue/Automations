The report arrived on his desk at 6:58 a.m., dropped there by the overnight courier service the state used for sensitive materials, and Slade did not touch it for sixteen minutes.

He made coffee first. The machine in the hallway outside his office was a twelve-cup drip unit that the facilities team had replaced three times in eleven years, always with an identical model, and it produced coffee that tasted of the plastic it lived in. He filled his mug, a plain white state-issue thing, the kind that accumulated in government offices the way sand accumulated in shoes, and carried it back to his desk and sat down.

7:14 a.m. The fluorescent panels overhead had not yet warmed to their full brightness. They hummed at a frequency he had stopped noticing years ago but could hear now, in the quiet before his assistant arrived.

The report sat in a padded envelope, already opened by the mail room. Someone had printed a routing slip and stapled it to the outside: *18CH-447 / Section 106 Compliance Review / Att: G. Slade, Director.* Below that, in the mail room's rubber-stamp shorthand: *RCVD 06:41.* He pulled the envelope toward him and looked at it without opening it further.

He had been in this role eleven years. Before that, he had spent six years doing mound surveys across the mid-Atlantic, Virginia, Delaware, the Eastern Shore, two seasons in western Maryland where the ground was harder and the finds were older and the winters came in sideways off the ridge. He had written reports like the one in this envelope. He knew what they weighed when they were serious.

This one weighed serious.

He pulled it out. Forty-three pages, three-hole-punched, clipped into a clear-front binder. University of Maryland letterhead on the cover page, the departmental seal in the upper left corner, and below it:

*Field Assessment and Cultural Resource Significance Evaluation*
*Site 18CH-447, Charles County, Maryland*
*Submitted pursuant to 36 CFR Part 800, Section 106 Compliance Review*
*Principal Investigator: Dr. Tayak Moyaone*

He set his coffee down without drinking it.

He had signed her suspension notice six weeks ago. He had signed it because it was legally defensible. The permit extension had lapsed, the Section 106 consultation window had not been properly calendared, and Marsh at the agency had sent the risk assessment memo with the tracked-change draft that replaced *forthwith* with *immediately* and absorbed every other edit into a clean final. He had signed it. He had not left a voicemail.

He turned the cover page.

The table of contents was eight lines. *I. Site Description and Excavation History. II. Stratigraphic Analysis. III. Artifact Inventory and Significance Assessment. IV. Documentary Evidence. V. Oral History and Affidavit Record. VI. Chain of Custody Documentation. VII. Conflict of Interest Disclosure. VIII. Conclusion and Recommendation.*

Section VII. He had not expected a conflict of interest disclosure.

He turned to it first, not because he was looking for a way out, but because eleven years of compliance review had taught him that the disclosures were where the real document lived. The careful language, the deliberate exposure of the thing that could be used against you, told him more about a researcher's honesty than anything in the findings.

It was four paragraphs. He read them twice.

Dr. Moyaone disclosed that she was a member of the Piscataway tribal community, that she had a direct familial connection to the oral tradition recorded in Section V, and that her grandmother was the affiant in the oral history document attached as Exhibit F. She disclosed that she had been aware of this connection prior to beginning the excavation and had sought independent verification of each significant find. She named the independent verifiers. She noted that she had considered recusing herself and had decided against it on the grounds that recusal would remove the person most qualified to interpret the site from the site, and that this decision was itself a matter of record.

Slade read that last sentence a third time.

*She had considered recusing herself and had decided against it.*

He had known researchers who buried disclosures in footnotes, who used the passive voice to soften the admission, who attached them as appendices with the implicit hope that reviewers would not reach page forty-one. This disclosure was on page thirty-one, in the body of the report, in plain declarative sentences. It read like someone who had thought carefully about what honesty required and then done it.

He turned back to Section I.

The site description was precise without being showy. She wrote the way he had been trained to write: measurements, compass orientations, soil descriptions using Munsell codes, provenience recorded to the centimeter. He recognized the discipline. He had used it himself, on sites in the Shenandoah Valley and the Patuxent lowlands, on mornings that smelled of creek mud and wet grass, when the ground gave up its evidence slowly and you had to earn every datum.

He turned to Section II. The stratigraphic analysis was forty-seven lines of dense technical description. Seven distinct strata. The earliest deposit dated by associated materials to approximately 100 BC.

He stopped.

He looked at the number again. *100 BC.* Not colonial. Not contact-era. Two thousand years before Thomas Calvert received his patent.

He picked up his coffee. It had gone cold. He drank it anyway.

Outside his office, the building was beginning to fill. He could hear the elevator, the card reader beeping at the main door, the distant sound of the network printer cycling through its morning warm-up. His assistant would arrive in twenty minutes. The Governor's office would want his recommendation on the colonial landmark designation by end of business Friday. Today was Tuesday.

He turned to Section IV. *Documentary Evidence.*

The first exhibit was a photographic reproduction of a 1697 petition, the paper foxed at the edges, the ink dark brown and slightly oxidized along the letter-strokes. He could read the long *s* characters, the archaic spelling, the deferential opening formula. He had seen documents like this before, in the Maryland State Archives, in the Calvert County records room. He had never seen one attached to a Section 106 report.

He read the petition's first paragraph. *The humble Petition of the Piscataway People of Charles County, being the ancient possessors of the land at the high ground adjacent to Mattawoman Creek.*

He set the page down on his desk.

*Ancient possessors.* He knew that phrase. He had seen it once before, in a footnote in a 1978 Maryland law review article he had read during his first year in the role, when he was still learning the documentary record. Judge Notley's language, from a Provincial Court ruling that had been systematically reversed after his death.

The phrase had not appeared in a primary document before. Not in anything he had reviewed.

He looked at the petition reproduction for a long moment. The paper in the photograph was folded along two old creases, the kind that came from decades of storage in a court folio. The handwriting shifted between two different hands, one careful and practiced, one less so. At the bottom, below the signatures, a clerk's notation in a third hand: *Entered this day, Thomas Ware, Clerk.*

He turned the page and kept reading.

The stratigraphy section began on page twelve. Slade read it the way he had always read stratigraphy: from the bottom up, the way the ground actually worked, oldest first.

Stratum Seven. The deepest layer. Dark, undisturbed subsoil, Munsell 10YR 2/1, with a single cultural deposit at the base: one copper pendant, face-up, placed rather than dropped, associated organic material dated by AMS radiocarbon to approximately 100 BC, plus or minus forty years. The pendant was described in a separate artifact record, cross-referenced to Exhibit D. Provenience: Grid C-4, 187 centimeters below datum. No disturbance. No intrusion from above.

He had excavated a Woodland-period site in the Patuxent lowlands in his third year out of graduate school. He remembered the color of that soil, the same near-black, the same density, the way it held moisture even in August. He remembered the morning they found a deposit at a hundred and sixty centimeters and the field director had gone very quiet for a moment before she said anything. You learned, in this work, to recognize the difference between a thing that had been lost and a thing that had been placed.

This had been placed.

Stratum Six. A secondary deposit above the base, dated to approximately 500 CE. One carved antler figure, fragmentary, associated with a thin lens of red ochre. The report noted that the figure's style was consistent with Late Woodland period manufacture in the mid-Atlantic region and cited three comparative examples from published literature. Slade checked the citations. They were correct.

Stratum Five. Dated to approximately 1200 CE. One shell gorget, undecorated, with a single suspension hole. Provenience: Grid C-4, 143 centimeters below datum. The gorget had been placed on a prepared surface, a thin layer of clean sand that did not occur naturally in the surrounding soil matrix, which the report interpreted as deliberate placement rather than accidental deposition. Slade had seen this before, in the mid-Atlantic sites: the careful bed of imported sand, the object laid on it with intention. You did not do that by accident. You did not do that without knowing what you were doing and why.

Stratum Four. Approximately 1200 CE, a different deposit within the same general horizon. A second shell gorget, older in manufacture than the one above it, with two suspension holes and faint incised lines along the inner edge. Cross-referenced to Exhibit H. The report noted that the two gorgets appeared to have been deposited at different times within the same stratum, separated by a thin lamination of wind-blown silt that suggested a gap of years, possibly decades, between placements.

Someone had come back. More than once.

Stratum Three. Approximately 1740 CE. One ceramic pipe bowl, dark-fired, cord-marked exterior, consistent with mid-eighteenth-century Piscataway manufacture. The report cited a comparative analysis by a ceramicist at the Smithsonian whose name Slade recognized from conference proceedings. Cross-referenced to Exhibit I, which included a photographic reproduction of a second document: a partial folio from Charles County Court records, dated 1691, bearing a partially legible name, *Sioſ*, and a legal action against the Mount Friendship land grant. The folio was water-damaged. The name ended in a long *s* and a dash where the paper had failed.

He looked at the folio reproduction for a moment. The handwriting was colonial clerk's hand, cramped and slanted. The damage ran diagonally across the lower third of the page, as if water had pooled in the fold. Whatever name had been there was gone.

Stratum Two. Approximately 1697. One folded paper document, oilskin-wrapped, recovered from a secondary deposit fifteen centimeters below the surface. The document was the petition. He had already read the petition. He turned the page.

Stratum One. A glass trade bead, blue, recovered near the surface. The report cross-referenced it to a note in the oral history affidavit: a gift from a Lenape trading partner, the one object Saya had kept from her own mother.

He turned the page.

The artifact chain section was four pages. It cross-referenced every object to its provenience record, its chain-of-custody form, its conservation log, and its current storage location. The pendant was at the university conservation suite, catalogued as 18CH-447-051, received by a conservator named Adaeze Okonkwo at 15:05 on the day of recovery. The gorgets were in the state archaeological repository. The pipe bowl was in the state repository. The petition document was in the state repository, transferred under a separate NAGPRA-adjacent protocol that the report explained in a footnote.

Every transfer was signed. Every date matched. There were no gaps.

He had reviewed artifact chains for eleven years. He had seen gaps. He had seen transfers that skipped a signature, custody forms that listed a storage location without a receiving date, field bags that arrived at the repository with no corresponding field log entry. Gaps happened. Sometimes they were innocent, a field assistant who forgot to sign, a form misfiled in the wrong box. Sometimes they were not.

This chain had no gaps.

He set the artifact section down and picked up Section V. *Oral History and Affidavit Record.*

The affidavit was attached as Exhibit F, but the section began with a two-page summary. He read the summary first.

The oral history had been transmitted through a documented chain of named individuals across multiple generations, the earliest verifiable link being a woman referred to in the affidavit as Saya, associated with the 1697 petition. Each subsequent keeper was named, with approximate dates of transmission and the relationship to the preceding keeper. The chain ran from Saya forward. Okwi had wrapped the petition in oilskin and placed it in the mound when Saya's petition failed, and from that act of burial the chain of names had passed through Tessa, Matta, Ida, until it reached the affiant. Okwi was not a keeper of the tradition. He was the one who had understood that what could not survive in the courts might survive in the ground.

He turned to the last line of the summary.

*The affidavit attached as Exhibit F was provided by Nora Moyaone, age 82, of Billingsley Road, Charles County, Maryland, who has held the oral tradition in its current form for sixty-two years.*

He read it again.

*Nora Moyaone. Billingsley Road. Charles County.*

He knew that road.

He had driven it every day for the first eighteen years of his life. It ran south from Route 6, past the old Swann property, past the stand of tulip poplars that his father had always used as a landmark when giving directions, past the low spot where the road flooded in heavy rain and you had to judge whether the water was moving or standing before you drove through. He had driven through it standing once, in his mother's car, and the engine had died and he had walked two miles to the nearest house to use the phone.

He had grown up two miles from Billingsley Road.

He sat with that for a moment. The HVAC system clicked and a faint smell of hot dust moved through the vent above his desk. Somewhere down the hall the network printer was cycling again, its warm-up hum rising and falling.

He turned to the personal disclosure section. He had read it first, but he read it again now.

Dr. Moyaone disclosed that she was a Piscataway descendant. That she had a direct familial connection to the oral tradition in Section V. That her grandmother was the affiant in Exhibit F.

Her grandmother was the woman on Billingsley Road.

He had not known there was a Moyaone family on Billingsley Road. He had not known, growing up two miles away, that the woman in one of those houses had been carrying a chain of names for sixty-two years. He had not known, and he was a man who had spent eleven years studying the documentary record of Indigenous presence in this part of Maryland, who had a prior career doing mound surveys in the mid-Atlantic, who had read the 1978 law review article and recognized Judge Notley's phrase when he saw it in a primary document for the first time.

He had not known.

He thought about what that meant. Not in terms of the report, which was in front of him and whose evidentiary record was not affected by what he had or had not known growing up. He thought about it in the other way. The way that had nothing to do with his job.

He picked up the affidavit, Exhibit F, and turned to the first page.

It was typed, double-spaced, on plain white paper. The title read: *Affidavit of Nora Moyaone.* Below the title, in the standard jurat format, her address: Billingsley Road, Charles County, Maryland. Her age: 81 at the time of signing. She would be 82 now, in the present timeline of his reading. The notary's embossed seal was in the lower right corner of the last page. The commission expiration date was March 2026.

She had signed it in a hand that was slightly unsteady but deliberate, the letters formed carefully, as if she had taken her time. He ran his thumb across the notary's embossed seal, feeling the shallow ring pressed into the paper.

He read the first three paragraphs. She described receiving the oral tradition from a woman named Ida, who had received it from her own grandmother, who had received it from a woman she called Matta's line, a chain that went back, by her account, to a woman named Saya who had stood in a colonial assembly in 1697 with ink drying on her palm.

He stopped at that phrase. *Ink drying on her palm.*

He looked at the petition reproduction again, at the bottom of the page where the signatures were. Below the names in practiced hands, a single handprint, small, pressed in dark ink, the lines of the palm still legible after three hundred and twenty-seven years. A clerk's notation beside it: *her mark, Saya.*

He had read past it the first time. He had read it as a mark, the way illiterate signatories made marks. He looked at it again now.

It was not a mark. It was a handprint. Deliberate, full-palmed, pressed flat.

He set the affidavit down on top of the petition reproduction and looked at his desk. The binder was open to Exhibit F. The coffee cup was empty. The HVAC clicked again.

His assistant would be in in ten minutes. The Governor's office wanted his recommendation by Friday.

He turned to the conclusion section and began to read.

He had not filed the photocopies. He had not shown them to anyone. He had not written them into any memo or referenced them in any correspondence. They sat in the desk drawer on the left side, the deep one, the one that stuck slightly in damp weather, under a yellow legal pad, and each morning when he pulled the drawer open for a pen he saw the edge of the top sheet and did not take them out, and each evening when he left he was aware of them the way you are aware of a noise in another room that you have decided not to investigate.

He had found them by accident, or something close enough to accident that he had not yet decided what to call it. He had been at the Charles County Government Building records room six weeks ago, a Tuesday afternoon, going through published deed indices for a separate matter, a colonial mill site on the Patuxent, nothing to do with 18CH-447, when the chain of names in one volume had snagged him. Not a search. A stumble. Phil at the security desk had waved him through with a slow palm-out hand raise, and the clerk, a woman in her forties with reading glasses pushed into her hair, had retrieved the volumes without being asked, unlocking the glass case after she watched him work through the deed books for forty minutes. He had not explained what he found himself looking at. She had not asked.

He laid the photocopies on the desk beside Tayak's report.

The chain ran six instruments deep. He had arranged them in order, oldest first, the way you lay out a stratigraphic profile.

*Liber 4, folio 312. Charles County Court, 1683. County court order, forty shillings consideration, 120 acres at the high ground near Mattawoman Creek, to William Dent.*

He had read the boundary description that afternoon until he had it by memory. *The high ground near Mattawoman Creek formerly occupied by the Piscataway.* Eleven words. The colonial clerk had written them in a clerk's hand, economical and practiced, the long *s* in *Piscataway* curling slightly at the top. Eleven words that assumed their own sufficiency. *Formerly occupied*, as if occupation were a condition that ended cleanly, like a lease.

He read them again now.

Below Dent: Thomas Pruett, 1741. Below Pruett: his son James by will, 1778. Below James: Caleb Morse at an estate partition sale in 1801, the first deed to redraw the boundary description, the phrase *formerly occupied by the Piscataway* finally dropped, replaced with compass bearings and a reference to a stone marker that no longer existed. Below Morse: George Wills at a county tax sale in 1847. Below Wills: *Harold Slade, 1922, eight hundred dollars, Liber 27, folio 314.*

His great-grandfather's name in faded type.

He had known the farm went back to Harold. He had grown up knowing that. Harold Slade, 1922, eight hundred dollars. His grandmother had said it the way you say a founding myth, with the satisfaction of a thing that explained everything. He had not known what Harold had bought it from, or what George Wills had bought it from, or what Caleb Morse had bought it from, or what the 1683 court order had displaced to make William Dent's 120 acres possible at forty shillings.

He had not gone looking. He had stumbled into it, and then he had kept pulling the thread, because that was what he did. He was a man who followed chains of instruments to their source, except that in eleven years of reviewing the documentary record of Indigenous presence in this part of Maryland he had never once followed a chain of instruments that ended with his own name.

He laid the photocopy of the 1683 instrument beside the petition reproduction from Tayak's report. The petition was dated 1697. The court order was dated 1683. Fourteen years between them. The same creek. The same high ground.

He thought about Elena.

Three weeks ago she had called him on a Sunday evening, which was unusual. Elena called on Sundays when something had changed, when she had made a decision she wanted him to hear from her directly rather than from someone else. He had been in the kitchen. Carol was at the table with the Washington Post. He had taken the phone into the back hallway, which was where he went when he needed to hear clearly.

Elena had said: *I've accepted a position with the Piscataway tribal legal team. I wanted you to know before it was on the record anywhere.*

He had said: *All right.*

She had said: *It's the recognition petition. I've been building the legal framework for four years. This is the right move.*

He had said: *I know.*

She had said: *Dad.*

He had said: *I know, Elena. I'm not arguing with you.*

There had been a pause. He could hear, in the background of her apartment, the particular silence of a person who has prepared for a harder conversation and is recalibrating.

She had said: *I'm telling you because I think you should know who's on the other side of your desk.*

He had not said anything.

She had said: *I'm not asking you to do anything. I'm giving you the chance to be honest.*

He had said: *I understand.*

Then they had talked for twenty minutes about other things, her apartment, the drive from Annapolis, whether he and Carol were coming down for Thanksgiving, and he had gone back into the kitchen and Carol had looked up from the Post and he had said *Elena says hi* and Carol had said *how is she* and he had said *good, she's good* and he had sat down and not said anything else.

He had not told Carol what Elena was working on. He had not told anyone. He had not put it in writing.

He looked at the two documents side by side. The 1683 court order. The 1697 petition.

*The high ground near Mattawoman Creek formerly occupied by the Piscataway.*

The petition's handprint, Saya's handprint, was in the reproduction on the right. He had been looking at it for three hours. He looked at it again. Small, pressed flat, the lines of the palm still legible. A clerk's notation beside it: *her mark, Saya.* He had read it as a mark. It was not a mark. He had understood that for the last forty minutes and he was still understanding it.

He thought about Elena finding flint in the creek bottom as a child. She had been seven or eight. She had come up the bank with her hands full of worked stone and asked him what it was and he had told her: *Piscataway, probably. People lived here a long time before us.* She had held the flint for the rest of the afternoon and brought it to dinner and set it on the table beside her plate. Carol had said *not at the table* and Elena had put it in her pocket and he had not thought about it again for thirty years.

He thought about it now.

He pulled the desk drawer open again, the left one, the deep one, and looked at the space where the photocopies had been. The yellow legal pad was still there. He pushed the drawer closed.

He thought about what the 1683 instrument meant for the report on his desk. Not in terms of his family. That was a separate problem, one he had not solved and was not going to solve this afternoon. In terms of the report. In terms of the designation.

The colonial landmark designation for Site 18CH-447 was built on the premise that the site was associated with the 1687 Calvert patent, Thomas Calvert's grant, the *Friendship* tract, the colonial settlement at the high ground. That was the period of significance in the packet. That was the designation's evidentiary basis.

But the 1683 instrument, four years before the Calvert grant, described the high ground as *formerly occupied by the Piscataway*. The colonial record itself acknowledged prior Indigenous occupation at the moment of displacement. The landmark designation, as written, was not wrong about what it said. It was wrong about what it omitted.

The report in front of him documented that omission in forty-three pages of careful, qualified professional language, with a copper pendant in stratum seven and a chain of names going back to 100 BC and a handprint on a 1697 petition and an 82-year-old woman on Billingsley Road who had been carrying the oral tradition for sixty-two years two miles from his family's farm.

He sat with that.

The HVAC clicked. The hot-dust smell moved through the vent. Down the hall the network printer cycled again, its warm-up hum rising and then settling into the rhythm of a long print job.

He thought about what Elena had said. *I'm giving you the chance to be honest.*

She had not said: do the right thing. She had not said: use your position. She had said: be honest. As if honesty were the specific thing at risk. As if she had identified, with the precision she brought to everything, exactly what he was in danger of failing to be.

He looked at the desk. The binder open to Exhibit F. The photocopies beside it. The empty coffee cup. His phone, face-down, the way he left it when he needed to think.

He turned it over. He looked at the screen for a moment. Then he picked it up and dialed.

The Governor's office answered on the second ring. He recognized the voice, a policy aide whose name he always forgot, a careful young man who logged calls with time and caller and a one-line purpose and then routed them to the right portfolio.

Slade said: *This is Garrett Slade, Section 106 Compliance Review. I'm calling about the colonial landmark designation for Site 18CH-447 in Charles County. I need to pass a recommendation to the Legal Counsel's office.*

The aide said: *I can take a message, or I can transfer you.*

Slade said: *Transfer me.*

He waited. The hold music was a generic instrumental, slightly too loud. He looked at the handprint in the petition reproduction while he waited. Small. Pressed flat. Three hundred and twenty-seven years old and still legible.

Legal Counsel's office picked up.

He said: *I cannot recommend the colonial landmark designation for Site 18CH-447 as currently written. The period of significance is inaccurate and the designation packet omits material evidence of prior Indigenous occupation documented in the site record. I'm not in a position to put my name on it.*

The woman on the other end said: *Can you send that in writing?*

He said: *I'll send a summary conclusion. The detailed reasoning will be in the file.*

She said: *All right. By Friday?*

He said: *Yes.*

She said: *Thank you, Mr. Slade.*

He said: *Director.*

She said: *Sorry. Director Slade.*

He ended the call. He set the phone down on the desk, face-up this time.

He had not said why. He had not mentioned the 1683 instrument. He had not mentioned Elena. He had not mentioned Nora Moyaone on Billingsley Road or the handprint or the copper pendant or the fact that his great-grandfather's name was in Liber 27, folio 314, at the end of a chain of instruments that began with a county court order displacing the Piscataway from the high ground near Mattawoman Creek.

He had not needed to. The recommendation was the recommendation. The reasons were in the file, or they were in the drawer, or they were in him, in the part of him that had grown up two miles from Mattawoman Creek and had never once asked why the high ground was called the high ground.

He looked at the photocopies. He picked them up, tapped the edges square against the desk, and put them back in the drawer. He left the drawer open this time.

He turned to the conclusion section of Tayak's report and read it through to the end.

He had read it once already, quickly, in the first pass through the report. Now he read it again.

Tayak's conclusion ran four pages. The language was careful in the way that cost something, not hedged, not defensive, but precise the way a measurement is precise, leaving no room for imprecision to hide. She had written: *The stratigraphic and documentary evidence, taken together with the oral history affidavit submitted as Exhibit F, constitutes a continuous record of Piscataway presence at Site 18CH-447 from approximately 100 BC to the present.* She had not written *proves*. She had written *constitutes a record*. The distinction was the kind that only mattered to people who understood what the ground could and could not say.

Slade read the sentence again.

Then he turned back to Exhibit F.

The oral history affidavit was twenty-two pages, typed, double-spaced, on plain letterhead from a notary office in La Plata. The jurat at the bottom of the last page was dated fourteen months ago: *Subscribed and sworn before me this 9th day of September, 2023.* The notary's embossed seal had pressed a shallow ring into the paper that he could feel with his thumb. The affiant was listed as Nora Moyaone, age 81 at the time of signing. She would be 82 now. Resident of Billingsley Road, Charles County, Maryland, her entire life.

He had read the affidavit twice in the first hour. Now he was reading it a third time, and he was reading it differently, not for the legal architecture, not for the evidentiary weight, but for what it said.

Nora had organized the chain the way you organized a chain: link by link, each one named, each one connected to the next by what had been handed down and what had been kept. She had begun with herself. She wrote that she had received the oral history at age nineteen from her grandmother, Ida, in a kitchen in Charles County that smelled of woodsmoke and old coffee, that Ida had sat with her hands flat on the table and said *I need to tell you something before I lose the words for it.* Nora had written that sentence in the affidavit without apparent awareness that it was the most precise description of oral tradition he had ever read in a legal document. She had not called it oral tradition. She had called it *the thing Ida told me.*

The chain ran backward from Nora through Ida, and from Ida through a woman Nora called her grandmother's grandmother, a name Slade did not recognize, a woman who had died in the early years of the twentieth century. Then further back: a woman Nora called Tessa, associated with the shell gorget, who had stood alone at the mound after her brother walked past her on the road without stopping. Then Matta, who had sold pipe bowls to an English factor for eleven years and used that cover to sustain what the cover was meant to replace. Then Saya, who had pressed her palm flat on the petition because she had not wanted to write her name in English and had not wanted to make a mark that looked like she could not write.

He stopped at Saya.

He had read the 1697 petition three times in the past two hours, and each time he had come back to the same place: the handprint. Tayak's report included a high-resolution reproduction of the petition's final page, the ink brown-black and slightly eaten into the rag paper, the long-*s* characters that looked like *f* without the right-hand crossbar, the formulaic deferential language, *The humble Petition of the Piscataway People, humbly beseeching*, and at the bottom, below the signatures and the marks, a handprint. Small. Pressed flat. The ink slightly uneven at the edges of the palm, darker at the heel, lighter at the fingertips, as if the hand had been lifted carefully rather than peeled away.

He looked at his own hand on the desk. Then he looked at the reproduction.

He turned to Exhibit D.

The artifact chain of custody was thirty-one pages, tabbed, with catalog numbers and GPS coordinates and provenience notes for each object: the glass bead in stratum one, the oilskin-wrapped petition in stratum two, the ceramic pipe bowl in stratum three, the shell gorget in stratum four, and in stratum seven, at a depth of one hundred and eighty-seven centimeters below datum, in undisturbed matrix, the copper pendant. Catalog number 18CH-447-051. Recovered at 14:22 on a Tuesday in October. Transferred to the conservation suite at 15:05. Current custodian: Adaeze Okonkwo.

He had done enough mound surveys to know what a hundred and eighty-seven centimeters meant. It meant the pendant had not moved since it was placed. It meant whatever was below it had been below it for a very long time, undisturbed, in the dark.

He turned to Exhibit A, the field assessment proper, and found the footnote he had marked on the first pass. Footnote 23, page 31, in the section on documentary corroboration. Tayak had written: *The phrase 'ancient possessors' appears twice in the colonial record for Charles County, both instances traceable to the ruling of Judge Thomas Notley, Provincial Court, whose language was systematically reversed after his death by a bench that did not share his views on Indian land rights. The reversal process caused his original language to appear twice in the court record, once in the ruling itself and once in the instrument of reversal, which quoted the phrase in order to repudiate it. The phrase therefore survived its own erasure.* The footnote cited a 1978 Maryland law review article, a single footnote in that article, and nothing else. Tayak had found it. She had known what she was looking at.

*The phrase therefore survived its own erasure.*

He sat with that for a moment.

His assistant knocked. He did not answer. He heard her pause outside the door, then her footsteps moving back down the hall toward the outer office. The network printer cycled again. The HVAC clicked twice and went quiet.

He turned back to the conclusion.

Tayak had organized the final four pages as three numbered findings and a summary. The findings were: one, that the stratigraphic sequence at Site 18CH-447 was unbroken from the lowest deposit to the surface, with no evidence of post-depositional disturbance; two, that the documentary record, the 1683 county court order, the 1687 Calvert patent boundary description, the 1697 petition, the colonial court entry by Thomas Ware, established continuous colonial acknowledgment of Piscataway presence at the high ground, including at the moment of displacement; and three, that the oral history affidavit, taken together with the artifact chain, constituted independent corroboration of the stratigraphic findings, in that the objects named in the oral chain corresponded to the objects recovered in stratigraphic sequence, in the correct order, at the correct depths.

He read finding three again.

The objects named in the oral chain corresponded to the objects recovered in stratigraphic sequence, in the correct order, at the correct depths.

He thought about what that meant. Nora Moyaone, eighty-two years old, on Billingsley Road, had been carrying a list of objects in a list of names for sixty-two years. She had received the list from Ida, who had received it from her grandmother, who had received it from someone before that, the chain running back through Tessa and Matta and Saya to a woman whose name Nora would not write in the affidavit but whose existence the pendant confirmed. And the list was correct. Not approximately correct. Not suggestively correct. Correct in the way a measurement is correct: the glass bead in stratum one, the pipe bowl in stratum three, the gorget in stratum four, the pendant in stratum seven, each one where the chain said it would be, at the depth the ground confirmed.

He had spent eleven years in Section 106 review. He had read hundreds of field assessments. He had read affidavits and petitions and chain-of-custody forms and NAGPRA consultation letters and tribal historic preservation reports and Section 106 finding letters and responses and counter-responses. He had read careful, qualified professional language his entire career.

He had never read anything like this.

Not because it was unusual to have oral tradition corroborate stratigraphic evidence. That happened. It was not common, but it happened. What was unusual, what he had been sitting with for the better part of an hour without being able to name, was the precision. The chain had not said *there are old things in the ground.* The chain had said: *here is the glass bead, here is the pipe bowl, here is the gorget, here is the pendant.* In order. At depth. Across two thousand years of continuous deposit.

Someone had been paying attention for two thousand years.

He closed the binder. He did not close it because he was done. He closed it because he needed to stop looking at it for a moment.

The office was quiet. The HVAC had been off for several minutes. Through the wall he could hear, faintly, the sound of someone in the next office moving a chair. Outside the window the light had changed. The flat November white of mid-afternoon had shifted toward something grayer, the sky pressing lower over the parking structure across the street. He had not noticed the time passing. His coffee cup was empty and cold. His assistant had knocked twice.

He thought about the 1683 instrument in the drawer.

He had told the Governor's Legal Counsel that the designation packet omitted material evidence of prior Indigenous occupation. That was true, and it was enough, and it was what the record would show. What the record would not show was that he had come across the 1683 instrument six weeks before Tayak's report arrived, while looking for something else, and had kept it in a drawer. What the record would not show was that he had read his great-grandfather's name in Liber 27, folio 314, at the end of a chain of instruments that began with a county court order for forty shillings. What the record would not show was Elena, and the four years she had spent building the legal framework for the Piscataway federal recognition petition, and the call on a Sunday evening when she had said *I'm giving you the chance to be honest* with the precision she brought to everything.

None of that was in the record. The record showed: recommendation not to proceed. The reasons were in the file, or they were in the drawer, or they were in him, in the part of him that had grown up two miles from Mattawoman Creek and had never once asked why the high ground was called the high ground.

He opened the binder again.

He turned to the summary, the last page of the conclusion, and he read the final paragraph.

Tayak had written: *The site known as 18CH-447 is not a colonial landmark. It is a Piscataway burial ground. The colonial record acknowledges this. The ground confirms it. The oral tradition names it. These are not three separate arguments. They are one argument, made three times, in three different languages, across three hundred and twenty-seven years. The designation as currently written would record one of those languages and erase the other two. This report recommends that the designation not proceed as written.*

He read it twice.

Then he sat back in his chair and looked at the ceiling, at the acoustic tiles and the recessed fluorescent panels casting their flat, consistent light over everything in the room, the binder, the photocopies, the empty cup, the phone face-up on the desk, the drawer he had left open.

*Three different languages.* Across three hundred and twenty-seven years.

He thought about Nora Moyaone on Billingsley Road, carrying sixty-two years of names. He thought about the handprint, small and pressed flat, the ink darker at the heel. He thought about his daughter, who had spent four years building something she believed in, and who had called him not to ask for help but to give him the chance to be honest.

He thought about the pendant in the conservation suite, catalog number 18CH-447-051, copper, a hundred and eighty-seven centimeters down, in the dark for two thousand years.

He reached over and picked up the photocopied deeds, Liber 4, Liber 7, Liber 27, tapped their edges square against the desk, and set them on top of the closed binder. Not inside the drawer. On top of the binder. Where he would see them in the morning.

He was not done with them. He did not yet know what he would do with them. But he was not going to put them back in the drawer and close it again and call that enough.

Outside, the parking structure's lights had come on, orange-white against the darkening sky, and the first commuters were pulling out of the lower levels, their headlights sweeping the concrete pillars in slow arcs. Slade watched them for a moment, the ordinary, unhurried movement of people going home, and thought about Elena, who would be in her office now, or driving, or reading a file on her phone the way she always did at red lights, and who did not know what he had just done, and who would find out, and who would have a question when she did.

Not whether he had done the right thing. She would know the answer to that.

The question she would ask, the one he did not yet have an answer for, was what he was prepared to say out loud, and to whom, and when.

He left the binder and the deeds on the desk and turned off the lamp and walked out of the office into the corridor, where the radiator was still ticking and the overhead lights buzzed faintly and his assistant had already gone.
