The notification came at 6:47 in the morning, while Kimi was still in bed.

She'd slept with her phone face-down on the pillow beside her — a bad habit she'd never managed to break — and when the buzz came she grabbed it without opening her eyes, the way you answer a call from someone you've been waiting on. The screen read *Washington Post* and below that a headline she had to blink twice to parse:

**PISCATAWAY TRIBE SAYS MARYLAND BURIAL MOUND HOLDS PROOF OF FOUNDING BLOODLINE — ROUTE 301 EXPANSION IN QUESTION**

She sat up.

The apartment was the kind of quiet that Silver Spring gets at 6:47 a.m. on a Thursday — distant traffic on Colesville Road, a neighbor's shower running through the wall, the refrigerator cycling on in the kitchen. Kimi pulled her knees up and opened the article.

Dana Osei had the byline. She'd known it would be Dana; Dana was the only Post reporter who'd been following the Piscataway recognition case long enough to have Marcus Redcloud's email address. The piece was long — 1,200 words at least, maybe more — and the first four paragraphs were solid. Kimi's video. The Route 301 connector. The Phase I permit. The thirty-eight-day window before Garrett Slade's bulldozers hit the site. Dana had the timeline right and the stakes clear, and for about forty seconds Kimi felt something close to relief.

Then she hit paragraph five.

*An anonymous tribal source, speaking on condition of anonymity because of the sensitivity of ongoing negotiations, said the excavation has already yielded evidence pointing to what the source described as "proof of a direct bloodline connection to the Piscataway founding families" — a claim that, if substantiated, could significantly complicate the state's plans for the corridor.*

Kimi read it again.

She read it a third time, moving her lips slightly, the way she used to do in school when she couldn't make a sentence mean what it seemed to mean.

*Proof of a direct bloodline connection to the Piscataway founding families.*

The phone felt heavier than it should. She set it face-up on the comforter and stared at the water stain on her ceiling — the one shaped roughly like Delaware — and tried to think clearly.

She had not said that. She had not said anything close to that. In her conversation with Dana two nights ago, she had said *significant archaeological find*, she had said *evidence of continuous habitation*, she had said *the kind of discovery that changes the legal picture* — but she had not said bloodline. She had not said founding families. She had not said proof, because she knew — because Tayak had told her, firmly, more than once — that you did not use the word proof in an archaeological context until you had peer-reviewed results and a council resolution and a lawyer standing next to you when you opened your mouth.

She picked the phone back up and scrolled. The comment section was already running. Forty-three comments in forty minutes, which meant the push notification had gone wide. She didn't read the comments. She'd learned that much.

What she did instead was open her texts with Marcus Redcloud.

He had messaged her at 6:31, which meant he'd been awake when the story dropped, which meant someone had tipped him.

*Have you seen the Post piece. Call me before you call anyone else.*

Which meant Tayak was lying in a tent in Charles County right now thinking about it, and not sleeping, and not calling Kimi back.

It was not Marcus Redcloud. It was a push notification from the Post app: *Related: Maryland state officials launch inquiry into Native burial site claims.* The article had been up for four hours. The inquiry had already been picked up. The regional TV stations would have it by morning.

She turned the screen face-down.

The question that sat in the room with her — the one she could not answer and could not stop asking — was not whether Voss had called Dana. She was nearly certain he had. The question was why. What he wanted that required the story to run now, in this form, before the excavation had produced a peer-reviewed result, before the tribe had passed a resolution, before Tayak had established anything scientifically that could support the phrase *founding families* in a court or a hearing room or anywhere that mattered.

What did you gain by forcing the story early?

You gained standing. You gained the right to insert yourself as the responsible institutional voice, the one who could manage the claim, moderate the community, translate the science for the press. You became necessary. You became the person Slade's office would call, because you were already on the letter.

You gained Tayak's excavation.

She sat up in the dark. The sodium light from the parking lot caught the edge of the laptop on the desk, the closed screen, the draft she had not saved.

She had eighty thousand followers and a viral video and a phone full of messages she had not answered, and none of it was leverage for what she was thinking about now. What she was thinking about now required evidence, not reach. It required Tayak to be willing to name it, and Tayak was not ready to name it, and Marcus Redcloud was not replying to her text, and the certified letter was already in the mail.

She picked up the phone and looked at Marcus Redcloud's name again. The message still sat there, read, unanswered.

*I think I know who talked to Dana.*

She thought about adding a second line. She thought about typing the name. She thought about what it would mean to put that name in a text message, in writing, on a phone that was not encrypted, to a council chair who was already navigating Slade's inquiry and Voss's involvement and a formal letter with numbered questions.

She did not type the name.

She set the phone down and looked at the dark window and thought: someone close to the excavation had wanted this story to run. Had needed it to run now, in this shape, with this phrase. And that person was still out there, and still had Slade's office on the line, and still had the cc line on the letter, and had not yet done whatever he had called Dana to set in motion.

The first move had already happened. She had not seen it coming.

She did not know what the second move would look like, or when it would come, or whether Tayak — alone in a field in Charles County with a locked kit and a thirty-eight-day clock — would see it in time.
