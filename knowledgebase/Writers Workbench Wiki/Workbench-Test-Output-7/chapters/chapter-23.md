The notification came at 6:47 in the morning, while Kimi was still in bed.

She had slept with her phone face-down on the pillow beside her, a bad habit she had never managed to break, and when the buzz came she grabbed it without opening her eyes. The screen read *Washington Post* and below that a headline she had to blink twice to parse:

**PISCATAWAY TRIBE SAYS MARYLAND BURIAL MOUND HOLDS PROOF OF FOUNDING BLOODLINE, ROUTE 301 EXPANSION IN QUESTION**

She sat up.

The apartment was the kind of quiet Silver Spring holds at 6:47 on a Thursday morning. Distant traffic on Colesville Road. A neighbor's shower running through the wall. The refrigerator cycling on in the kitchen. Through the window the air carried the faint iron smell of wet pavement, the overnight rain still lifting off the asphalt of Georgia Avenue. Kimi pulled her knees up and opened the article.

Dana Osei had the byline. She had known it would be Dana. Dana was the only Post reporter who had been following the Piscataway recognition case long enough to have Marcus Redcloud's email address. The piece ran at least 1,200 words, and the first four paragraphs were solid. Kimi's video. The Route 301 connector. The Phase I permit. The thirty-eight-day window before Garrett Slade's bulldozers hit the site. Dana had the timeline right and the stakes clear, and for about forty seconds Kimi felt something close to relief.

Then she hit paragraph five.

*An anonymous tribal source, speaking on condition of anonymity because of the sensitivity of ongoing negotiations, said the excavation has already yielded evidence pointing to what the source described as "proof of a direct bloodline connection to the Piscataway founding families", a claim that, if substantiated, could significantly complicate the state's plans for the corridor.*

Kimi read it again.

She read it a third time, moving her lips slightly, the way she used to do in school when she could not make a sentence mean what it seemed to mean.

*Proof of a direct bloodline connection to the Piscataway founding families.*

The phone felt heavier than it should. She set it face-up on the comforter and stared at the water stain on her ceiling, the one shaped roughly like Delaware, and tried to think clearly.

She had not said that. She had not said anything close to that. In her conversation with Dana two nights ago, she had said *significant archaeological find*, she had said *evidence of continuous habitation*, she had said *the kind of discovery that changes the legal picture.* She had not said bloodline. She had not said founding families. She had not said proof, because she knew, because Tayak had told her firmly and more than once, that you did not use the word proof in an archaeological context until you had peer-reviewed results and a council resolution and a lawyer standing next to you when you opened your mouth.

She picked the phone back up and scrolled. The comment section was already running. Forty-three comments in forty minutes, which meant the push notification had gone wide. She did not read the comments. She had learned that much.

What she did instead was open her texts with Marcus Redcloud.

He had messaged her at 6:31, which meant he had been awake when the story dropped, which meant someone had tipped him.

*Have you seen the Post piece. Call me before you call anyone else.*

Two minutes after that, a forwarded email. Dana Osei's original inquiry to the tribal office, sent at 6:31 a.m. the previous day, with no message from Marcus attached. Just the forward. The absence of any comment was its own kind of comment.

Kimi got out of bed. She went to the kitchen and filled a glass of water from the tap and drank half of it standing at the sink, looking at the strip of morning sky visible between the building across the alley and the rusted edge of her own fire escape. The sky was a pale, washed-out grey. The day had not decided yet what it wanted to be.

*Proof of a direct bloodline connection.*

She thought about who else Dana might have called. The council members, Tayak's graduate students, the Nanticoke consultant Tayak had mentioned. She thought about whether any of them might have said something careless, something that Dana had sharpened into that sentence. She thought about whether Dana had done the sharpening herself, taking a hedge and pulling it taut until it sounded like a claim.

She thought about Tayak, asleep in a tent in Charles County, and felt the water sit cold and heavy in her stomach.

Tayak would see the article within the hour. Tayak would read paragraph five. Tayak would read *direct bloodline connection* and *founding families* and *proof*, and Tayak would know, because Tayak always knew, that those words had not come from the field. Those words had come from someone talking to a reporter.

The only person Kimi knew had been talking to Dana Osei was herself.

She set the glass down on the counter harder than she meant to. The sound rang through the kitchen. Then the apartment went quiet again, just the refrigerator and the traffic and the shower next door finally shutting off.

Her phone buzzed. Not Marcus Redcloud. Not Dana.

The screen read *Tayak*.

Kimi looked at it for three full seconds, the phone vibrating against her palm, the name lit up in the grey morning light coming through the kitchen window. Then she answered.

She said, "Hey."

Tayak said, "Have you read it."

Not a question. Kimi pressed her back against the kitchen counter and looked at the floor. The linoleum had a crack running from the base of the refrigerator toward the door, and she had been meaning to put a rug over it for eight months.

"Yes," she said. "I read it."

"Paragraph five."

"Yes."

Silence. Not the silence of a bad connection. She could hear Tayak breathing. It was the silence of someone who had decided not to say the first thing that came to mind.

"Did you say that to her," Tayak said. "Direct bloodline connection to the founding families. Did you say those words."

"No." Kimi pushed off the counter and walked to the window. The alley below was empty except for a recycling bin someone had left in the center of the pavement. "I didn't say that. I didn't say anything close to that."

"Okay."

"Tayak, "

"Did you say proof. Did you use that word."

"No. I know not to use that word. You've told me, "

"I know what I've told you."

Kimi stopped. The refrigerator hummed. Somewhere in the building above her a door closed, and she heard footsteps cross a floor and then nothing.

"I believe you," Tayak said.

The way she said it was worse than if she hadn't. Flat and careful and already somewhere past the question.

"Then, "

"The damage is the same either way."

Kimi opened her mouth and closed it. She turned from the window and leaned against the wall beside it, the paint cool through the back of her sleep shirt. On the counter her glass of water sat with a ring of condensation spreading slowly underneath it.

"I gave her background," Kimi said. "That's all it was. She already knew about the excavation. She had the permit number, she had looked up the Phase I filing. I just gave her context. I talked about the oral tradition. I talked about what the community has been saying for generations about this land. I said the find was significant, I said it changed the legal picture, "

"What did you say about the oral tradition."

Kimi pressed her hand flat against the wall. "I said that the community has maintained continuous knowledge of the site. That the stories go back, that there is a chain of knowledge connecting living people to the ancestors buried there. I said that is not nothing, legally. That it matters."

A pause. She could hear something in the background on Tayak's end. Wind, maybe, or the sound of a tent flap. Tayak was outside. Tayak was standing in a field in Charles County at seven in the morning having this conversation.

"And you said this to a Washington Post reporter," Tayak said.

"She has covered the recognition case for three years. She is sympathetic, "

"She is a reporter."

"She is not the enemy."

"Kimi." Still no heat in it. That was the unbearable thing. There was no heat. "She took what you said and she made it into proof of a direct bloodline connection to the founding families. That sentence is in print right now. It has been in print for, " a pause, and Kimi imagined Tayak checking her phone, "forty-seven minutes. Slade's office will have seen it. "I know." Kimi's voice came out smaller than she wanted. "I know that."

"I have not established a bloodline connection. I have not established anything scientifically. I have a cobalt glass bead and a colonial-era petition and a ceramic sherd and a stratigraphy profile that Wren drew. That is what I have. I do not have proof of anything that Dana Osei's source described."

"I didn't say you did. I didn't say any of that."

"Someone did."

"It wasn't me."

Silence again. Four seconds. Kimi counted them without meaning to, watching the ring of water under her glass spread another millimeter across the laminate.

"I believe you," Tayak said again. Then: "It doesn't matter."

Kimi pushed off the wall. "How does it not matter? If I didn't say it, "

"Because the article exists. Because that sentence exists. Because in an hour, or two hours, or three, someone at the Trust is going to send a formal inquiry citing that paragraph, and the Trust is going to ask me to account for a claim I never made, and I am going to have to spend time I do not have explaining why an anonymous tribal source said something to the Washington Post that I cannot substantiate and did not authorize." A breath. "That is what matters. Not who said it."

Kimi pressed her fingers against her eyes. The kitchen light felt too bright. She had not eaten anything and her stomach knew it.

"I was trying to help," she said. She heard how it sounded. She said it anyway.

"I know you were."

"The video worked. You said so yourself. The video got people's attention, it put the Route 301 timeline in front of, "

"The video was different."

"How."

"Because the video was true. Everything in it was verifiable. You showed the site, you showed the permit number, you showed the thirty-eight-day window." A pause. "You didn't put words in my mouth."

The sentence landed and stayed there.

Kimi walked back to the window. Below, a man in a grey hoodie was dragging the recycling bin back toward the building's rear entrance, the plastic grinding against the pavement in short, effortful bursts.

"I'm going to fix this," she said.

"Don't."

"I can call Dana. I can ask for a correction, "

"Do not call Dana." For the first time something moved in Tayak's voice. Not anger, but something adjacent to it, something controlled. "A correction draws more attention to the paragraph. A correction becomes its own story. Do not call Dana, do not post anything, do not respond to any comments or DMs or press inquiries. Do you understand me."

"Yes."

"I need you to tell me you understand."

"I understand." Kimi's throat felt tight. "Tayak, I'm sorry. I'm genuinely, "

"I know."

And then the line went quiet. Not dead. She could still hear the wind on Tayak's end, the faint sound of the field. Kimi waited for something else, some closing word, some instruction, some version of *we'll figure it out.*

Tayak said, "I have to go."

And hung up.

Kimi stood in the kitchen with the phone in her hand. The man in the grey hoodie had gotten the bin to the door and was propping it open with his foot, struggling with the latch. She watched him until he got it, until the door swung open and he dragged the bin inside and the alley was empty again.

Her phone buzzed. A push notification from NBC4 Washington with a headline that had already picked up the Post story and added its own: *Piscataway Tribe Claims Burial Mound Proves Ancient Family Bloodline, Complicating Major Highway Project.*

She turned the phone face-down on the counter.

The refrigerator hummed. The traffic on Georgia Avenue was picking up. Through the thin wall she could hear her neighbor's television come on, morning news, the low murmur of anchors, and she stood in the too-bright kitchen, not eating, not calling anyone, not doing the one thing she had promised herself she would never do in this fight, which was make it worse.

Her laptop was still open on the bed. The Post article was still on the screen. She went back to the bedroom and looked at it without touching the keyboard.

Paragraph five.

She read it one more time, and this time she read it not looking for what she had said or hadn't said, but looking for what Dana had needed. What shape the story required. What gap a reporter fills when a source gives her *continuous knowledge* and *chain of connection* and *changes the legal picture* and the story needs a sharper edge.

*Proof of a direct bloodline connection to the Piscataway founding families.*

Dana had not invented it from nothing. Dana had taken the softest thing Kimi had said and pulled it until it held a shape the article needed. Kimi had handed her the clay and Dana had made something with it, and the fact that Kimi hadn't meant to hand it over didn't change what Dana had built.

And in her third question, she had used a specific phrase: *Can you confirm whether the site contains material evidence of a direct bloodline connection to the Piscataway founding families, as suggested by community members close to the excavation?*

*As suggested by community members close to the excavation.*

Kimi read the sentence again.

Dana had arrived at Marcus Redcloud's inbox already holding that phrase. Already knowing what to ask. She had not built it from Kimi's background conversation. She had brought it with her.

Which meant someone had given it to her before Kimi ever picked up the phone.

Kimi sat on the edge of the bed. The Post article was still open in the browser tab behind the email, paragraph five still there, and she had the sudden clear sense of watching two things that had started as separate objects click together into a single mechanism she had not understood she was building.

She thought about who else Dana might have spoken with. Not the council. Marcus Redcloud had clearly been blindsided. Not Tayak, who had been explicit about not talking to press. The graduate students were possible, but Wren was careful and quiet, and Tayak kept the team close.

*Community members close to the excavation.*

Voss.

Elliot Voss, who had filed the permit disclosure at 4:17 in the afternoon the day the document came up. Who had left a voicemail Tayak had listened to twice and not returned. "Look at your email," he said, without greeting.

She was already in it. "The Slade letter."

"You've seen it."

"Just now. Three pages."

The Attorney General.

"Marcus. The AG's office is copied."

"I know."

"His office tracks online metrics in real time. The story was at four thousand shares by eight-fifteen." A pause. "Someone on his staff had this drafted before most people had their coffee."

"Yes."

"No."
