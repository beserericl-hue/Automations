The needle went in, came back, went in again.

Nora had been at the frame since four o'clock. The kitchen was dark except for the gooseneck lamp clamped to the frame's crossbar, its light falling in a tight circle on the last panel, and the small blue glow of the clock on the stove. Five forty-seven. The rest of the house was still.

Her hands hurt. They had hurt since October, the knuckles swelling overnight and stiff until she worked them loose each morning, but she had learned to thread the needle before the stiffness fully settled and to keep moving after that. The ache was manageable when the hands were busy and worse when they were not. She used a size-ten between needle, short and precise, the kind her grandmother had called a thinking needle because it didn't rush you. She had worn through two of them finishing this panel.

The panel showed the mound from above.

She had worked it in cotton: deep summer green for the grass, a darker thread for the shadow on the north face where the slope steepened, a narrow band of raw sienna along the eastern edge where the excavation trench had been. She'd had to order the sienna from a shop in Annapolis because the fabric store in La Plata didn't carry it, and when the bolt arrived she held it to the window for a long time before she cut anything, making sure the color was right. It was. It was the exact color of the soil at that depth, which she knew because Tayak had shown her a photograph.

At the center of the panel, slightly south of the mound's crown, she had appliquéd the figure.

Small. No taller than Nora's thumbnail. A woman, standing, one arm slightly raised, holding something that was more suggestion than detail. A narrow shape, a trowel. You had to know what you were looking at to see it, but if you knew, you saw it immediately. Nora had stitched the figure's outline in dark thread and filled it with a close satin stitch, then gone back over the raised arm with a single strand of darker thread to give it weight. The face was a single knot. She hadn't tried to make it a portrait. It wasn't meant to be a portrait. It was meant to be a fact.

She had been working toward this panel for sixty-two years without knowing what it would show. That was not quite right, but it was close enough. She had known there would be a last panel. She had known it would close the story. She had not known the story would close like this, with a woman standing beside a mound that had been excavated and covered back and was now, as far as the state of Maryland was concerned, protected in perpetuity. That was a legal phrase that meant something and also meant nothing, depending on who was watching. She had not known the figure would be Tayak. She had not known Tayak would exist, when she started.

The first panel had been made by a woman four generations back, a woman whose name Nora had carried for sixty-two years and had not said aloud in all that time except into the ground, the way Ida had taught her. The first panel showed a river and a high bank and a small rise above the waterline, and it was rough work, the stitching uneven in places, the proportions not quite right, but it was unmistakably the same place. Nora had understood that the first time she saw it, standing in Ida's kitchen at nineteen years old, looking at the quilt spread across the table. The quilt was a map and a record and something else, something she didn't have a word for then and still didn't quite have a word for now, except that it was the kind of thing that needed to be finished.

She had made eleven panels herself. The first ten had taken thirty years. The last one had taken thirty-two.

The needle went in.

She was working the final border now, a narrow binding of deep-indigo cotton she'd cut on the bias so it would lie flat around the corners, and she was hand-finishing the back with a ladder stitch, small and even, the thread pulled just tight enough to hold without puckering. Her grandmother had taught her the ladder stitch on a winter afternoon when Nora was eight years old, guiding her hand through the motion: in, across, in, across, until the seam disappeared. *You're not sewing it shut*, her grandmother had said. *You're making it so no one can see the work. The work should be invisible. Only the thing itself should show.*

Nora had thought about that for sixty-two years.

Outside, a car passed on Billingsley Road, headlights sweeping across the kitchen ceiling. The clock changed to five forty-eight. The furnace clicked on and sent a low breath of warm air through the floor vent beside the frame, and Nora shifted her feet away from it automatically. Heat was bad for the cotton. She had learned that the hard way with the third panel, which had dried out and cracked along a seam she'd had to repair twice.

She pulled the last stitch through, knotted it, clipped the thread close with her small scissors.

She sat back and looked at what she had made.

The mound, green and closed. The figure, small and clear, arm raised. The border, dark and even. The whole panel no larger than a pillowcase, but it held sixty-two years of intention, and it held the panel before it, and the panel before that, all the way back to the rough river-and-rise that the first woman had made with uneven stitching and a certainty Nora had spent her whole life trying to understand.

She pressed her palm flat against the finished surface. The cotton was cool and slightly stiff where the thread painting was densest, soft and yielding where she'd left the batting to breathe. It smelled of cotton and the faint mineral trace of the steam iron she'd used last night to set the appliqué, and underneath that, very faintly, the woodsmoke smell that lived in the house and had lived there as long as Nora could remember.

She sat with her hand flat on the panel and did not move.

Then she reached for the phone on the counter. Tayak was coming today, and there was something Nora had been waiting sixty-two years to say out loud, and she was going to need a little more time to be ready.

The knock came at seven twenty-three.

Nora heard the car first: the particular sound of gravel settling under tires, then the engine cut, then a door. She had folded the finished panel face-down over the quilting frame before she put the coffee on. Not because she was hiding it. She wasn't ready to have it looked at yet. There was a difference, though she couldn't have explained it to anyone who didn't already understand.

She went to the door before Tayak could knock again.

Tayak stood on the porch in the grey morning light, wearing a dark canvas jacket with a field badge still clipped to the breast pocket from some meeting or other, her hair pulled back the way she always wore it for work. She was holding a bound report, the kind universities printed on heavy stock with a glossy cover, and she had it open to a bookmarked page, holding it the way you hold something you've been reading in the car and haven't put down yet. The foreword was visible. Nora could see Voss's name at the top of the page without her glasses.

"Come in," Nora said. "Coffee's on."

Tayak came in. She stamped her boots on the mat without being asked, which she had done since she was nine years old and which Nora still noticed, every time. The kitchen smelled of coffee and the faint mineral trace of last night's ironing, and the furnace was running its low morning breath through the floor vent. Nora went back to the counter and poured.

Tayak set the report on the kitchen table. She set it down carefully, with a small release of breath, the way you set down something you've been carrying a long time. She didn't say anything about it. Nora didn't look at it.

"Sit down," Nora said.

Tayak sat. She unclipped the field badge and set it beside the report, a small unconscious gesture, as if she were taking off a coat. Nora brought the two cups and sat across from her.

The report sat between them. Nora could see the cover from where she sat: the site number, the institutional seal, Tayak's name below Voss's, and below that the date and the commission's approval stamp. She had not touched it. She was not going to pick it up and read it here at the table with Tayak watching her. She already knew what it said. She had known, in the way you know things before they are written down, for longer than the report had existed.

Tayak wrapped both hands around her cup. Her knuckles were chapped from fieldwork, the skin dry across the joints the way it got in November. She looked at the table, then at Nora.

"He wrote a foreword," she said.

"I saw."

"He didn't apologize."

"No," Nora said. "Men like that don't." She said it without heat. It was simply a thing she had observed over eighty-two years, like the way heat cracked cotton along a seam if you weren't careful.

Tayak's mouth pressed together briefly. Then she let it go. "It's a good foreword," she said. "That's almost worse."

Nora drank her coffee. Outside, a car passed on Billingsley Road, going south toward La Plata, and the kitchen was quiet again except for the furnace.

She was aware of the quilt on the frame behind her, folded face-down. She was aware that Tayak had not looked at it yet, or had looked and was waiting, the way Tayak waited for things, with a patience that came from knowing how to read ground. Tayak had learned that patience in the field, from Carver and from Marant and from the ground itself, but she had also been born with some portion of it. Nora knew where that portion came from. It was not from Tayak's mother.

She did not let herself think about Tayak's mother. Not now. There was a room in Nora's mind for that, and she kept the door closed on mornings that required her to be present.

"How long did you drive?" Nora asked.

"Left College Park at six." Tayak turned the cup in her hands. "Read the foreword twice in the parking garage before I got on the road."

"And?"

"And I needed to come here." She said it simply, without apology. "I needed to show you the thing before I decided how to feel about it."

Nora looked at her granddaughter across the table: the chapped knuckles, the field badge beside the report, the way she held the cup with both hands the way she had held things since she was small, as if warmth were something to be conserved. Nora had raised her from nine years old. She knew every version of this face.

"You already know how to feel about it," Nora said.

Tayak didn't answer right away. She looked at the report on the table between them. "He wrote that the excavation represents a significant contribution to the understanding of continuous Indigenous presence in the mid-Atlantic region." She paused. "He wrote *significant contribution.*"

"Yes."

"It's my site. My eleven months. My data."

"Yes."

"And he wrote a foreword."

"Yes," Nora said. "That's how it goes. That's been how it goes for three hundred years." She set down her cup. "You know that. You knew that before you drove down here."

Tayak was quiet. Then she said, "I wanted to show you the stamp. The commission's approval stamp. On the cover." She touched the corner of the report without opening it. "I wanted you to see that it's real."

Nora looked at the stamp. She could read it from where she sat: the state seal, the date, the designation number. She had waited for something like this for a long time. Not this exactly. She had not known what form it would take. But something that meant the ground was protected, that the commission had put its name on a document that said so, that there was now a legal phrase attached to the mound that meant, for the moment, that no one could take it.

She did not feel what she had expected to feel. She felt something quieter. Something that had more to do with the folded quilt on the frame behind her than with the stamp on the cover of the report.

"It's real," she said.

The coffee was cooling between them. The furnace clicked off and the house settled into its morning silence, the kind of silence that had lived in these walls as long as Nora had, older than the furniture, older than the photograph on the shelf in the other room that she did not look at when she needed to be steady.

Tayak looked up from the report and her eyes went, finally, to the quilting frame in the corner. Nora watched her see the folded shape of it: the dark indigo binding visible at the edge, the thickness of the cotton batting, the way the frame held it. Tayak looked at it for a moment without speaking.

Neither of them mentioned it.

Nora pushed back from the table.

She did it carefully, the way she did everything now: weight forward on her palms first, then a slow transfer to her feet, the chair scraping on the linoleum. Tayak started to rise. Nora stopped her with a look.

"Stay there. I need the room."

She went to the quilting frame and lifted the quilt free of it with both arms. It came with the slight resistance of something that had been held in one position for a long time, the cotton batting dense and settled. She carried it to the table the way you carry something that matters, not reverently, not with ceremony, but with attention. She set it down across the cleared surface and began to unfold it, pushing the coffee cups and the report to the far edge.

The quilt opened in sections, each fold releasing a new panel into the light.

Tayak was quiet. Her hands came off the cup and went flat on the table, the way they went flat on the ground when she was reading a surface. Nora registered this without comment.

The first panel was in the top left corner. Indigo thread, faded to the color of a winter sky at the horizon: not blue anymore, not quite grey, something between that had no name in English. The stitching was small and even, each stitch placed with a patience that had nothing to do with speed and everything to do with time. It showed a rise in the land. Just that: a gentle rise above a flat ground, with lines suggesting water to the right, and a stand of trees close on all sides. No figure. No object. Only the rise, and the sense that someone had looked at it for a long time before deciding how to put it into cloth.

"How old is this panel?" Tayak said.

"The woman who made it was born around 1860. She started the quilt when she was in her forties, from what I can work out." Nora smoothed the edge of the panel with the flat of her hand. "She knew the mound. She had been there."

Tayak leaned forward. The indigo stitching caught the kitchen light, the overhead fluorescent that Nora had been meaning to replace for two years with something warmer, and had not.

"She knew what it was?"

"She knew what it was." Nora moved her hand to the next panel. "Her daughter added this one."

The second panel was worked in a darker thread: navy and brown, with a strip of cream at the base that might have been the river terrace or might have been snow. The rise was still there, but larger now, more defined, and there were two figures at its base. Small, no faces, just the suggestion of posture: one upright, one bent at the knees.

"Two people at the mound," Tayak said.

"Yes. Her daughter knew a little less than she did, but she knew enough to add the people." Nora unfolded another section. "That's how it went. Each woman added what she had."

The panels continued across the table. Five of them, six, seven, each one slightly different in thread color and stitch style, each one showing the mound from a slightly different angle or season: summer green, autumn brown, the bare lines of winter. Some panels were technically accomplished, the piecing clean and the appliqué tight. Others were rougher, the figures blocky, the perspective uncertain. One panel, Nora thought it was the fifth, made sometime in the 1940s, showed the mound with a road running past it on the western side, a thin brown line of chain-stitched thread that had not been there in the earlier panels.

"Someone knew about the road," Tayak said.

"Someone was paying attention." Nora reached the final fold. "Here."

She turned the last section over and spread it flat.

The final panel was the largest. Nora had given it more space than the others because she had more to say, and because she had learned, in eighty-two years, that the end of a thing needs room. She had worked it in the last three months, the free-motion quilting done in October, the thread painting in November, the binding finished this morning before Tayak arrived. The cotton was a deep green: not indigo, not brown, the green of a mound in late spring when the grass has come back and the soil has settled and you cannot see from the surface what is held below. She had used free-motion stitching to suggest the texture of the grass, the thread laid down in long curved lines that caught the light differently depending on the angle.

The mound was shown from above.

And beside it, small, at the southern edge: a figure. A woman. Standing, not bent, with one arm slightly extended. In the extended hand, a small triangular shape that might have been a trowel.

Tayak looked at it for a long time.

The furnace had not clicked back on. The kitchen was very quiet. Outside, the light on Billingsley Road had the flat quality of a November morning that has decided not to commit to sun or cloud, and the bare sycamores along the road edge were the color of old bone.

"That's me," Tayak said.

"Yes."

"You put me in the quilt."

"I finished the quilt this morning." Nora sat back down across from her. "I've been working toward that panel for three months. I knew what it needed to look like. I just had to wait until there was something to put in your hand."

Tayak looked up. "The commission stamp."

"The commission stamp."

Tayak looked back at the panel. Her jaw worked slightly, the way it did when she was holding something in that she had not yet decided whether to say. Nora waited. She was good at waiting. She had been waiting for sixty-two years, in one form or another, and she had learned that the right kind of waiting was not passive. It was a form of attention, like keeping your hands on the ground.

"Your great-great-grandmother's grandmother made the first panel," Nora said. "I made the last. You're in the middle."

Tayak was still looking at the final panel. "What does that mean?"

"It means the chain is not finished." Nora put her hand on the edge of the quilt, on the binding she had mitered at the corner and hand-stitched on the back this morning, the ladder stitch small and even, the thread pulled snug but not tight. "It's only handed."

Tayak looked at her. "Handed to who?"

"To whoever comes after you." Nora kept her voice even. "That's not your question to answer yet. Your question is what you do with what you've been given."

"I've been given a designation and a foreword I didn't write and eleven months of data that's now someone else's publication."

"You've been given the mound," Nora said. "Protected. Standing. With a figure in it who has a trowel."

Tayak was quiet for a moment. Then she reached out and touched the final panel: not the figure, but the green ground around it, the thread-painted grass. Her fingertip moved across the surface where the quilting was dense and sculpted, the stitching tight enough to feel like texture under the skin.

"You used free-motion quilting for the grass," Tayak said. Not a question.

"I did."

"I can feel where the layers are thicker." She moved her finger. "Here, and here. Like stratigraphy."

Nora had not thought of it that way when she was working. She let Tayak have the observation, because it was true, and because it was Tayak's way of reading a thing: through her hands first, then her eyes, then whatever came after.

"The first panel," Tayak said. She moved her hand back to the top left corner, to the faded indigo rise above the flat ground. "This woman. She knew the name?"

"She knew some of what I know. Not all." Nora folded her hands on the table. "Each generation knew a little less or a little differently. That's what happens when you carry a thing through hard times. You lose some of it. You hold on to what you can." She paused. "What matters is that no one set it down."

Tayak looked at the spread of panels across the kitchen table: seven women's work, laid out in sequence, the thread colors shifting from indigo to navy to brown to cream to the deep spring green of the final panel, the mound present in every one, always the same rise, always the same ground, always the same question underneath it.

"Seven panels," she said.

"Seven strata," Nora said.

Tayak looked at her. Nora met her eyes and held them.

"You knew," Tayak said. "About the strata."

"I knew what Ida knew, and Ida knew what her grandmother knew, and her grandmother knew what the women before her knew." Nora kept her voice steady. "We didn't have your numbers. We didn't have your measurements. But we knew there were layers. We knew each layer was a different time, a different keeping. We knew it went down a long way."

Tayak's hands were flat on the quilt now, on the final panel, on the green ground around the small figure with the trowel. Her chapped knuckles were pale against the fabric.

"How long?" she said.

Nora looked at her granddaughter: the field badge on the table, the report with the commission stamp at the edge of the quilt, the hands that had spent eleven months reading the ground. She thought about the room in her mind with the closed door. She thought about the name she had held for sixty-two years without saying it on the phone. She thought about the first panel with its faded indigo rise and the woman who had made it, who had been born in 1860 and had stood at the mound and known what it was, and she thought about the woman before her, and the woman before her, and the woman at the bottom of it all, the one whose name required a pause before the second syllable, the one who had pressed her hands flat on the earth when the earth was only a small rise above the river terrace with forest close on all sides.

"Long enough," Nora said, "that the name at the beginning has been waiting for you to stand in the right place before I say it."

Tayak's hands did not move.

"Am I standing in the right place?" she said.

Nora looked at the quilt. At the mound in every panel. At the figure in the last one, small and green-grounded, with a trowel in her hand.

"Almost," she said. "Not yet."

Tayak looked up from the quilt.

"Why not yet?"

Nora did not answer immediately. She got up from the table, her knees taking a moment to agree to it, and carried her mug to the counter, where she set it down without refilling it. Outside the kitchen window the light had shifted. The morning was going thin and grey, the way November mornings did in Charles County, the color draining out of the sky from the top down.

She stood with her back to Tayak for a moment. Not hiding. Just finding the words in the right order.

"I waited," she said, "until the excavation was over."

"I know."

"I waited until the mound was closed. Until the report was published."

"I know that too." Tayak's voice was careful. Not angry. But the question was in it. "Why?"

Nora turned around. She leaned against the counter and looked at her granddaughter across the kitchen table, across the quilt, across the seven panels laid out in sequence like a stratigraphic column read from the wrong end: the oldest at the top, the newest in front of her hands.

"Because I didn't know what the right place looked like," she said. "Until you were already standing in it."

Tayak waited.

"When you were nine," Nora said, "you were not standing in the right place. You were standing in a kitchen in this house wondering when your mother was coming back." She said it plainly, the way you say a thing that has been true for so long it has stopped being painful and started being just true. "When you were twenty-two and finishing your degree, you were not standing in the right place. You were standing in a classroom learning to read the ground. That was necessary. But it wasn't the place."

"And when I was in the trench," Tayak said.

"You were close." Nora folded her arms across her chest, not against Tayak but against the cold that lived in the kitchen this time of year, the cold that came up through the old linoleum and settled in her hips. "When you called me from the site, when you'd found the pendant, I thought, maybe now. But the mound was still open. The state was still moving. Voss was still, " She stopped. "There were still things that could go wrong."

"There were things that went wrong."

"Yes." Nora looked at her steadily. "And you handled them."

Tayak's hands were still on the final panel. She had not moved them since she had pressed her fingertips into the dense-quilted grass and said *like stratigraphy*, and Nora had let her have that. Now Tayak's palms were flat on the green ground around the small figure, the same posture the first woman had used two thousand years ago on the actual earth.

Nora did not point that out. Some things you let a person arrive at themselves.

"I need to ask you something," Tayak said.

"Ask."

"Did you not show me the quilt before, " She paused. She was choosing the words the way she chose which layer to trowel. "Was it because of her? Because of my mother?"

The kitchen was very quiet. The refrigerator made its low hum. Outside, a car passed on Billingsley Road, slowed at the curve, and was gone.

Nora did not open the room with the closed door. She had not opened it in thirty-five years and she was not going to open it now, not in front of Tayak, not without warning, not without time enough to close it again properly afterward.

But she did not lie.

"Partly," she said.

Tayak's jaw moved. She looked down at the quilt.

"She saw it," Nora said. "Once. When it was early, when I had only four panels finished. She came by on a Sunday and I had it out on the table, and she looked at it the way you look at something you don't know what to do with." Nora kept her voice even. The evenness cost her something, but she kept it. "She didn't ask me about it. I didn't explain it. And then she, "

She stopped.

Tayak did not look up. She was looking at the quilt. At the fourth panel, the brown-threaded one, the indigo of the earlier panels gone, the mound the same but the ground around it different, harder, the thread painting tight and close like a field in dry weather.

"She left in spring," Tayak said quietly. "1989."

"April."

"I know it was April."

They were both quiet. The refrigerator hummed. Nora's hands were cold where she had them crossed on her chest and she pressed them harder against her sleeves.

"I thought," Nora said, "that if I showed you the quilt before you were ready, before you had the ground under you, before you had the work and the evidence and the standing, I would be giving you a weight you couldn't carry yet. I thought it might break something." She paused. "I thought it might break you the way it, "

She stopped again.

Tayak looked up. Her eyes were dry. She was not going to cry and Nora was not going to cry and they both knew this about each other.

"The way it broke her?" Tayak said.

Nora did not answer. She did not have to. The silence held the answer and they both sat inside it for a moment, the grief on one side and the answer on the other, and the quilt between them on the table with its seven panels and its thread-painted grass and its small figure standing beside the closed green mound.

"She wasn't broken," Tayak said finally. Not defending. Just correcting.

"No," Nora agreed. "She wasn't." She looked at the quilt. "But she wasn't standing in the right place either. And I didn't know how to tell the difference, then, between someone who needed the weight and someone who needed more time."

"How do you know the difference now?"

Nora looked at her granddaughter's hands on the final panel. At the field badge on the table. At the copy of the published report with the commission stamp, which Tayak had brought and set down without comment, as if it were a thing she was still deciding what to do with.

"Because you came back," she said. "You came back to the ground. You stayed in the trench when it was hard and you stayed at the table when it was harder and you didn't set it down." She unfolded her arms and put her hands flat on the table, on the border of the quilt, on the binding she had hand-stitched this morning. "She couldn't stay. That's not a judgment. Some people can't stay. But you can. You have. That's the difference."

Tayak looked at the quilt for a long time.

"She didn't know," Tayak said. "Did she. About the chain. About the name. About any of it."

"I hadn't told her yet." Nora's voice was very quiet. "I was waiting for the right place."

"And you ran out of time."

"Yes."

The word sat between them, small and exact, like a measurement.

Tayak's thumb moved across the binding at the corner, the mitered corner, the ladder stitch Nora had pulled snug but not tight this morning in the early light. Her thumb found the seam where the two fabric strips met at the angle, the join almost invisible under the stitching.

"You didn't run out of time with me," she said.

"No." Nora's chest was tight. She kept her hands flat and her voice level. "I didn't."

Tayak looked at her. Then she looked back at the quilt, at the final panel, at the small figure with the trowel standing beside the closed green mound.

"Tell me the name," she said. "Tell me now."

Nora looked at the panel. At the mound she had quilted from the drone photograph Tayak had sent her in September, the one taken after the site was closed and the turf relaid and the datum stakes removed, the mound just a green rise above the river terrace with forest close on three sides, the way it had looked for two thousand years before anyone with a permit had come near it.

She had quilted the figure last. She had used a thread that was almost the same green as the ground, so the figure was there if you looked but not obvious if you didn't. She had given her a trowel because Tayak had one. She had made her small because the mound was large. She had made her standing because Tayak was standing.

"Not here," Nora said. "Not at this table."

Tayak's eyes came up.

"You said almost," Tayak said. "You said *not yet*. What's the rest of it?"

Nora folded her hands on the binding. "You need to be at the mound," she said. "Standing on the ground. With your hands on the earth the way she put her hands on the earth." She held Tayak's gaze. "I'll come with you. We'll go in the spring, when the frost is out of the ground. You'll press your hands flat and I'll say the name into the ground the way it's meant to be said, the way Ida said it to me, the way it's been said since the beginning." She paused. "That's the right place. Not a kitchen table."

Tayak was quiet for a moment.

"Spring," she said.

"Spring."

Tayak looked down at the quilt. At the figure in the final panel, small and green-grounded, almost invisible if you didn't know to look. Then she lifted her hands from the fabric and placed them in her lap, and Nora watched her do it, watched her let go of the panel, let go of the ground, and understood that Tayak was already counting the months.

The kitchen had gone quiet. Outside, a car moved slowly down Billingsley Road, its tires loud on the cold asphalt, and then the sound faded and there was only the refrigerator's hum and the light coming flat through the window above the sink.

Nora stood.

She did not say anything. She went to the ironing board in the corner, still set up from this morning, the iron cold now, the padded cover printed with faded blue diamonds, and she folded the quilt the way she always folded it: first in half lengthwise, the panels facing inward to protect the thread-painted surfaces, then in thirds, then once more, until it was a rectangle she could carry with both arms. The cotton batting gave it a weight that felt right. Not heavy. Substantial. The kind of weight that meant something was inside.

She carried it back to the table and set it in front of Tayak.

"Take it," she said.

Tayak looked at it. She did not reach for it immediately. Her eyes moved over the folded edge, the binding strip mitered at the visible corner, the small pucker where Nora had pulled the ladder stitch snug at four in the morning in the bad light.

"Grandmother."

"Take it."

Tayak lifted it with both hands. She held it against her chest the way you hold something you are not sure you deserve, and Nora watched her stand up from the table and look around the kitchen as if she had forgotten where the door was.

"Your great-great-grandmother's grandmother made the first panel," Nora said. "I made the last. You're in the middle."

Tayak looked at her over the folded quilt.

"That's a long middle," she said.

"Yes," Nora said. "It is."

Tayak carried it to the door. She had to shift it to one arm to work the handle, and Nora watched her do it without offering to help, because Tayak did not need help and they both knew it. The door opened on cold air and the smell of dead leaves and wet clay from the fields, and Tayak went out.

Nora stayed at the table. She heard the car door open, the soft thud of the quilt being laid on the back seat, the door closing again. She heard Tayak's footsteps on the porch steps, lighter now without the weight. She heard the driver's door.

She went to the window above the sink.

Tayak's car, the old Subaru with the cracked rear bumper and the university parking decal fading at the corner, pulled out from the gravel strip beside the road and turned south on Billingsley. Nora watched it go. The brake lights came on once at the bend where the road dipped toward the creek bottom, red in the grey afternoon, and then the car was gone behind the tree line.

The refrigerator hummed.

Nora stood at the window for a moment with her hands on the edge of the sink, the porcelain cold under her palms, and looked at the road where the car had been. The fields on the far side were brown and flat, picked clean, and above them the sky was the color of old linen. She did not cry. She had not cried since the morning Kimi called at three-forty with the designation letter, and she did not intend to start now.

She had sixty-two years of practice.

She turned from the window and began to clear the cups., -

The office was on the third floor of the anthropology building, at the end of a hallway that smelled of carpet adhesive and the particular institutional damp of a building that had been renovated twice without ever being fixed. The door had a printed nameplate: *Dr. T. Moyaone, Archaeology / Indigenous Studies*. Beside it, on the corkboard, a posted office-hours schedule annotated in blue pen where the Tuesday slot had been moved to Thursday.

Students came to the door and stopped.

Not because of the nameplate. Not because of the schedule. Because of what was on the wall directly opposite the door, hung on a wooden rod threaded through a sleeve Tayak had sewn along the back herself, the rod resting in two small brackets she had put up on a Saturday morning in October with a level and a drill borrowed from the department office.

The quilt.

Seven panels, each one a layer. The first panel: a rise in the land above a flat ground, the stitching faded indigo, the proportions not quite right, the certainty in it unmistakable. The middle panels: other figures, other objects, a glass bead catching light, a pipe bowl, a gorget, a pendant, each one quilted into its stratum with thread so fine the stitching looked like it had grown there. And the last panel: the mound from above, the way a drone sees it, green and closed and round above the river terrace, the forest close on three sides, and beside it a small figure standing in the thread-painted grass.

The figure had a trowel.

She was almost invisible if you didn't know to look. The thread Nora had used was nearly the same green as the ground around her, and she stood without drama, without gesture, just standing. Present. The way you stand when the work is done and the ground is closed and you are still there.

Students came through the door and saw her and stopped.

Some of them asked about it right away, before they had even sat down, before they had pulled out their laptops or their reusable water bottles or their questions about the midterm. *What is that?* Or just: *That quilt.* And Tayak would look up from her desk and say: *That's my grandmother's work. Seven generations. Two thousand years. Sit down and I'll tell you.*

Some of them came back later, not for office hours, not for grades, just to stand in the doorway and look at it again. The small figure in the last panel, almost hidden in the green. The trowel in her hand.

The first thing they saw when they walked in.

The last thing, sometimes, they forgot.
