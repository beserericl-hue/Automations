The Research Integrity Office was on the fourth floor of Hornbake, past a row of faculty mailboxes nobody checked anymore and a bulletin board still pinned with a flyer for a spring colloquium that had already happened. Tayak found the right door at 9:51 and sat down in one of the two chairs outside it. Stackable polypropylene, the kind that left marks on the backs of your thighs. She put her manila folder on her lap.

She had been in this building twice before. Once to dispute a payroll error. Once to sign forms for the excavation permit. Both times she had left feeling the building had processed her and moved on without noticing she had been there.

The folder held eleven pages. The NSF submission record, timestamped April 14, with Voss listed as sole PI. Three pages of lifted language set side by side with her own preliminary assessment: her words, his proposal, no attribution. Farida's email, printed at seven that morning before Farida could think better of having sent it, describing the proposal as reading like someone had stripped the attribution layer off a document. And the permit clause, Section 4-C, which Voss had signed, granting the descendant community prior access to the daily log. That clause made his use of her unpublished coordinates and site data without her knowledge something more than academic discourtesy.

Eleven pages. She had numbered them by hand.

Through the door she could hear his voice.

Not the words. The register. That careful, regretful register he used when he was performing deliberation: the voice of a man who had thought deeply about something and arrived, with great reluctance, at a position that happened to benefit him. She had heard it in his office three weeks ago when he suggested co-authorship on the preliminary findings. She had heard it on the phone when Priya forwarded the NSF listing and Tayak called him directly, before filing anything, giving him the chance he had not deserved. *I understand your concern, Tayak. Let me look into how this happened.*

How this happened. As though the proposal had assembled itself.

She set the folder flat on her knees and pressed her palms down on it. The hallway smelled of printer toner and the particular kind of carpet that had been cleaned too many times with something cheap. The HVAC moved air from left to right. Somewhere down the hall a photocopier worked through a long job, one page at a time.

She had told herself, driving up Route 301 that morning, that she would not need anything from this room except the record. The hearing would create a record. Whatever they decided, there would be a record of what she had filed, what she had documented, what she had said.

She closed the folder.

Dr. Cecile Marant had told her, in her second year at Howard, that the most dangerous moment in any investigation was the moment before you had the answer. Not because you might be wrong. Because you might be right and it might not matter. *The ground tells you the truth*, Cecile had said. *Institutions tell you what they've decided.* She had been talking about site reports, about the gap between what the stratigraphy showed and what the published interpretation said. *Political sensitivity.*

She had used that phrase herself, in the preliminary field assessment, in the permit application, in every careful document she had written over the past four months. She had used it as a description of the site's significance. In Halverson's mouth it was something else. It was a door being held open just long enough for her to see what was on the other side, and then very gently closed.

"The state compliance review is ongoing," he continued. "The Trust has an active file. There's been media coverage. There may be Hill interest: I don't know, but it's not impossible. She said it aloud, once, quietly, in the cab of the truck. It sounded smaller out loud than it had in the room. That was the thing about language like that. She walked to the trench.

She crouched at the edge and looked at it for a moment the way she always looked at a profile before she touched it. Reading it. Letting it read her back.

She picked up a trowel.

The soil in the unit was cool and faintly damp, the smell of it the mineral-organic smell of undisturbed subsoil, the smell that meant nothing had been here since the last person who put something here. She worked the blade at the angle the layer required, not the angle she preferred, and the soil came away in the thin, controlled increments the work demanded. She did not think about Halverson. She did not think about the letter that would go in her personnel file within five business days, the letter that would note the complaint, the corrective action, the panel's finding, the panel's recommendation that all parties focus on the productive continuation of the excavation work. The HVAC in Halverson's room had made a soft, continuous whir she had heard under every word spoken in that room: a sound designed to be inaudible, the sound of a building maintaining itself regardless of what happened inside it. Here there was wind in the oaks and the creek moving somewhere below the site and the small sound of the trowel in the earth.

She worked until the light was fully gone and the headlamp was throwing shadows she couldn't read clearly enough to trust. Then she worked a few minutes more.

When she finally set the trowel down and sat back on her heels, the night had settled completely around the site, and the high ground was dark except for her headlamp and the faint glow of a security light at the far end of the field road. She pulled out her field notebook, the red-covered one, the one she had brought into Halverson's room and set on the table in front of her like a statement of intent, and wrote the day's end notes in the margin of the morning's log. She wrote it in the same hand she always used, the careful, loop-free hand that Carver had drilled into her: *the notebook is the site's memory, not yours.*

She closed the notebook.

She did not write: *the hearing was today.* She did not write: *Voss will amend his proposal.* She did not write: *I was told to consider the optics.* None of that belonged here. The notebook was for what the ground held, not for what the institution had done with the day.

She covered the unit, weighted the tarp at the corners, and walked back to the truck. The clay on her boots was fresh now, the good dark red-brown of the subsoil, and it would dry on the mat overnight and she would scrape it off in the morning and it would be the only record that she had been here at all.

She sat in the driver's seat. She did not start the engine immediately.

Tomorrow Wren would be here. Tomorrow there was another centimeter to clear, and then another below that, and the stratigraphy did not care what had happened in a carpeted room forty miles north. The ground kept its own record. It had been keeping it for two thousand years before Halverson's building was built, and it would keep it after the building was gone.

She started the truck.

The backup camera blinked on, showing the dark field road behind her, the pale edge of the tarp, the oaks at the margin of the site standing in the headlights for a moment before she turned and they fell away into the dark.

She drove north. The site disappeared in the rearview mirror. She already knew what she was coming back to find.
