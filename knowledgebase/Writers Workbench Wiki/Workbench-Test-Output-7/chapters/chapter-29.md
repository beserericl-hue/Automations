The cold came in under the door before he knocked.

Tessa felt it first — a draft that shouldn't have been there, moving along the floor where the boards met the sill, pushing against the hem of her wool petticoat where she stood at the hearth. She had been awake since before the sky changed, coaxing the fire back from its nighttime coals, and she knew the feel of this house the way she knew her own breathing. The draft was wrong. Someone was standing close on the other side of the door, blocking the wind, which meant the wind could find the gap.

She did not move toward it. She waited.

The knock came soft. Three times, then a pause, then once more. The old signal from when they were children and he wanted her to let him in through the back without waking their grandfather.

She crossed the room and lifted the latch.

He stood in the grey before-light, hat in his hand, turning the brim in a slow circle the way he did when he was ashamed of something. He wore a coat she had not seen before — dark wool, well-cut, with brass buttons that caught what little light there was. English buttons. The coat of a man who had decided something.

"Tessa," he said.

She did not step back to let him in.

His face was her face in the way that all siblings share a face — the same wide-set eyes, the same line of the jaw — but his was arranged differently now, held in a careful stillness she didn't recognize from their childhood. He had learned to make his face say less. She didn't know when that had happened.

"I came before light," he said. "So no one would see."

"And yet you came."

The brim of his hat kept turning. Behind him, the yard was dark and still. The oak at the fence line was a black shape against a sky that had begun, barely, to grey at its eastern edge. A crow called once from somewhere in the tree line and went quiet.

"I am going to tell Hale," he said. "I want you to know that before I do it. I am not doing it behind your back."

She felt it land in her chest the way a stone lands in still water — not a splash but a weight, dropping straight down.

He looked up, and his eyes were desperate in the way she remembered from when he was eleven and had broken their grandfather's clay pipe and needed her to believe it wasn't his fault. "Tessa. You know what they're saying. There's a war. Every man with a musket is looking for a reason to look at us sideways. The Maryland Gazette printed three pieces last month about Indian sympathies with the French. Three. Men read those things aloud in the taverns."

"I know what they print."

"Then you know what Hale could do with a rumor if it came to him wrong." He took a half step forward. She did not move. He stopped. "I am going to tell him there may be objects. That I'm concerned for your safety. That I think a visit would settle the matter." He swallowed. "I am telling you so you have time. That is all I can do."

The cold was thorough now, coming through the open door around him, finding the back of her neck above her cap. The fire behind her gave off its low heat and the smell of woodsmoke mixed with the dried herbs she had hung from the beam — sage and something sharper, the smell of the mound's earth still faint in the bundle she had brought inside two nights ago and not yet decided where to put.

She thought about that bundle. She thought about the shelf above the door where she kept the gorget wrapped in linen, and the three small bundles of tobacco she had prepared for the next visit to the high ground, and the gourd rattle with the cracked handle that had been their grandmother's, which she kept in the oak chest under a folded blanket.

None of it was here now. She had moved it. She had moved it four days ago, when something — not a sound, not a sign, just a feeling that had lived in her chest like a splinter — had told her to.

She looked at her brother's face and understood that the feeling had been about him.

"You're asking me if I've hidden things," she said.

"I'm asking you to be safe."

"You're asking me to tell you I've hidden things so you can be easy in your mind about what you've done."

His jaw tightened. The hat stopped turning. "I did this for you."

"You did this," she said, "because you are afraid. And because you have decided that if you become useful enough to them, they will not look at you the way they look at the rest of us." She kept her voice level. She had learned that from their grandfather — the man who had taught them both that the loudest voice in a room was always the most frightened. "You may be right. I don't know. But don't call it protection and expect me to thank you."

He stood very still. In the growing light she could see the brass buttons more clearly — five of them, polished, the kind a man bought deliberately.

"They're calling it heathenism," he said quietly. "That's the word Hale will use. Heathen ceremonies. Tessa, they took Crow's Foot to the county jail in La Plata. He was not a sick man when they brought him in."

"I know what happened to Crow's Foot."

"Then you know what Hale could do."

She looked at him. He was forty-one years old. He had their grandfather's broad forehead and their mother's careful eyes, and he had, she realized, been frightened for a long time — not newly frightened, not frightened by the war or by the men taken up in Nanjemoy, but frightened in the way that had been building in him since they were children and the world had kept showing him what it did to people who were visible in the wrong way.

She understood that fear. She had it herself. She had simply made a different choice about what to do with it.

"I have been going to the parish at Port Tobacco for three years now," he said. "Father Aldric knows my name. When they came asking about the men at Nanjemoy, he told them I was a communicant in good standing. He said it in writing." He set the hat down at his side. "That piece of paper is the only reason I am sitting here and not in a cell."

"I'm glad you're sitting here," she said.

"Then come with me. Come to the parish. Father Aldric will receive you. You're already baptized — it would not even be a full conversion, only a return. A public return. You'd be on the register. You'd have a name on a piece of paper in a church that the constable reads."

"I have a name on a piece of paper already."

"Not the right paper."

She looked at him. "They took Crow's Foot," she said. "And he was a communicant."

Thomas's jaw tightened. "He had not been to services in two years. Everyone knew it. He had gone back to — " He stopped.

"To what?"

"To the old ways." He said it carefully, like a man navigating a floor with rotten boards.

"The old ways," she said.

"Tessa."

"I'm not angry. I'm asking you to say what you mean."

He looked at the hat in his hand. "He was conducting ceremonies. In the woods. At the mound." He paused. "People saw him. People talked. The constable had three witnesses."

She thought of the mound. The oaks above it, their roots going deep into the packed soil, the smell of damp earth and old leaves. The gorget sitting in its wrapping of bark cloth in the hollow she had made at the mound's edge, still unfinished, its second hole not yet drilled.

"And you believe," she said, "that if I come to Port Tobacco and sit in a pew and let Father Aldric write my name in his register, that will be enough."

"I believe it is more than you have now."

"What I have now," she said, "is a house that looks like a baptized woman's house. A Bible on the shelf. A constable who will find nothing to alarm a Christian soul." She paused. "What I have now is enough."

"For how long?"

She did not answer that. She would not pretend to him that she knew.

"I'll think on it," she said.

He knew. She could see it in the small tightening around his eyes, the way he picked up the hat and held it again. He knew she was not going to think on it, that *thinking on it* was what she said when she meant *no* and did not want to spend the rest of the evening arguing.

"All right," he said.

He put his hat on.

"Thomas," she said, and she used the name deliberately, the English name, so he would hear what she thought of it. "Go home. Go to your church. Tell your constable you've done your duty."

He looked at her for a long moment. His face did the thing it had learned to do — went still, went careful. But underneath it she could see the boy who had broken the clay pipe, who had needed her to say it wasn't his fault, who had always needed her to make the world smaller and safer than it was.

She could not do that for him. She had not been able to do it for a long time.

"I am not going to report you," she said. She said it plainly, because it was true and because she wanted him to carry it. "You're my brother. I won't say your name to anyone. But I won't forgive you either. Not today. Maybe not ever. I don't know yet."

He nodded once and went out into the dark.

She stood in the doorway until she heard no more footsteps. Then she stepped back inside, lifted the latch, and stood with her hand flat against the door for a moment — feeling the cold wood, the iron of the latch, the solidity of the house around her. The fire had settled lower while she stood there. She went to the hearth and fed it a piece of split oak, and she watched the flame take hold.

The constable would come. Perhaps today. Perhaps in a few days.

She looked at the room — the table, the benches, the oak chest, the shelf above the door. Everything ordinary. Everything exactly as a baptized woman living quietly would keep it. The shelf above the door held a Bible, a brass candlestick, and a small looking-glass with a crack across one corner. The chest held blankets and two pewter plates and a length of undyed linen she had been meaning to hem for three months.

Nothing else.

She thought of the high ground. The mound in its stand of oaks, the soil darker there than anywhere else in the county, packed and layered and holding what it held. She had moved the gorget there four days ago — not finished yet, still needing its second suspension hole drilled through — and the tobacco bundles, and the rattle, and the small clay figure she had made last winter that she had not yet decided what to do with.

She had moved them because the splinter in her chest had told her to. And the splinter had been right.

The fire caught fully now, filling the room with its orange light, and the smell of woodsmoke thickened, and somewhere outside the crows were building toward their morning argument in the oaks, and Tessa stood at the hearth and thought about her brother's brass buttons and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that the mound was the only safe place left.

The constable came on a Tuesday, at midmorning.

She heard the horses first — two sets of hooves on the packed dirt of the lane, unhurried, the sound of men who were not trying to surprise anyone. She was at the hearth stirring a pot of maize porridge when the knock came, three solid raps, and then the voice: "In the king's name. Open for Constable Hale."

She set the spoon on the hearthstone. She smoothed her petticoat. She opened the door.

There were two of them. The constable was a broad man in a plain wool coat the color of old bark, his felt hat pulled low against the grey sky. He carried a short wooden staff in his right hand and a folded paper in his left. The man behind him was younger, thinner, with the look of someone's apprentice doing an errand he had not asked for. He held the horses.

"Constable Hale," she said.

"Mistress." He looked past her into the room. Not rudely — just looking, the way a man does when he is already deciding what he will write down later. "I have a complaint laid before the county court. I am required to inquire into it and to look about the premises. I would ask your permission to come in."

"You have it," she said, and stepped back.

He came in alone. The younger man stayed with the horses.

The room smelled of woodsmoke and porridge and the dried onions hanging in a braid from the rafter above the window. The constable stood in the middle of it and turned slowly, taking it in. The wide pine boards underfoot, their gaps dark with old grime. The rough table with its two benches. The oak chest against the wall, iron-banded, its lid closed. The spinning wheel in the corner, her distaff leaning against it with a half-worked length of flax still wound on. The Bible on the shelf above the door.

His eyes stopped on the Bible.

She said nothing.

"You live alone," he said.

"I do."

"No family nearby."

"My brother is in Port Tobacco."

He walked to the chest and looked at her. She nodded. He lifted the lid. Inside: two folded lengths of linen, a winter petticoat, a pair of shoes with the buckles gone dull, a small earthenware crock sealed with cloth and twine. He lifted the crock, turned it, set it back. He closed the lid.

He looked at the loft.

"There's a ladder," she said. "On the left side of the chimney."

He found it and went up. She heard his boots on the boards overhead, moving slowly from one end to the other, the creak of his weight where the boards ran thin near the east wall. A pause. Then he came back down.

He crossed to the hearth and looked at the things hanging there — the iron pot, the ladle, the kettle hook with its worn leather wrapping. He looked at the axe leaning by the door. He looked at the window, its four small panes of wavy glass letting in a flat, milky light.

"The complaint," he said, "concerns the keeping of heathen objects. Idols, or the like. Instruments of superstitious observance."

"I keep no such things," she said.

He looked at her steadily. He was not unkind. That was the thing she had not expected — he was simply doing the work, the way you do work you have been given, without particular pleasure or malice. "The complaint was made under oath," he said.

"I understand."

"Is there a cellar?"

"A root cellar. Through that door."

He went through. She heard him moving among the stored apples and the turnip bin and the small cask of salt pork. The smell of cold earth and brine drifted back into the room. The maize porridge had gone quiet on the fire — she crossed and stirred it, because there was nothing else to do with her hands and she did not want him to come back and find her standing still in the middle of the floor like a woman waiting to be caught.

He came back.

He stood at the table and looked at her for a moment, and she looked back at him, and the wind pressed against the small panes and made a thin sound around the door frame, and the tallow candle on the shelf guttered once.

He reached into his coat and took out a second folded paper and set it on the table.

"I am required to leave this," he said. "It is a notice of inquiry. It states that a complaint has been received and investigated and that the matter remains open to the court's consideration." He paused. "You are advised to present yourself to the county court if you have evidence to offer in your own behalf."

"I understand," she said.

He picked up his staff. He looked around the room one more time — not suspiciously now, just the last look of a man completing a task, making sure he had not missed anything he would need to account for later.

"Mistress," he said, and touched the brim of his hat.

"Constable."

He went out. She heard the younger man say something she could not make out, and Hale's short reply, and then the two sets of hooves moving back down the lane, unhurried as they had come.

She stood at the table. The paper sat in front of her, folded in thirds, sealed with a small blob of red wax pressed with a plain seal. She did not pick it up immediately. She looked at it the way you look at something you have been expecting long enough that its arrival is almost a relief.

Then she picked it up and broke the seal and read it.

The language was formal and cramped, the clerk's hand small and precise. *Whereas a complaint hath been duly received and sworn before the Justices of Charles County Court, touching the keeping and practice of heathenish and superstitious observances by one Tessa, residing on the south road below the creek, it is hereby ordered that the said Tessa be put on notice that she is held under observation of the court and that any evidence of idolatry or practices contrary to Christian order shall be presented forthwith to the said Justices, who will proceed as the law directs. Given under the hand of the court this 14th day of October, in the year of our Lord 1762.*

She read it twice. Then she folded it again along its original creases and carried it to the hearth and held it over the fire. The wax caught first, a small blue flicker, and then the paper browned and curled and went up in a clean, quick flame. She held it until the last corner was gone and then dropped the ash into the coals.

She straightened and stood at the hearth, watching the coals settle.

*Any evidence.* There was none. There would be none. Everything she was — everything she carried, everything she kept — was three-quarters of a mile away in the packed earth of the mound, and the mound was older than the county court and older than the king's name and older than the word *heathen*, and it would be there long after Hale's paper had rotted in whatever chest he kept his records in.

She was still angry. She had not let herself feel it while he was in the room, and now it moved through her chest like heat from a banked fire — not at Hale, who was only a man doing his work, but at the shape of the thing, the fact of it. Her brother's voice before dawn: *I want you to know before I do it.* The small mercy of the warning and the wound of the act together, the two of them inseparable, the way they had always been inseparable, the way she supposed they would remain.

She would not report him. She had decided that in the moment he told her what he planned to do, had decided it so quickly and completely that it had not even felt like a decision. She would not give Thomas — she could not call him by his old name anymore; he had put it down himself and she would not pick it up for him — she would not give him the satisfaction of knowing she had tried to strike back. And she would not give Hale the satisfaction of watching two of them tear at each other.

But she would not forgive him either. Some things you could hold together: gratitude and anger, understanding and refusal. She had learned that from their mother, who had held contradictions the way other women held their children — close, and carefully, and without pretending they were something simpler.

She went to the shelf and took down the Bible. She opened it to no particular page and looked at the columns of small black type. Then she closed it and put it back.

Outside, the crows had started up again in the oaks above the lane. The wind had steadied from the northwest, carrying the smell of rain still an hour or two off. She looked at the window. The flat grey light was thinning toward afternoon.

The gorget was at the mound. The second hole still needed drilling. She had the small iron awl in her apron pocket — she had put it there this morning without thinking about it, the way you carry the thing you know you will need before you have decided to go.

She put on her cloak and went out.

The lane ran south between two lines of old oaks whose branches had gone bare enough that the sky showed through in patches, grey and close, the light the color of pewter left in a drawer. She walked quickly. The ground was soft from two days of rain and her shoes left clean prints in the clay that the next rain would erase.

She did not think about Thomas on the walk. She had given him enough of the afternoon already.

The mound sat at the edge of the tree line where the slope leveled off above the creek bottom, a low rise covered in broom sedge gone tawny with the season, a few young cedars taking hold along the northern face. She had known this place since she was small enough that her mother had to lift her over the fallen chestnut at the path's end. The chestnut was long rotted now, only a dark seam in the ground to mark where it had been.

She went to the eastern side, where the soil was softer and the sedge grew thinner, and crouched and worked her hands in under the root mat at the base of the slope. The bundle was there — wrapped in two layers of oilskin, tied with a cord she had replaced in the spring. She unwrapped it without standing.

The gorget lay in her palm.

She had been working it since April. The shell was whelk, thick-walled, the outer surface ground smooth with a piece of sandstone she kept for the purpose. The shape was a rough oval, slightly wider than her palm, with a spiral she had incised in the center — not deep, just enough that a finger could follow the line. The first suspension hole was clean and round, drilled from both faces to meet in the middle. The second was started but not finished, a shallow pit that had not yet broken through.

She took the awl from her apron pocket and turned the gorget over and found the angle she had been working at. The light was poor. She had known it would be poor. She worked by feel, pressing the iron tip into the pit and rotating it slowly, not forcing it, letting the tool find the grain of the shell the way her mother had taught her to find the grain of wood — not against it, never against it, always with it, always patient.

The shell was dense here. She had expected that.

She thought, without meaning to, about the magistrate's office in La Plata. She knew the clerk there — a thin man with ink permanently in the creases of his knuckles who had once helped her establish a boundary question on the south field after the neighbor's fence moved. She had stood in that office and given her name and her mark and the clerk had written it down without looking at her face, which was exactly the kind of invisibility that was useful. She could go back. She could say: *the complaint was given by my brother, who calls himself Thomas now, who gave my name to the constable Hale as a means of securing his own standing with the parish.* She could say that. She had the standing. The clerk knew her mark.

She pressed the awl and felt the shell give slightly, the pit deepening by the width of a thumbnail.

Going to the magistrate would mean being seen. Not the useful invisibility of the boundary question, but the wrong kind of visibility — a woman making a complaint against her converted brother during a war year, when the county court was already nervous about anything that smelled like Indian trouble, when the *Maryland Gazette* was running reports of frontier raids every second week and men were reading them aloud in the tavern to men who could not read. A woman named Tessa who had just received a court notice for heathenish observances, walking into La Plata to name her informant. She could hear how it would sound. She could see the clerk's face change.

It would not help her. It would only make her easier to find.

The awl broke through.

She felt it — a small, clean give, the tip emerging on the far side of the shell — and she stopped and held the gorget up against the grey sky and looked through the new hole. Light came through it, a tiny circle of sky.

She sat back on her heels.

The anger was still there. She was not going to pretend otherwise, not even to herself, out here where there was no one to perform steadiness for. Her brother had walked into a church and put down his name and come to her door in the dark and said *I want you to know before I do it*, and she had stood in her own kitchen and listened, and now Hale had come with his younger man and his folded paper, and she had burned the paper in her own hearth, and now she was sitting in the cold at the edge of a burial mound with shell dust on her hands and an awl in her pocket, and Thomas was — where? At the parish, probably. Or at the tavern where the men who had accepted him gathered to drink and read the war news and feel, for a little while, like they were on the right side of something.

She understood why he had done it. She had always been able to understand the thing she could not forgive, and she had stopped expecting those two facts to resolve.

He had been afraid. He had looked at the shape of what was coming — the war, the county court, the way men like Hale moved through the countryside with their warrants and their staffs and their absolute confidence in the king's name — and he had calculated that the only safety available was the safety of the converted, the named, the registered. He was not wrong about the danger. He had never been wrong about the danger. He had only been wrong about what she was willing to trade for protection.

She would not trade this.

She smoothed the gorget with her thumb, following the spiral from its center outward. The incised line was fine enough that you had to look for it in poor light, but your finger found it immediately. That was what she had wanted — something that declared itself to touch rather than to sight. Something a person would have to hold to know.

The mound had been holding things for longer than she could trace. Her mother had brought her here and said: *this is where they put what mattered*. She had not explained further. She had not needed to. Tessa had been seven years old and she had understood that the mound was a kind of keeping, a way of saying *this happened, this person lived, this was real*, addressed to no one in particular and to everyone who would come after.

She had been adding to it since her mother died. The pipe bowl Matta had left her, which she had placed in the second layer with her own hands. Small things. Careful things. Each one a sentence in a language she did not have a name for, addressed to the same general future.

The gorget was different. The gorget was specific.

She knew, with the same quiet certainty she brought to most things she had thought through completely, that she would put it in the mound before the ground froze. Not as a surrender. Not as a burial of herself. As a record. As a way of saying: *I was here, I held this, I did not let them take it from me, I chose where it went.*

Thomas had chosen his path. She had chosen hers. The difference was that his choice required her erasure and hers required nothing from him at all.

She wrapped the gorget back in the oilskin and tucked the bundle under the root mat and pressed the soil back into place. She stood and brushed the clay from her skirt. The cedars along the northern face moved in the wind, a dry, papery sound.

The rain smell was closer now. She had perhaps half an hour.

She looked at the mound — the low rise of it, the sedge bending, the packed earth that held everything she had put there and everything that had been put there before her, layer on layer, each layer someone's answer to the same question. She did not say anything. She had never been the kind of person who talked to the dead, or to the ground, or to the sky. She simply looked, the way you look at something you have made a decision about, confirming the decision to yourself without ceremony.

Then she turned and walked back up the lane toward the house, the first drops of rain beginning to spot the clay around her feet, the oaks closing behind her.

She came back the following evening, when the light had gone grey and flat and the tree line to the west was just a black edge against a sky the color of old pewter. She carried the gorget in her apron pocket, wrapped in its oilskin, and she carried the small iron-bladed knife she used for root work, and she carried nothing else.

The mound was quiet. A crow had been working the field to the south all afternoon — she had heard it from the house — but it was gone now, and the only sound was the wind moving through the sedge and, somewhere deeper in the trees, the slow drip of last night's rain still falling from the oak canopy onto the leaf mat below. The ground was soft from the rain. That was good. It would be easier to open and easier to close.

She went to the northern face, where the earth was oldest and the layers most distinct, where the sedge gave way to a low shelf of packed clay. She had been here enough times to know the ground by feel. She knelt and pressed both palms flat against the surface, the way her mother had taught her — not to divine anything, not to ask permission, only to feel what was there. The clay was cold and damp and dense. Under her hands the mound held its silence the way a held breath holds.

She began with the knife, working the blade along the edge of the shelf where the soil was loosest, cutting through the mat of fibrous roots without tearing. The roots of sedge were fine and pale and they came away in small sections, and she laid each section aside in order on the ground beside her, so she could replace them the same way. She had done this before. She knew how to open a layer without destroying it.

Beneath the root mat the soil was darker, damper, and it smelled of iron and old leaves and something older than either — the deep, mineral smell of undisturbed earth, the smell that always made her stop for a moment and simply breathe it. She worked slowly, using her fingers now instead of the knife, pulling the soil back in small handfuls and setting it to the left. She was not going deep. She did not need to disturb what was already there. She needed only a depression, a pocket, a place of receiving.

She was working above the second layer — she could feel the slight change in soil density beneath her fingers, the faint compaction that was different from the surrounding earth, the way a scar is different from the skin around it, and she kept above it, opening a space in what she understood to be the mound's third layer. She smoothed the floor of the depression with her palm and then sat back on her heels and looked at what she had made: a small hollow, roughly the width of both her hands set side by side, the walls clean and dark, the bottom level.

She took the gorget from her pocket.

She unwrapped it without looking at it, the oilskin coming away in her hands, and then she held the gorget in both palms and looked at it for the last time. The spiral caught what little light was left in the sky. The two suspension holes were dark and small. The shell was the color of old cream, slightly translucent at the thinner edges, and the incised line of the spiral ran from the center outward in a single unbroken curve that she had spent the better part of two months cutting and refining and smoothing until it was exactly what she had wanted it to be. She had worn it once, on the cord, at her collarbone, the morning after Hale had come and gone. She had stood in her kitchen and worn it for perhaps an hour while she thought through what she was going to do, and then she had taken it off and wrapped it and put it under the root mat, and she had not worn it since.

She set it in the depression, face up, the spiral toward the sky.

Then she said his name. Once, quietly, the way you say a word you are writing down — not to call him, not to curse him, only to put it in the record. *Thomas.* The cedars moved. The dripping in the oak canopy continued its slow count. She did not say anything else. There was nothing else that needed saying. The name was the record. The name said: *my brother did this, and I know why, and I do not forgive it, and I did not report him, and this is where I have put the account of it, because this is the only place I trust to keep it whole.*

She began to fill the depression.

She worked the soil back in with her hands, pressing it gently around the gorget, not packing it hard, letting it settle. The gorget disappeared by degrees — first the suspension holes, then the outer edge of the spiral, then the center, then the last pale gleam of shell — and then there was only dark soil and her hands moving over it, smoothing. She replaced the root sections in order, pressing each one back into place along the cut line, tamping the edges down with her thumb. When she was done she sat back and looked at the surface. In the poor light it was indistinguishable from the rest of the northern face. If you did not know, you would not know.

She sat there for a little while, not doing anything. The cold was coming up through the ground into her knees and she did not move. The sky had darkened from pewter to the deep blue-grey that comes just before full dark, and the first stars were beginning to appear above the tree line, faint and far. The crow had not come back. The mound was quiet.

She thought about her brother in his church pew, sitting straight-backed and attentive the way he had always sat when he wanted to be seen sitting well, and she thought about herself kneeling here in the cold with clay under her fingernails and the smell of turned earth on her hands, and she understood, with the same flat certainty she brought to most things she had finished thinking about, that the distance between those two positions was not one she was going to close. He had made his calculation. She had made hers. His calculation required her to become someone the county court would not notice, someone without ceremonies and without gorgets and without a mound she visited at dusk. Her calculation required only that she continue to be exactly what she was, which was a woman who knew where her dead were and what they had left her and what she owed them.

The mound held it now. Matta's pipe bowl in the second layer. The gorget in the third. Her mother's memory in the first, though she had not placed that — that had been placed before her, by hands she did not know the names of. The mound held all of it, layer on layer, each layer someone's answer to the same question, and the question was not *what happened* but *who was here, who cared, who chose to keep it*. She was the latest answer. There would be others after her, or there would not. She could not control that. She could only do what she had done, which was to put the thing in the ground and smooth the surface and trust the earth to hold it longer than she could.

She stood. Her knees ached from the cold. She brushed the clay from her skirt with both hands, a dry, businesslike sound in the quiet, and she picked up the knife and the oilskin and tucked them into her apron. The root sections she had set aside were gone — she had replaced them all. There was nothing left on the surface to show she had been here.

She looked at the mound one more time. Low, dark, patient. The sedge bent in the wind along its southern edge. The packed earth of the northern face held its silence.

She turned and walked up the lane toward the house.

The dark came down around her as she walked, the lane narrowing between the oaks, the clay soft underfoot from last night's rain. She could smell woodsmoke from the chimney — she had left the fire banked and it had held, which meant the house would be warm when she reached it. She would heat the salt pork she had left in the pot. She would eat at the table in the firelight. She would sleep.

And the mound would hold what she had put there, the way it had always held what had been put there, the way it would go on holding it after she was gone and her name was forgotten and the lane had grown over and the house had fallen and the field had gone back to wood. The mound would hold it. Layer on layer. Each layer a record. Each record addressed to no one in particular and to everyone who would come after.

She walked home in the dark and did not look back, and the trees closed behind her, and the mound stood in the field in the silence and the cold, keeping everything it had been given, as it had always done, as it would do for longer than she had words to say.
