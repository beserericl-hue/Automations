The television threw blue light across Nora's hands.

She had not moved from the armchair since Tayak's call. The thermos of coffee on the side table had gone cold an hour ago, but she had not noticed until she poured a cup and drank it anyway — thick, bitter, the filter grounds settled at the bottom like silt. She set the cup down without looking at it.

The news was on. She had turned it on for the weather and then not turned it off.

The anchor was a woman with dark hair pulled severely back, speaking in the measured cadence that meant something had happened that the station considered important but not catastrophic. Nora understood the tone before she understood the words. Then the image cut away from the studio — its curved desk, the LED wall behind it pulsing with a map of Maryland in cool blue — and there was the commission room, the pale birch table, the water carafes and the microphones and the overhead lights that had made everything look flatter and more official than it felt from inside.

And there was the quilt.

Nora went still.

She watched her own hands move across the fabric. On the screen they looked older than she thought of them — the knuckles swollen, the skin loose over the tendons — but they moved with certainty, smoothing the central panel flat, pressing the corners down as if the quilt might otherwise lift and drift away from her. She had not known she was doing that. She had thought she was simply placing it on the table.

The camera held on the quilt for a moment before cutting to her face.

She looked at herself the way she looked at photographs of her mother: with the recognition that comes a half-second late, the slight adjustment the mind makes when it encounters a face it knows in a context where it did not expect to find it. The woman on the screen was eighty-two years old. Her hair was white and pinned back. She sat with her hands flat on the quilt's surface and spoke in a voice that was steadier than Nora remembered it being.

She could not hear what she was saying. She had left the volume low.

She reached for the remote on the arm of the chair and turned it up, and her own voice filled the room.

*— and my grandmother told me when I was nineteen years old. She sat with her hands flat on the table, like this, and she said: I need to tell you something before I lose the words for it. She was seventy-one. She died four years later. I have held what she told me for sixty-two years.*

The commission room was quiet on the screen. Nora could see the backs of heads in the foreground — the commissioners, the legal staff, a young man with a notepad whose pen had stopped moving.

*I did not tell my daughter. I should have. She was gone before I found the right moment, and I kept waiting for the right moment, and there was no right moment, and then she was gone.*

Nora watched herself say this. She had not planned to say it. It had come out of her the way water comes through a crack in old plaster — not a decision, just a fact of pressure and time.

The anchor returned, speaking over a still image of the quilt — the central panel, the woman figure with her arms raised, the stitched lines Nora had spent eleven months on in the years after Ida died. The caption read: *Nora Moyaone, 82, Piscataway elder, Charles County.* The text was small and white below her image, on a blurred background of the commission room's pale wood paneling.

Nora turned the volume back down.

Outside the window the November dark was complete. The triple-glazed glass gave back a faint reflection of the room — the lamp on the side table, the armchair, the small shape of herself in it — and beyond that nothing, just the field and the tree line she could not see but knew was there. She had lived in this house for forty-one years. She knew where everything was in the dark.

She thought about her daughter.

She allowed herself thirty seconds of it, which was more than she usually allowed. Her daughter had been thirty-one years old in 1994. She had seen the quilt once, in its early years, and said it was beautiful in the way people say something is beautiful when they are thinking about something else. Nora had not told her what the quilt was for. She had been waiting until the quilt was finished. Then her daughter was gone — not all at once, which would have been a different kind of grief, but slowly, a slow recession, like a tide that does not come back in — and the quilt had sat in the sewing room for years with the central panel half-done, and Nora had told herself she would finish it when she was ready, and she had not been ready for a long time.

She had told Tayak instead. Not everything, and not all at once, and not soon enough — but she had told her.

On the screen, the news had moved on. A man in a suit was speaking about something in Oslo. Nora watched him without hearing him.

The fear she had carried into the commission room that morning was still present, but it had changed shape. It was no longer the fear of speaking. It was the fear that speaking, now, after thirty years of not speaking, was a different kind of wrong — that she had done the right thing at the wrong time, and that the thirty years were not simply a delay but a cost paid by someone else. By her daughter, who had not been told. By Tayak, who had grown up with a grandmother who kept the most important thing in a room with the door closed.

She pressed her palms flat on her thighs, the way she had pressed them on the quilt on the commission table.

The coffee cup was cold. The lamp made a small warm circle on the side table. The news anchor's voice was a murmur she could not make out.

Nora sat in the blue light and did not look away from the screen, and did not allow herself to decide yet whether what she had done was enough.

She reached for the remote and muted the television.

The silence was immediate and complete. The triple-glazed window held the November dark on the other side of it like a wall, and the room was so quiet she could hear the thermos on the kitchen counter settling — a small tick of cooling metal.

She sat with the silence.

She had spent sixty-two years filling silence with other things. Work. The quilt. The ceremonies she attended and the ones she organized and the ones she simply showed up for because showing up was what you did when you were the one who remembered. She had been good at filling silence. She had been expert at it.

Now she let it stay.

Her daughter had been twenty-three years old when Nora should have told her. That was the number she had never let herself say plainly. Twenty-three. Old enough to hold it. Old enough to understand what the quilt was for, why the names mattered, what the mound meant to the family that had tended it and lost it and tended it again across two thousand years of tending. Nora had looked at her daughter at twenty-three and thought: not yet. She had looked at her at twenty-six and thought: when the quilt is further along. At twenty-eight she had thought: when things are more settled between us. At thirty-one her daughter had begun the slow recession that Nora still did not have a clean word for — not a leaving, not a breaking, just a withdrawal, a tide going out — and then there had been no moment, and then there had been Tayak, nine years old, standing in Nora's kitchen with her shoes still on, and the door to her daughter's room had closed in Nora's mind and she had not opened it since.

Eight years. That was how long she had between her daughter turning twenty-three and the recession beginning. Eight years in which she could have sat down at the kitchen table, hands flat on the surface the way Ida had done, and said: *I need to tell you something before I lose the words for it.*

She had not said it.

She had been afraid of many things in those eight years. She had been afraid her daughter would not hold it carefully. She had been afraid she would hold it too carefully — that the weight of it would change something between them that Nora was not ready to see changed. She had been afraid that saying it aloud would make it real in a way that the silence kept it from being real, which was the most dishonest of all the fears and the one she had worked hardest not to look at directly.

Her daughter had not known. She had left without knowing. Wherever she was now — and Nora did not allow herself to think about where she was now, not tonight, not with the commission room still lit up in her memory — she was carrying nothing of what Nora had carried. The chain had skipped her. It had gone from Nora to Tayak with a generation missing in the middle, like a quilt panel pulled out and lost, and you could see the gap if you knew where to look.

Tayak had grown up without the name.

Nora had told herself, in the years after her daughter left, that she would tell Tayak when Tayak was old enough. Then she had told herself she would tell her when Tayak was established in her work. Then she had told herself she would tell her when the moment was right, and the moment had not been right, and the mound had nearly become a parking easement for a heritage-tourism development run by a man in a Columbia office park whose registered address was a suite number.

That was what the silence had almost cost.

She had not cried in the commission room. She did not cry now. Crying was not the thing. The thing was the arithmetic of it, which she had never done before, not plainly, not the way she was doing it now in the muted blue light of the television with the November dark outside the window and the thermos ticking in the kitchen.

Her daughter: one generation.

Tayak's childhood: nine years old, standing in Nora's kitchen with her shoes on, and Nora had taken her in and fed her and loved her and taught her the creek and the watercress and the names of the trees along the eastern fence, and had not told her the one thing that would have told her who she was.

The mound: eleven months of excavation, a thirty-day clock, a state suspension notice, and Garrett Slade's name on a document that could have ended it.

Those were the numbers. She would not add to them or subtract from them. She would not tell herself it could not have been otherwise, because it could have been otherwise — she had simply not made it otherwise, and now she was eighty-two years old and the arithmetic was what it was.

She set the remote on the side table.

She got up from the armchair, which took a moment — her knees in November were a separate negotiation from her knees in July — and she went into the kitchen and poured the cold coffee down the sink and rinsed the cup and set it on the drying rack. The LED strip under the cabinet came on when she moved past it, throwing a thin bar of white light across the dark composite counter. She stood in it for a moment.

Then she went to the sewing room.

The room was small enough that she could touch both walls if she spread her arms, which she never did, but she knew it was possible. She had arranged it herself twenty years ago when the children's bedroom had finally emptied: the Husqvarna machine on the long table under the window, the cutting mat to its left with the metric grid worn faint at the center from use, the plastic storage boxes stacked along the far wall sorted by color — blues in the bottom row, earth tones in the middle, the whites and creams and undyed cottons on top. The adjustable lamp was already on. She had left it on since morning.

She did not turn on the ceiling light. The lamp was enough.

She pulled the step stool to the shelving and reached the top box — the undyed cottons, folded in quarters, edges aligned because she had folded them herself and she folded things correctly. She lifted out a piece that was roughly the right size, a half-meter of plain cotton-muslin she had bought at Stoff og Stil in the spring for a backing she had never gotten around to making. It was the color of old paper. Good. She did not want white. White was not the right color for what she was going to make.

She spread it on the cutting mat and smoothed it flat with both palms, the way you smooth a thing when you want to understand it before you mark it. The fabric had a slight tooth to it — not rough, just present. She could feel the weave under her hands.

The chalk was in the tin on the shelf above the machine. White chalk, a tailor's wedge, slightly worn at the tip from the last panel she had done. She picked it up and stood over the fabric and did not draw immediately. She looked at the blank rectangle for a moment the way she had looked at Tayak's face in the commission room — not waiting for something to appear, but letting what she already knew arrange itself.

She knew the shape of the mound. She had known it since she was nineteen years old and Ida had taken her out to the high ground above Mattawoman Creek on a January morning with the frost still on the grass and said *this is what I need you to remember*. She had stood at the edge of it with her hands in her coat pockets and she had looked at it and she had thought: it is smaller than I expected. And Ida had said: it is exactly the right size. She had not understood that for years.

She drew the outline in one motion — the long low curve of it, the way it rose from the surrounding ground not sharply but gradually, the way a held breath rises in a chest. She drew it from memory and it was correct. She had drawn it before, in the central panel of the quilt that was now on Tayak's worktable or wherever Tayak had put it, and in the smaller panels she had made and unmade over the years when she needed to think with her hands instead of her head. She knew the curve the way she knew the shape of her own knuckles.

Then she stopped.

The thing she was trying to draw now was not the mound closed. It was the mound open. And she had never drawn that before, because it had never been open before. Not in her lifetime. Not in Ida's lifetime, or Ida's grandmother's lifetime, or in any lifetime she knew the name of going backward through the chain. The mound had been closed and kept and walked around and spoken over and remembered, and that was what it was — a kept thing, a buried thing, a thing held in darkness because the darkness was what protected it.

And now Tayak had opened it.

She stood with the chalk in her hand and looked at the outline she had drawn — the long low curve, the mound as she had always known it — and she felt something she did not have a precise word for. Not grief. Not relief. Something that had the weight of both without being either.

She drew the opening.

Not a wound. Not a cut. She drew it the way Tayak had described it in the field — a careful rectangular trench, the walls straight, the corners clean, the kind of opening that was made with attention and intention and respect for what was underneath. She drew it at the center, where the deepest layer was, and she made it the right proportion: small relative to the whole, the way a door is small relative to the wall that holds it.

Then she stopped again.

Inside the opening she needed to draw the light.

This was the part she had not been sure she could do. Not because she didn't know what it looked like — she knew exactly what it looked like, had known since she was sitting in the armchair watching the news and the thought had arrived fully formed, the mound open, the light coming out, and she had understood that this was the next panel and that she was going to make it tonight. She had known the shape of it immediately. What she had not been sure of was whether she could render it in fabric and thread in a way that was honest. Not decorative. Not symbolic in the way that symbols become decorative when they are handled carelessly. She needed it to be the actual thing, which was not fire and not sunlight and not anything she had a name for in English or in the Piscataway words she knew, which were not many. It was the thing that had been in the ground for two thousand years and that Tayak had found with a Marshalltown trowel and a field log and eleven months of careful, stubborn, methodical work.

She drew it as lines. Not rays — rays were too much like a child's drawing of the sun. She drew them as they actually moved, which was not outward from a center but upward and slightly outward, the way heat moves above a warm surface, the way breath moves in cold air. She drew six lines, then added a seventh because seven was the right number and she knew why it was the right number, though she did not say it aloud. She drew them in chalk, faint and tentative, the way all first marks should be.

She stepped back and looked at it.

It was right. It was the thing she had meant to draw.

She went to the storage boxes and found the thread she needed. Not gold — gold was wrong, too ceremonial, too much like a decision made for the viewer. She found a cream-colored thread, almost the same tone as the undyed fabric, just warm enough to read against it. The light would be nearly invisible from a distance. You would have to come close to see it. That was correct. That was how it actually worked — you had to come close, you had to be willing to get down in the ground, you had to do the work before the thing revealed itself.

She threaded the needle on the first try. Her hands were steady. They were always steady in this room. Whatever her knees did in November, whatever her back said after an hour in the armchair, her hands were steady when she was working. She had been grateful for this for forty years and she was grateful for it now.

She sat down at the machine, then changed her mind and stayed at the hand-stitching table. This part she would do by hand. The machine was for the piecing, for the structural work, for the parts that needed to be fast and strong. The light coming out of the mound she would do by hand, one stitch at a time, because it had taken two thousand years to get here and she was not going to rush the last part of it.

She began with the first line — the leftmost, the one that rose at the sharpest angle. Running stitch, small and even, the needle going in and coming up in a rhythm she did not have to think about. The lamp threw its light directly onto the fabric. The room smelled of warm dust from the bulb and the faint metallic sharpness of the needle and the particular smell of new cotton, which was different from old cotton the way new wood is different from old wood. The Husqvarna sat silent beside her, its digital display dark.

She worked.

The house was very quiet. The triple-glazed window above the cutting mat held the November dark without letting any of it in — she could see her own reflection in it, bent over the fabric, the lamp making a small bright pool around her hands. Outside there was nothing, or there was everything, the fields and the creek and the high ground two miles east where the mound was, where Tayak had gone back to after the commission building, where the work was still going on. She did not know if Tayak was there now. She thought probably yes.

She finished the first line and began the second.

She was not tired. She had expected to be tired — she was eighty-two years old and she had sat in a commission room for four hours and testified in front of a panel and a camera and her own granddaughter and whatever she had said had been correct and necessary and it had cost her something she was still calculating. She had expected the tiredness to arrive when she sat down to work, the way it sometimes did, the way the body waited for permission. It had not arrived. She felt instead the thing she sometimes felt in the early stages of a panel she knew was going to be good — a kind of forward lean, not physical but something underneath physical. The sense that the work knew where it was going even if she was only following.

She was following.

The second line. The third. Each one slightly longer than the last, rising from the opening in the chalk outline, the cream thread nearly invisible against the undyed cotton, the light that had no name accumulating stitch by stitch.

She thought about Tayak's hands on the pendant in the field log photograph — she had seen the photograph on Kimi's phone, a close-up of the artifact in the tray, the copper green with age, the shape of it familiar in a way that had made her sit down abruptly in Kimi's passenger seat and press her own hands flat on her thighs. She had not told Kimi why. She had said: *give me a minute.* And Kimi had given her a minute, which was one of the things Nora appreciated about Kimi — she knew when to be quiet.

The pendant had looked exactly as Ida had described it. Exactly. The curve of it, the weight of it even in a photograph, the particular way the copper had aged into itself rather than away from itself. Ida had held it once, briefly, when she was a girl, before it was buried. She had described it to Nora the way you describe something you have touched and cannot touch again: carefully, completely, with the specific details that only touch gives you.

Nora had carried that description for sixty-two years. And then Tayak had found the thing itself.

She set the needle down for a moment and pressed her palms flat on the fabric, on the chalk outline of the mound, on the faint lines of the light coming out of it. The cotton was slightly cool under her hands. She held them there.

She was thinking about the arithmetic again, but differently now. Not the cost of it. The other side of it. Tayak had found the pendant. Tayak had opened the mound. Tayak had stood in the commission room and read the field report into the record and the commission had ruled in their favor, and Nora had said the things she should have said thirty years ago, and the mound was still there, and the chain was still intact, and the name at the bottom of it — the first name, the name she had been carrying since Ida's kitchen — that name was still known. It was still held. It had not been lost.

She lifted her hands. She picked up the needle.

The fourth line. The fifth. The sixth.

She was thinking about the seventh line, the last one, which she would make slightly different from the others — not longer, not brighter, but angled differently, turned just slightly toward the east, toward the creek, toward the direction the name had traveled. She had not decided this consciously. She knew it the way she knew the curve of the mound, the way she knew the shape of Tayak's stillness in the commission room. Some things you do not decide. You simply do them correctly.

She worked until the room felt different — not later, exactly, but settled, the way a room settles when the person in it has stopped waiting for something and started doing the thing they came to do. The lamp hummed its faint warm hum. The needle moved. The light accumulated in the fabric, stitch by stitch, cream on cream, almost invisible, almost there.

She did not look at the clock.

She would look at the clock when she was finished. Not before.

The seventh line was done.

She had angled it east, as she'd known she would, and it looked right — not different from the others in any way a stranger would notice, but turned, aimed, the way a person turns their face toward something they have been waiting to see. She held the panel at arm's length. The lamp caught the cream thread and the lines of light rose from the mound's open center in a way that was almost luminous, almost not there, depending on how you held it.

She set it down on the cutting mat.

She should have been finished. The panel had what it needed. The mound, the opening, the light. That was the design she had planned in the kitchen with the television still on and Tayak's voice on the phone still warm in her ear.

But she looked at the rim of the mound — the chalk outline she had drawn and then stitched over, the curved lip of the opening — and it was empty. She had made the light come out of it and she had made the mound hold the light and she had not put anyone there.

She picked up the chalk pencil.

She was not sure what she was doing. That was not quite true. She was sure in the way she was always sure when a panel told her something she had not planned — the certainty came before the understanding, which was the right order, the order she had learned to trust across forty years of this work. She drew a small figure at the rim. Not inside the mound, not walking away from it. At the rim. Standing there, looking in.

The figure was perhaps four centimetres tall. She had drawn it in three strokes: a vertical line with a slight lean forward, a curved suggestion of a head, two short lines for the arms hanging loose at the sides. It was not detailed. It did not need to be. The posture was enough — that forward lean, that particular quality of someone who has arrived at the edge of something and has not yet decided whether to go further.

She threaded a new needle with a darker thread. Not the cream. Something with more weight. She went through her sorted boxes until she found the right colour — a warm grey-brown, the colour of field soil in November, the colour of the creek bank at low water. She held it against the panel. Yes. That.

She began to sew the figure in.

It was slow work. The figure was small and the stitches had to be small to match, and small stitches at midnight required the kind of attention that was not tired attention but the other kind, the kind that comes when everything unnecessary has burned off and what remains is just the eye and the hand and the thing being made. She was aware of the lamp's warmth on the back of her right hand. She was aware of the faint whirr of the machine when she used it and the comparative silence when she did not. She was working by hand now. The figure required it.

She was halfway through the figure's outline when she understood who it was.

Not Tayak. She had thought, drawing it, that it might be Tayak — Tayak standing at the edge of the trench, looking down into the stratum where the pendant waited. That would have been the obvious reading. A record of the excavation, the archaeologist at the rim. But it was not that. She knew it was not that the way she knew the angle of the seventh line. The figure was leaning forward in the wrong way for Tayak. Tayak's posture at the edge of something was a different posture — precise, contained, the stillness of a person who has already decided and is now only executing the decision. This figure had not decided. This figure was standing at the rim and looking in and had been standing there for a very long time without going further.

Thirty years.

She did not stop sewing. She kept the needle moving, the small careful stitches building the figure's outline in the warm grey-brown thread, and she thought: yes. That is correct. That belongs there.

She was not being cruel to herself. She had had the argument with herself already, in the commission room, in the car on the way home, in the kitchen while the coffee went cold. She had made the accounting. The thirty years of silence had cost something she could not recover, and she was not going to pretend otherwise, but the panel was not an accusation. A panel that was only an accusation was bad work. It was not honest. The honest thing was to put the figure there because the figure had been there — she had stood at the rim for thirty years, knowing the name, knowing the chain, knowing what Tayak was doing two miles east at the mound, and she had not spoken — and the honest thing was also to make the figure looking in, not looking away. She had been looking in the whole time. That was the other part of it, the part she had not let herself say until now. She had not been indifferent. She had been afraid, and the fear had looked like caution, and the caution had looked like protection, and the protection had lasted thirty years and cost Tayak things Nora could not calculate because she did not know all of them.

But she had been looking in. The whole time.

She finished the figure's outline and began the small interior stitches that would give it weight, keep it from being just a line. She used the grey-brown thread for the body and then, without planning it, changed to a darker thread for the figure's hands — not black, but close, a deep charcoal that she found in the third box, under a fold of batik cotton she'd bought at the Stoff og Stil on the main road two winters ago and never used. The hands needed to be visible. She did not know why, only that they did.

Small hands, slightly raised. Not reaching into the mound. Not pressing flat against the rim. Just raised slightly, the way hands raise when a person is about to do something they have not yet done.

She held the panel at arm's length again.

The mound. The opening. The light rising from it in seven lines, cream on undyed cotton, the seventh turned east. And at the rim, the figure — small, dark-handed, leaning forward, looking in.

She thought: *it is a record.*

Not a confession. Not a punishment. A record, the way the mound itself was a record — the way every layer of it was a record of a person who had stood at the edge of grief and done the thing they knew to do with it. Saya had done it. Tessa had done it. Matta, Okwi, Waya, the woman at the bottom of the chain whose name Nora had been carrying for sixty-two years. They had all stood at the edge of something and they had all done the thing, and the thing they had done had traveled forward two thousand years and arrived in Tayak's hands in a trench near Mattawoman Creek on an autumn morning in the year 2024.

And Nora had stood at the rim for thirty years.

And then she had not.

The figure belonged there because both things were true. The thirty years of standing, and then the not-standing. The silence, and then the testimony. You could not have the record without both.

She set the panel down on the cutting mat and pressed her palms flat against it — against the mound, against the light, against the small dark-handed figure at the rim. The cotton was warm from the lamp now. She held her hands there for a moment the way she had pressed them flat in Kimi's passenger seat when she saw the photograph of the pendant, the way Tayak pressed them flat in the field when she was reading the ground, the way the first woman had pressed them flat two thousand years ago and said a name into the earth.

She lifted her hands.

She looked at the clock on the wall above the machine. Twenty past midnight. She had been in the sewing room for four hours.

She was not tired.

She thought she might add one more thing to the panel — not tonight, but tomorrow, or the day after, when she could see it fresh. A very small detail at the base of the mound, below the opening, where the first burial was. Not the pendant. Not the wooden figure. Just a mark, a small dark stitch, to indicate that something was there. That something had been there for a very long time, waiting to be found.

She folded the panel carefully, the figure on the inside so the thread would not catch, and set it on the shelf above the machine where she kept work in progress. She turned off the adjustable lamp. The sewing room went dim, lit only by the glow from the hall.

She stood in the doorway for a moment and looked at the shelf where the panel sat folded, the mound inside it, the light inside the mound, the figure at the rim.

Then she went to make coffee.

The coffee was already made — she had forgotten she'd set the machine before coming back to the sewing room after the news. The thermos on the counter was still warm when she pressed her palm against its side. She poured a cup and stood at the kitchen window with it. Outside was dark, the kind of dark that comes after midnight in late autumn when there is no wind and no moon worth speaking of, just the faint orange wash from the road and the black shapes of the trees at the property line.

She drank the coffee black. It was strong and slightly bitter, the way she'd been making it for fifty years, and she did not mind the bitterness. She had stopped minding it a long time ago.

She thought about the panel on the shelf.

Not the figure at the rim — she was done thinking about that figure for tonight. She had put the figure there and she knew why and that was enough. She was thinking about the bottom of the mound. The small dark stitch she'd told herself she would add tomorrow, or the day after. The mark to indicate that something was there.

The problem was that she knew what the mark needed to be. She had known it since she set the panel down and pressed her palms flat against it. It was not a stitch. It was a figure. A second figure, smaller than the first, placed at the base of the opening — not looking up toward the light, not looking up toward the figure at the rim, but looking *out*. Out of the mound. Out of the panel. Out of the frame entirely.

She had not let herself think about whose face to give it.

She finished the coffee and rinsed the cup and set it on the drying rack. The kitchen was quiet in the way that kitchens are quiet after midnight — the refrigerator's low hum, the faint tick of the heating pipe under the floor, the triple-glazed windows sealing out whatever sound the road might have made. She dried her hands on the dish towel and stood for a moment with her hands flat on the counter.

The counter was cold composite, dark laminate, nothing like the cotton of the panel. But she kept her hands there anyway.

She was thinking about the name.

Not the name she had said in the hearing room — the old name, the first name, the one she had carried for sixty-two years. She had said that name clearly, in front of the commission, in front of the microphone, in front of the camera that had put her face on the evening news. She had said it and it had sounded different than it had ever sounded inside her. Larger was the word she'd used in her own mind, watching the panel, and she still thought that was right — larger, as if the room had given it dimensions it had not had when it lived only in her memory. As if the name had needed air.

She was thinking about the name she had not said.

Her daughter's name.

She did not say it now either. She had a rule about that, a rule she had kept for thirty-five years, and she was not going to break it at twenty past midnight in a quiet kitchen with a cold cup rinsed in the rack. Some rules held because they were right and some rules held because breaking them required more than you had available at a given moment, and she was honest enough with herself, at eighty-two, to know which kind this was.

But she was thinking about the face.

The figure at the bottom of the mound, looking out. She had told herself she did not know whose face to give it, and that was true in the sense that she had not decided. It was not true in the sense that she did not know. She knew. She had known since the moment the need for the figure arrived, the way you sometimes know a thing before you are willing to call it by its name.

Her daughter had been thirty-one years old in 1994. She had seen the quilt once, in its early years, before the central panel was finished. She had looked at it the way you look at something you cannot afford to want. She had not asked about it. She had left in spring of 1989 and she had not come back, and Nora had not said her name aloud since — she was not sure when. Long enough that the silence had become its own kind of structure, load-bearing, and she did not know what would happen to the rest of the house if she removed it.

She went back to the sewing room.

She did not turn on the adjustable lamp over the machine. She turned on only the ceiling light, which was softer, and she took the panel down from the shelf and unfolded it on the cutting mat and looked at it.

The mound. The opening. The seven cream lines of light on undyed cotton, the seventh turned east. The small dark-handed figure at the rim, leaning in.

She looked at the space at the base of the opening. The place where the first burial was. Where the woman whose name she'd carried for sixty-two years had been put into the ground, and where Tayak had found the pendant in a trench near Mattawoman Creek, and where the chain of names had begun.

She thought: *it would be a small figure. Smaller than the one at the rim. You would almost miss it if you were not looking.*

She thought: *she would be looking out, not up. Out of the mound and out of the panel and out of the frame, into whatever space the viewer occupied. The way some figures in old paintings look out — not at you, exactly, but through the frame, into the room, into the present.*

She thought: *I do not have to know her face tonight. I only have to know she belongs there.*

She picked up the needle. Not to sew — she just held it, the way she sometimes held a thing to think with it. The needle was a Schmetz, a size ten, the kind she'd been using for thirty years, and it was familiar in her fingers the way familiar things are familiar: not noticed, just present, the way breath is present.

The chain had begun with a woman who had buried her daughter and pressed her palms flat into the earth and said a name. That was where it started. Not with a decision to preserve something, not with a plan — with grief, and with the thing grief does when it has nowhere else to go. It makes a mark. It puts something in the ground. It says a name so the name does not disappear.

Nora had buried her daughter in a different way. Not in the earth. In silence. In the closed room she did not open, in the name she did not say. And the silence had lasted thirty-five years and it had done what long silences do: it had become indistinguishable from the walls of the house, from the structure of the days, from the person she had become inside it.

And then Tayak had called her from outside the commission building, and Nora had heard the steadiness in her voice — not calm, not easy, but held, the way you hold something when you have decided you will not drop it — and Nora had gone into the hearing room the next morning and said the name she had been carrying for sixty-two years, the first name, the old name, the name that had traveled two thousand years to arrive in a trench near Mattawoman Creek, and it had sounded larger in the room than it had ever sounded inside her.

She had not said her daughter's name. She had not been asked to.

But the figure at the base of the mound was looking out.

She set the needle down on the cutting mat. She smoothed the panel with her palms — the mound, the light, the figure at the rim — and she sat with it for a long time without moving. The ceiling light was steady above her. The sewing machine sat dark and quiet to her left, its LED indicator off, the bobbin thread still threaded through from the last seam. The clock on the wall read twenty-eight minutes past midnight.

She was not tired.

She was thinking that the figure at the base of the mound did not need a face she could name tonight. It needed only to be there, looking out, the way the woman at the bottom of the chain had been there — not asking to be found, not waiting to be found, only present, the way things placed in the earth are present: patient, below everything, holding their position while the world above them changed and changed and changed and did not stop.

She could give it a face later. She had time. Tayak would be back at the site in the morning, and Kimi would be working on the archive, and Marcus would be doing whatever Marcus was doing now that the commission had ruled, and the mound would be there, protected, the light coming out of it in the way she had stitched it — not flooding out, not bursting, just rising, the way light rises when something that has been covered for a very long time is finally, carefully, opened.

She folded the panel again. She put it back on the shelf.

She turned off the ceiling light and stood in the doorway in the dark, and she thought that tomorrow she would call Tayak. Not about the panel. Just to hear her voice. Just to say that she had watched the news and seen her face and seen the quilt on the commission table under the institutional lights, the soft fabric against that pale birch veneer, and that she had thought: *there it is. There it finally is.* And she had not been surprised, and she had not been afraid, and she had been, for the first time in a very long time, something she did not have a precise word for — not proud, not relieved, not at peace, but something that lived near all three of those things without being any of them exactly.

She would call in the morning. She would not say any of that. She would just call, and Tayak would pick up, and that would be enough.

She went to bed. She lay in the dark with her hands flat on the blanket, on the wool throw with the Selbu stars that she had had for twenty years, and she looked at the ceiling, and after a while she slept.

The panel stayed on the shelf in the sewing room, folded, the mound inside it, the light inside the mound, the figure at the rim with its small dark hands slightly raised. And at the base of the opening, the space where the second figure would go — not yet stitched, not yet faced, not yet named. Present the way an intention is present. Waiting, the way things wait when the person who will do the necessary work has finally, after a very long time, decided to begin.
