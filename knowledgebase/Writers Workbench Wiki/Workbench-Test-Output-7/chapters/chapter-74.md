The wooden figure lay across both her palms. Tayak held it the way she had learned to hold anything irreplaceable: level, still, her elbows braced against her thighs so her hands could not shake.

It was small, shorter than her hand was wide, and carved from something dense that had not rotted. The grain ran lengthwise, pale where the earth had leached it, darker at the joints of the limbs. Whoever had cut it had known what they were doing. The lines were not decorative. They were deliberate. A human figure with one arm raised, and along the raised arm, worked into the surface in a series of notched cuts, a sequence she had not seen before in this context. Not clan marks. Not tally marks. Something else.

She had been describing it for four minutes.

"The arm," she said again. "The raised one. There are seven cuts along the forearm, then a gap, then three more. The gap is intentional. The spacing is too even to be accidental. And at the shoulder joint, where the arm meets the body, there is a deeper cut. Not structural." She paused. "Nora, it reads like a name."

The line was open. She could hear the faint carrier hiss that meant the call had not dropped. She could hear, or thought she could hear, Nora breathing.

She had driven to the site at four in the morning. The trench was cold in the way that deep earth is cold in October, not the cold of air but the cold of kept time, the cold of something sealed away from seasons. She had come down the ladder alone, Wren still at the surface managing the light rig, and she had brushed the last centimeter of fill from the figure with a boar-bristle brush so soft the work had taken twenty minutes. She had not called Nora until the figure was fully uncovered, lying in the floor of the chamber in the position it had been placed, one arm raised.

She had been waiting eleven months for this.

Her hope was simple and she did not let herself say it plainly even in her own mind: that Nora would hear the description and say yes, the way a door opens when you have finally found the right key. That the figure would match something in the chain of names Nora had been keeping for sixty-two years. That the ground and the story would meet.

Her fear was the other thing Nora had done before, the thing she did when Tayak pushed too fast or asked the wrong question from too far away. *I can't speak of that yet. Not on the phone. Come and sit with me and I'll tell you properly.* She had said it three times in the past year. Each time Tayak had driven to Billingsley Road and sat at Nora's kitchen table and waited, and each time Nora had told her something true but not the thing, not the center of it, and Tayak had driven back to College Park with the feeling of a door that had opened six inches and then held.

She was not going to let that happen today.

"The notation on the arm," she said. "Seven cuts, gap, three. I've seen something similar on a gorget from the Patuxent lowlands, Marant's site, not mine, but that was clan identification. This is different. The gap is load-bearing. It's doing work." She paused. "Nora. I need you to tell me if you know what this is."

The hiss on the line.

Then: "Say the cuts again."

Tayak let out a breath she had not known she was holding. "Seven. A gap of approximately four millimeters. Then three."

"And the deeper cut at the shoulder."

"Yes."

"Single cut or double?"

Tayak tilted the figure toward the work light. The beam was a clip-on LED Wren had rigged to the ladder's second rung, and it threw the chamber into a hard-edged brightness that made everything look both more and less real than it was. She brought the figure close to her face.

"Single," she said. "But wider than the others. Cut with something broader. Different tool or different pressure."

Silence.

Tayak checked the screen with her thumb. The timer read 4:17 and climbing. The signal was two bars, enough.

She looked at the figure in her hands. The raised arm. The seven cuts and the three. The chamber around her was roughly a meter and a half across, the walls dark reddish-brown, subsoil that had not seen light since the deposit was sealed. The smell was the smell of this place at every depth: iron and clay and something older underneath, something she had no word for, only the recognition of it. The way you recognize a voice before you know whose it is.

She had first smelled it at the Patuxent lowlands under Marant, her second field year, kneeling in a cut two meters deep into a flood deposit. Marant had said, without looking up from her own work: *The ground tells you the truth. Institutions tell you what they've decided.* Tayak had written it in her field log that night. She had not known then what it would cost her to keep believing it.

"Nora," she said. "I've been down here since four. I've got," she checked the screen again, "maybe two hours before I need to document this in full light and call Wren down. I need to know if this figure is in the chain."

Another silence. But this one was different. She could feel the difference between a door that is locked and a door that is simply closed.

"Where exactly did you find it," Nora said. Not a question. A positioning.

"Center of the chamber floor. Below the pendant layer. Below everything." She kept her voice level. "It's the deepest object in the mound, Nora. It was placed first. Or near enough to first that the stratigraphy can't separate them."

The hiss.

Then nothing.

Tayak sat with the figure across her palms and did not speak. The LED light hummed. Somewhere above her, through two meters of earth and the timber shoring Wren had rigged at the chamber's mouth, she could hear, or imagined she could hear, the first birds of the morning starting up in the tree line. Carolina wrens, thin-voiced, the ones that stayed when the others went south.

She waited.

The sound came without warning.

Not words. Not her name. A single break in the breath, short and involuntary, the kind of sound a person makes when something hits them before they have time to decide how to receive it. Then silence. Then the sound again, lower, and this time Tayak understood it was not the line.

She did not speak.

She had heard Nora angry, the flat controlled anger of a woman who had learned that raised voices cost you the room. She had heard her tired, the particular tiredness of someone who has been careful for decades and is still being careful. She had heard her careful itself, the way Nora measured out information the way you measure medicine, enough and not more. The Marshalltown trowel was still in her right hand. She became aware of it the way you become aware of something you have been holding so long it stopped registering, the worn wood of the handle, the slight flex of the blade. She did not set it down. Setting it down felt like something that would break something.

Eighty-two years old and alone in the house on Billingsley Road at, she checked the screen with her thumb without moving the phone from her ear, 4:21 in the morning. *She has been standing the whole time.*

It was not the sound of a woman in pain or frightened or failing. It was the sound of a woman arriving somewhere she had been walking toward for a very long time.

She knew what a deliberate fill looked like, how it differed in texture and color from the natural accumulation around it. The figure lay across her palms. One arm raised.

She thought: *something is right.*

She thought about what thirty days meant. She thought about Garrett Slade's bulldozers and the designation letter and the NAGPRA consultation and the field log and the permit and the chain-of-custody form and the photographs she still needed to take and the context number she still needed to assign and the call to Elena Slade and the call to Marcus and the call she had not yet returned and the stack of documents on the seat of her truck and Nora's affidavit in its kitchen drawer and the federal petition and the hearing and the thirty-day clock that had been running since the designation letter arrived and that was running right now, up above her, in the ordinary world where she had a name and a job and a trowel and a field log and a deadline.

She pressed her left hand harder against the earth floor.

*Something is in here,* she had thought, eleven months ago, on the first morning, pressing her hand to the mound's outer face in the cold before dawn.

She had been right. She had been right in a way she had not known how to be right. She had been right the way Yanu was right when she pressed this figure into the earth and spoke the name, not knowing who would come, not knowing when, not knowing whether the name would survive the distance, only knowing that the ground could hold what the living could not, and that holding was its own form of speaking, and that someone, eventually, would come with the right tools and the patience to listen.

She had come.

She had thirty days to make it matter.

She picked up the field log. She uncapped the pencil. Below the ruled line, in the small, precise handwriting she had used for eleven months, she wrote the context number. She wrote the date. She wrote the time. She wrote: *wooden figure, evidence bag 18CH-447-089, arm raised, carved notation, condition stable.* She wrote: *chamber floor, undisturbed clay matrix, cold, consistent with sealed deposit.* She drew the ruled line below the entry, separating observation from inference, the way Marant had taught her.

Below the ruled line, in the inference column, she wrote one word.

She looked at it for a moment.

Then she closed the field log, put the pencil in her breast pocket, and began to gather what she needed to climb out., -

The copper pendant lay in its evidence bag on the shelf above the figure's bag, tagged and logged: *18CH-447-051, recovered 14 September, chain of custody to Adaeze Okonkwo, conservation suite, 15:05.* Tayak had not looked at it since the day it came out of the ground. She looked at it now, before she reached for the ladder.

The pendant was small, a disc the size of her thumb, green-black with oxidation, the cord long since gone to nothing but the suspension hole still clean through. She had written in the field log: *copper alloy, worked, consistent with late woodland period, stratum five, undisturbed matrix.* She had not written what she had felt when the brush cleared the last of the matrix and she saw it lying face-up in the clay, the way something is placed when it is meant to be found.

She had thought then: *someone put this here for a reason.*

She thought now of Waya, who had carried this pendant and whose grief Tayak had not yet found the words for, and of the layers between that woman and Yanu below her, and of the figure in the deepest chamber and the name pressed into the earth above a child who had not yet learned to walk. They were not the same object, but they were the same act. A woman's hands pressing something into the earth above a loss, the way you press a hand flat on a back in the dark, the way you sit down beside someone without a word and let your weight say what words cannot.

She had thirty days.

She put her hand on the ladder and began to climb.
