The prescription bottle was the size of her thumb. She had set it on the table next to the salt, where she could see it without reaching for it, and she had been looking at it for the better part of an hour.

*Digitalis.* The doctor in Waldorf had written the name in careful script on the paper she still kept folded in her apron pocket, though she knew the word by now. Her own father had taken it, in the last years. She remembered the bottle on his windowsill, the same brown glass, the same white cap, and how she had not understood then what it meant — only that he moved more slowly and sat down more often and one morning did not get up. She had been twelve. She understood now.

The kitchen was quiet. The radio on the shelf had been off since eight, when the news finished, and the only sound was the tick of the wood stove settling as the fire burned low and the occasional creak of the house finding its position in the October cold. Through the single window above the sink, the yard was dark. The porch light had gone out in August and she had not asked anyone to fix it.

She got up and put another piece of oak on the fire. Not because she was cold — the kitchen was the warmest room in the house, as it always was, the walls holding the heat of the day's cooking — but because she needed something to do with her hands. The wood caught quickly, dry and splitting along the grain, and for a moment the smell of it sharpened the air, cut through the older smells of the evening: boiled sweet potato, fried onion, the cornbread she had baked on Tuesday and not yet finished. She left the stove door cracked a finger's width and stood watching the flame until her pulse steadied.

The telephone was on the wall beside the door to the front room. Black Bakelite, the receiver heavy and cool. She had put it there herself, five years ago, when the line finally came out this far on the road. Her neighbor had thought it a great extravagance. Ida had said nothing, only paid the man who installed it and learned the number for the exchange in town.

She crossed to it, lifted the receiver, and listened to the tone.

Then she set it back.

She returned to the table and sat down. The prescription bottle caught the light from the pendant above her — a plain glass shade, yellowed at the rim, the bulb too bright for the hour. She had been meaning to get a lower wattage. She had been meaning to do many things.

The doctor had said: *not immediately serious, but you must be careful*. He had said: *rest when you feel it, don't climb stairs too quickly, avoid cold air if you can manage it*. He had not said how long. She had not asked. She was sixty-four years old and had grown up in a house where you did not ask the doctor how long because the answer was always either a lie or a kindness, and she could not decide which was worse.

What she had decided, sitting in his office with her coat still buttoned because she had not wanted to stay long, was that she would not tell her son, and she would not tell her daughter-in-law, and she would not tell anyone who would immediately begin to manage her. What she would do first — before anything else, before she let herself think about the spring or the summer or what came after — was call Nora.

Nora was twenty years old. She was studying to be a teacher at the college in Baltimore, which Ida approved of, not because teaching was safe but because it required you to know things precisely enough to say them to someone who knew nothing. Nora had a jaw that made her look stubborn even when she wasn't, and she asked questions the way some people breathe — steadily, without thinking to stop. She would listen. She would not immediately begin to cry or to telephone the doctor herself or to suggest that Ida come and stay with someone.

Or she might. Ida did not know, entirely, what Nora would do. That was part of it.

She picked up her glass of water and drank. The water tasted of iron, as it always did in October when the ground was cold and the well ran slow. She had drunk iron-tasting water her whole life and had never minded it. She set the glass down and looked at the bottle again.

The story she needed to tell Nora was not short. She had known this for years — had known it the way you know a piece of work is waiting for you, a fence that needs mending or a hem that needs taking up, something you keep passing and not stopping for because the day is always too full and the light is always going. She had been waiting for Nora to be old enough. Then she had been waiting for the right moment. Then she had been waiting without a reason, which was the worst kind of waiting, because it had no end.

Her own grandmother had told her when Ida was nineteen. It had been winter then too, a different kitchen, a different house, the smell of woodsmoke and dried herbs and the good heavy smell of a fire that had been burning all day. Her grandmother had sat across the table with her hands flat on the wood and said: *I need to tell you something before I lose the words for it*. And Ida had listened. She had listened until the candle burned to a stub and the fire went to coals, and when her grandmother finished she had sat for a long time without speaking, and then she had said: *Tell me the beginning again*. And her grandmother had.

That grandmother had been seventy-one. She had lived another four years and died in her sleep in March, which was the right time to die if you had to, when the worst of the winter was over and the light was coming back.

Ida was sixty-four. The doctor had said *not immediately*, but she had heard the pause before the word, and she knew what lived in pauses.

She got up again. This time she went to the shelf and took down the coffee pot — aluminum, dented at the base from a fall years ago — and set it on the electric ring, because the old habit of coffee at any hour was stronger than the new instruction to limit it. She would have one cup. She would drink it slowly. And then she would pick up the telephone and call Nora's dormitory and ask the girl at the desk to fetch her, and she would wait, and when Nora came on the line she would say: *I need you to come this weekend. I have something to tell you and it cannot wait*.

She would not say: *I have a prescription bottle on my kitchen table and I am afraid I will run out of time before I run out of words*.

She would not say that.

The coffee began to smell. She stood with her back to the stove, facing the window, and in the dark glass she could see the kitchen behind her — the pale walls, the shelf of enamel cups, the salt and the bottle side by side on the scrubbed pine, the pendant light making a small bright circle on the table that left the corners in shadow. She looked like her own grandmother, she thought. The same set of the shoulders. The same way of standing as if the floor might require holding down.

She poured the coffee. She carried it to the table and sat, and she curled her hands around the cup, and she looked at the telephone on the wall.

She was afraid of two things, and she knew them both clearly enough to name them. The first was that she would frighten Nora — that she would say *come this weekend* and Nora would hear the thing underneath the words and would arrive already braced for a funeral, and then the telling would happen in the wrong key, too solemn, too weighted, and Nora would receive it as an inheritance of grief rather than what it was, which was a living thing, a story that had been passed hand to hand for two hundred years and had not died yet.

The second fear was worse. It was that she would wait too long. That she would put off the call for another week, and then another, and that one morning she would sit down at this table and find that the words had gone — not because she had forgotten them, but because the breath to carry them had simply stopped. And then the story would end here, in this kitchen, with the iron taste of well water and the smell of oak smoke, and no one would know what they had lost because they would not know it had existed.

She put her hand on the telephone receiver without lifting it.

*I got this from my grandmother*, she would tell Nora. *Who got it from hers. You are the next one. Don't wait as long as I did.*

She lifted the receiver. She listened to the tone. She asked the operator for the dormitory exchange in Baltimore.

The bus from Baltimore came in at half past eight, and Ida heard it from the kitchen — the low diesel groan slowing on the road below the hill, then silence. She had been sitting for twenty minutes with a second cup of coffee she had not meant to pour, watching the window. The glass showed nothing but black and her own reflection, pale and upright in the pendant light.

She heard Nora's boots on the path before she heard the knock. The girl had always walked that way, heel-first, like someone who expected the ground to be solid and was usually right.

"It's open," Ida said.

The door swung in and brought the cold with it — a hard, clean cold that smelled of turned earth and fallen leaves — and Nora came through it in a wool coat that was not hers, dark green, too wide across the shoulders, the buttons mismatched where someone had replaced them in a hurry. Her face was bright from the walk up the hill. She was twenty years old and looked it: cheeks wind-burned, hair pulled back in a knot that was coming loose, a small canvas bag over one shoulder.

"You didn't say what the errand was," Nora said, by way of greeting. She was already looking at the stove, the coffee pot, the cups. Not suspicious. Just taking inventory, the way she always did when she came into a room — a habit Ida had noticed since the girl was small.

"Sit down first."

Nora dropped her bag by the door and unwound a scarf from her neck — dark red, the kind they sold at the student union. She draped it over the hook and looked around the kitchen with the easy, unselfconscious attention of someone returning to a place they know by smell as much as by sight.

Then she saw the prescription bottle.

It was sitting where Ida had left it, beside the salt, in the circle of light from the pendant lamp. Brown glass, white cap, the pharmacy label with its small typed text. Nora's eyes went to it and stayed there for a moment — just a moment — and then she looked at Ida.

Ida said nothing.

Nora pulled out the chair across the table and sat. She set her hands flat on the pine, the way people do when they are deciding not to reach for something.

"Do you want me to pour?" she asked.

"I'll do it."

Ida got up and brought the aluminum pot to the table. She poured for Nora and then topped up her own cup, though it did not need it, and set the pot down between them. The kitchen was warm. The wood stove in the corner had been burning since mid-afternoon and the oak had gone to a deep, even heat that pressed against the skin like a hand. Outside, the wind had dropped. The single-pane window above the sink had a rime of condensation along its lower edge, and beyond it there was nothing — no light from the road, no moon.

Nora wrapped both hands around her cup. She was waiting.

"I'm not dying," Ida said. "Not this week."

Nora looked at the prescription bottle again. "But."

"But I have a condition. The doctor in Waldorf told me in September. It is not immediately serious." She heard herself use the word the doctor had used and felt the same thing she had felt in his office: the pause that the word was built around, the space inside *immediately* where the real meaning lived. "I have been careful. I limit the coffee."

Nora's mouth moved. She did not say what she was going to say.

"I called you," Ida said, "because I have something to tell you and I have been putting it off for three years, and I cannot put it off anymore." She sat down. The rag rug under her feet — worn to threads along the path between stove and table — shifted slightly as she settled her weight. "It is not about my health. My health is beside the point. I want you to understand that before I begin."

"All right," Nora said. Her voice was careful. She was doing what she did with music — Ida had heard her practice, had sat in the next room while the girl worked through a piece over and over, not drilling the notes but listening for the shape of it, the way one phrase leaned into the next.

"I need you to listen the way you listen to music," Ida said. "Not to remember the words. To remember the shape."

Nora looked at her across the table. The pendant light made a sharp line across her cheekbones and left her eyes in shadow, so that she looked older than twenty, or perhaps just very still.

"Tell me," Nora said.

Ida put her hands flat on the pine. The wood was smooth where generations of bread-making had worn it, rough at the edges where it had been scrubbed hard and the grain had lifted. She had sat at this table for forty years. She had made bread here, written letters here, wept here once after a name came back from a list that should not have been on it. She knew this table the way she knew her own hands.

She said: "Your great-grandmother's grandmother was Matta's granddaughter. And before her there was Tessa. And before Tessa there was a woman named Saya, who walked two days to put a piece of paper in front of men who would not read it."

Nora did not move.

The wood stove ticked once in the corner. The coffee steamed between them, a thin thread rising and curling and gone.

"These are not stories I made up," Ida said. "I got this from my grandmother, who got it from hers. Each one told the next one when the time came. My grandmother told me when I was nineteen. She sat where I am sitting now — different kitchen, different house, but the same table between us, more or less — and she said: *I need to tell you something before I lose the words for it*." Ida paused. "I am saying the same thing to you now. I have the words. I want to give them to you while I still have the breath to carry them."

Nora had gone very still in the way she went still when something mattered — not frozen, but gathered, the way water gathers before it moves. Her hands were still flat on the table. She had not touched her coffee.

"How far back does it go?" she asked.

"Further than I can name. Saya is the earliest one I can give you a story for. But she knew the ones before her, and they knew the ones before them, and the line goes back to the mound. To the ground itself." Ida looked at her. "You know the mound I mean."

A small change in Nora's face. Not surprise. Something older than surprise.

"I've walked past that mound my whole life," she said quietly.

"I know." Ida picked up her coffee cup and held it without drinking. "We all have. That is the point."

The coffee had gone dark and still in Nora's cup. She had not touched it.

Ida set her own cup down and folded her hands on the pine. There was a crack in the wood near her left thumb, old and dark with years of flour and water, and she had run her finger along it ten thousand times without thinking. She thought about it now. She was deciding where to begin.

"Matta," she said. "Start with Matta."

Nora waited.

"He lived in the middle time — not Saya's time, not our time. He was a pipe-maker. He had a workshop and he kept two sets of accounts." Ida looked at the crack in the table. "One set was for the English. What he sold, what he owed, what they thought he was. A craftsman. Useful. Safe." She paused. "The other set was for the people who came to him at night."

"What did he keep for them?"

"Names. Debts. Who was owed what and by whom. Who had land and who had lost it and where the old boundaries ran." Ida's voice was level, the way a schoolteacher's voice is level when the lesson is the kind that cannot afford to waver. "He was the one who remembered. That was his work — not the pipes. The pipes were the cover for the work."

Nora's hands moved slightly on the table. Not reaching for her cup. Just shifting.

"He had a granddaughter," Ida said. "My grandmother called her Matta's granddaughter — she did not know her name, or the name had been lost by then. But she knew the girl had been told everything Matta knew. He sat her down the way I am sitting you down now, and he gave her the names."

"How old was she?"

"Young. My grandmother thought young — younger than you." Ida looked at Nora. "He waited too long, my grandmother said. He was sick before he told her, and he had to speak fast, and some things were lost in the speed of it. She got most of it. Not all."

Nora absorbed this. The wood stove shifted, a small settling sound, and the heat in the room pressed closer for a moment and then steadied.

"Before Matta," Ida said, "there was a woman named Tessa."

She said the name the way she had been taught to say it — not quickly, not as a stepping-stone to the next thing, but as a thing that had its own weight and needed a moment of air around it.

"Tessa wore a shell gorget." Ida touched her own collarbone, briefly. "Here. A carved piece of shell on a cord. She wore it every day. My grandmother said it was the kind of thing a person wears so they do not forget who they are."

"What happened to her?"

"She had a brother. They argued about what it meant to survive." Ida's mouth made a small, dry line. "He thought survival meant becoming something the English could use. She thought survival meant becoming something they could not see." She looked at Nora. "They were both right. They never agreed. She outlived him."

"And the gorget?"

"It went into the ground. Into the mound." Ida said it simply, as though it were obvious, and perhaps it was. "That is what the mound is. It is not one thing. It is not one person. It is everything they could not carry anymore, put somewhere that would hold it."

Nora looked down at the table. The pendant light above them was the only light now — the candle on the shelf had burned to a stub an hour ago and neither of them had replaced it. The light made a hard circle on the pine and left the edges of the room in a gray softness that was almost like dusk.

"Tell me about Saya," Nora said.

Ida had been waiting for this. She had been moving toward it since she sat down.

"Saya could read and write English," she said. "Her father taught her. He had worked for a Jesuit — copying documents, my grandmother had said, though the detail had grown thin with years — and he taught Saya the language the way you teach a child to use a tool that might save their life." She spread her hands on the table. "She wrote a petition. A legal document, asking the colonial assembly to recognize the community's right to their land. She walked two days to put it in front of them."

"And they refused."

"They received it," Ida said. "That is different. They took the paper and said the matter would be considered. They did not refuse. They simply did nothing, which is a different kind of refusal — one that cannot be argued with, because there is no moment of no to point to. There is only the waiting, and then more waiting, and then the understanding that the waiting is the answer."

Nora was very still.

"When the petition failed," Ida said, "Saya took the bead — her mother's bead, a blue glass bead from a trading party, the kind the English brought — and she buried it in the mound. With the petition. Wrapped in oilskin." Ida paused. "She put it in the ground because the ground was the only thing left that would keep it."

The wind had come back up outside. It moved through the pines at the edge of the yard with a low, continuous sound, not violent, just steady — the sound of something that does not need to hurry because it has all the time there is.

"The mound," Ida said. "I need you to know where it is. Not approximately. Exactly."

She described it. She used the names her grandmother had used — the field names, the road, the rise of the ground, the way the mound sat slightly higher than the surrounding land and how you could see it from the track if you knew to look. She had walked past it herself, twice, on visits to her grandmother as a girl, and her grandmother had pointed without pointing — a slight turn of the head, a pause in the conversation — and said *that is the place* without saying it in words.

Nora listened with her whole body.

When Ida finished, Nora said: "How do you know all of this? How does it stay — how does it not get lost, or changed, or —"

"It does get lost," Ida said. "It does get changed." She said it without apology, because there was no use in pretending otherwise. "My grandmother's grandmother lost some of what she had been given. My grandmother lost some of what she had been given. I have probably lost things I don't know I've lost." She looked at Nora steadily. "That is why you cannot wait. Every time someone waits, something goes."

She stopped. She could feel her heart — not painfully, just present, the way you feel a door you know is there without touching it.

"The names before Saya," Nora said. "You said the line goes back further. What do you know about them?"

Ida shook her head. Not impatiently. Carefully, as though she were setting something down that she had carried a long way.

"I have names," she said. "A few. But I cannot give them to you as stories. I cannot give you the shape of their lives. They are just names to me, and a name without a story is almost nothing — it is a door with no house behind it." She looked at her hands. "My grandmother said those go further back than the words can reach. She said the ones before Saya are in the mound itself, not in the telling. The ground holds what the words have lost."

She believed this. She had believed it since she was nineteen years old and her grandmother had said it to her at a table not unlike this one, in a kitchen that smelled of coffee and woodsmoke and something older underneath, something that had seeped into the wood over generations. She had believed it and she had not questioned it and she was not questioning it now.

What she did not say — what it did not occur to her to say, because it had not occurred to her grandmother to say it, and had not occurred to the grandmother before that — was that the mound holding those names was not a metaphor. That the layers of the ground were not poetry. That the things put into the earth at different depths, at different times, by different hands, were themselves a record — not a spiritual one, but a physical one, the kind that could be read by someone who knew how to read it.

She did not know this. No one had told her. It had been true for two thousand years and no one in the chain had known to say it.

She said: "The words go back to Saya. The mound goes back further. That is all I can tell you."

Nora looked at her for a long moment. Then she nodded — not the nod of someone who is satisfied, but the nod of someone who is filing something carefully, putting it where it can be found later.

"I got this from my grandmother," Ida said, "who got it from hers. Each one waited too long. I waited too long." She looked at Nora across the pine table, across the worn path of the wood, across the forty years of bread and letters and one night of grief and now this. "You are the next one. Don't wait as long as I did."

The wind moved through the pines. The wood stove breathed its slow heat into the room. The coffee between them had gone entirely cold, and neither of them moved to warm it.

"How will I know," Nora said, "when it's time?"

Ida thought about this. She thought about it the way she thought about difficult problems her students brought her — not rushing, not performing consideration, but actually sitting with the question until it showed her its shape.

"You'll know," she said, "when you find someone who is ready to do something with it. Not just to hold it. To use it." She paused. "My grandmother waited until she found me. I waited until I found you. The right person is not the youngest one, or the closest one. It is the one who will carry it forward." She looked at Nora. "You are going to have to find that person yourself."

Nora said nothing. Outside, the first gray edge of something that was not yet light had begun to show at the bottom of the window — not dawn, not even close, but the faint, preliminary withdrawal of the deepest dark, the hour before the hour before the hour.

They had been talking all night.

The cold had settled into the kitchen the way cold does when no one has fed the stove in an hour — not sharp, but present, pressing through the wool of Ida's cardigan at the shoulders. She did not get up to add wood. The fire could wait.

Nora had been sitting with both hands flat on the table, palms down, the way she sometimes sat when she was thinking hard and did not want her hands to betray it. She was looking at the window, at that thin gray withdrawal at the bottom of the glass, and then she turned and looked at Ida and said:

"Why didn't you tell my mother?"

Ida went still.

It was not a flinching kind of still. It was the kind that comes when a question you have been expecting for a long time finally arrives and you find you are not as ready as you thought. She sat with it for a moment. The wood stove made a small sound, a settling of ash.

"I told your mother," she said.

Nora's hands moved. Just slightly. The fingers drew in a little, as though something had touched them.

"She was seventeen," Ida said. "She sat where you are sitting. She listened the way you listen to something you think will always be there." She looked at the table, at the worn path in the pine where generations of hands had crossed and recrossed. "The way you listen to rain. You hear it. You don't hold it."

The silence between them held everything Nora knew about her mother, which was not the same as everything there was to know, and both of them understood this without saying it.

Nora did not look away from Ida's face.

Ida felt her heart — not painfully, just there, the way it had been there all night, a door she was aware of without touching. She had not told the doctor about the nights when it felt heavier than it should. She had not told anyone. There was no useful reason to tell them.

"She was not the wrong person," Ida said. "I want you to understand that. She was not — I didn't misjudge her. She was seventeen and she had her mother's quickness and her father's stubbornness and she would have been —" She stopped. She was not going to do this. She was not going to perform grief for Nora's benefit when Nora was carrying her own. "She was not ready," she said. "And I was not ready to push her. And then she grew busy, and I had waited, and here we are."

She reached across the table and put her hand over Nora's.

Nora's hand was cold. Ida's was older and dryer and had made bread in this kitchen for thirty years and had held students' examination papers and had held her husband's hand at the end and it covered Nora's completely.

"The mound is still there," Ida said. "The bead is still there. Saya put it there so someone would find it. You understand that? She put it in the ground because the ground keeps things. Because she knew the words might not last but the ground would." She pressed Nora's hand lightly. "You don't have to find it. That is not your work. Your work is to make sure the person who does find it — knows what they're holding."

Nora looked at her.

"How will they know," she said, "if I haven't found it?"

"Because you'll have told them," Ida said. "The same way I'm telling you. The same way my grandmother told me. You find the right person and you tell them everything — the names, the story, the mound, Saya, all of it — and when they reach into the ground and their hand closes on something, they will know. They will know it the way you know the name of someone you have been told about your whole life and then suddenly meet in a room."

Nora was quiet for a moment. The gray at the bottom of the window had not changed. The dark was still the dark.

"I don't know if I have it all," she said. "What you told me tonight — I don't know if I'll remember it right."

"You won't," Ida said. "Not all of it. You'll remember the shape of it. You'll remember the names that matter. The rest —" She lifted her hand from Nora's and set it back in her own lap. "The rest will be yours. That's how it works. Each person adds what they have and loses what they lose and the thing still moves forward. It doesn't move forward perfectly. It moves forward."

She believed this. She had believed it since she was nineteen and her grandmother had said something like it to her in a kitchen that smelled of woodsmoke and old coffee, and she had written nothing down because there was nothing to write, because the writing was not the point, because the point was the passing, the act of one person sitting across from another and saying *here, take this, don't let it stop with you.*

Nora said: "What if I can't find the right person? What if I wait and wait and —"

"Then you tell the wrong person," Ida said. "You tell whoever is there. A wrong telling is better than no telling." She said it firmly, the way she had said things to students who were using precision as an excuse for paralysis. "But you will find the right person. I believe that. I believe it the way I believe the mound is still there — not because I can see it, but because I know the ground."

Nora looked at her for a long moment. Her eyes were the eyes of someone who has been awake all night and has moved past tiredness into something cleaner, some state on the other side of tiredness where things become very clear and very simple. She was twenty years old. She did not fully understand what she had been given tonight. Ida could see this. It did not worry her. Understanding was not the work of a single night. Understanding was the work of sixty years of carrying something and occasionally setting it down in the light and looking at it and picking it up again.

Nora nodded.

It was not the nod of someone who was satisfied. It was the nod of someone who was saying: *I have it. I don't know yet what I have. But I have it.*

Ida had been meaning to write it down for years. Not the names — the names were not for paper, her grandmother had been clear about that, the names were a thing the mouth held, not the hand — but the rest of it. The shape of it. The way a person might write down the plan of a house they had lived in for forty years, not because they might forget it but because it seemed wrong to leave no record at all.

She had not done it. She had thought about it and then put it aside and then thought about it again and then thought: *after the school term ends*, and then: *after the winter*, and then the doctor in Waldorf had said *not immediately serious* and she had understood that after the winter was not a guarantee.

She got out the typewriter the morning after Nora left. It was a Royal portable, black, the letter *e* slightly elevated on its arm from a bent striker she had never had repaired. She set it on the kitchen table and put in a clean sheet of paper and sat for a long time looking at the blank page.

She was not going to write the names. She was going to write what the names meant. The shape of the story. Saya and the petition. Tessa and the gorget. Matta and his two sets of accounts. The mound and what was in it. The chain of women who had carried the story forward, each one losing a little and adding a little, the way a river loses water to evaporation and gains it back from rain.

She would write it so that if something happened to her before she could speak to Nora again — before she could say the things that needed saying in person, at the mound, with the ground under their feet — Nora would have something. Not the whole thing. Not the names. But the shape. The context. The reason it mattered.

She began to type.

The letter *e* rode slightly high throughout. She did not stop to correct it. She typed for three hours, stopping only once to put more wood on the fire and once to make fresh coffee. When she finished she had six pages, single-spaced, the margins narrow because she had not wanted to waste the paper.

She read it through once. It was not perfect. Some of the names she had given without their stories, because the stories had thinned over the generations and she did not have enough to give. Some of the dates she had guessed at. The section about Saya's petition was the strongest — her grandmother had told that part with the most detail, had said it as though she had been in the room, as though she could still see the ink drying on Saya's palm.

She folded the pages in thirds and put them in a manila envelope and wrote Nora's name on the front.

She would give it to her in person. She would say: *This is not the story. This is the record of the story. Keep it with the story. The story lives in your mouth. This is just in case.*

She put the envelope in the drawer of the kitchen table, under the spare candles and the box of matches and the folded piece of paper with the doctor's careful handwriting on it.

Then she covered the typewriter with its cloth and went to make supper.

---

Nora came on Thursday, as she had said she would.

She came in the afternoon, before the light went, and Ida had the coffee ready and the cornbread from the morning still warm under a cloth. They sat at the kitchen table and Ida gave her the envelope and watched her open it.

Nora read it slowly. She read the way she listened to music — not for the words but for the shape, her eyes moving steadily down each page, turning them without hurry. When she finished she set the pages face-down on the table and put her hands flat on top of them.

"You typed this," she said.

"Tuesday morning."

Nora looked at the typewriter on its shelf. The cloth cover was back on it. She looked at it for a moment and then looked at Ida.

"The *e* rides high," she said.

"Always has."

Nora looked down at the pages under her hands. She was quiet for a moment. The wood stove made its small sound. Outside, the afternoon light was going flat and gray, the kind of light that comes before the early dark of November, and through the window the yard had the particular stillness of ground that has been cold for weeks and has settled into it.

"I'll keep this," Nora said. "With everything else."

"Yes," Ida said.

Nora looked at her. "You should have the doctor look at you again."

"I have an appointment in December."

"Before December."

Ida looked at her. Nora's jaw was set in the way it set when she had made up her mind about something and was waiting for the other person to arrive at the same conclusion. She had had that jaw since she was small. It was, Ida thought, the right jaw for the work ahead.

"I'll see about it," Ida said.

This was not a promise. Nora knew it was not a promise. She also knew it was the most she was going to get, and she took it.

She folded the pages back into the envelope and put it in her canvas bag, in the inside pocket with the zipper. She pressed the zipper closed with two fingers, checking it. Then she looked up.

"Tell me the beginning again," she said.

Ida looked at her across the table. The pendant light above them made its small bright circle on the scarred pine. The coffee steamed. The wood stove held its heat. Outside, the pines moved in the late-afternoon wind, that low continuous sound that was not quite a sound but more like the feeling of sound, the presence of something very large and very patient moving through the world.

She put her hands flat on the pine.

"Saya," she said. "Start with Saya."

---

The gray at the bottom of the window had become something else — not light yet, but the memory of light, the way a room looks when the darkness has begun to doubt itself. Nora was still at the table. Her hands were wrapped around the cold cup the way a person holds something they have forgotten they are holding.

"Are you going to be all right?" Nora said.

Ida looked at her. The question was the question of a twenty-year-old, which meant it was asking several things at once and knew it was asking only one of them.

"I'm going to sleep," Ida said.

Nora waited a moment, as though the rest of the answer might follow. It did not. She pushed back from the table — the chair legs catching on the worn ridge of the rug where thirty years of feet had compressed the rags to almost nothing — and she stood and put on her coat. The dark green wool coat, the one with the mismatched buttons. Ida watched her button it from the bottom up, the way young women do when they are thinking about something else.

At the door Nora turned.

She looked as though she wanted to say something that would be adequate to the night. There was no such thing. Ida knew this. After a moment Nora seemed to know it too.

"I'll come Thursday," she said.

"Good," Ida said.

The door opened. The cold came in — sharp, with pine in it, and the particular mineral smell of ground that has been cold for weeks and has given up nothing since. Then the door closed and Nora was gone, her footsteps quick on the wooden steps, then crunching on the frost-stiffened path, then nothing.

Ida stood at the window.

She could see Nora moving down the road — the dark green coat, the quick purposeful walk of someone who is cold and also not yet tired and also carrying something new and heavy and does not yet know how heavy it is. She watched until the green coat went around the bend where the pines closed in on both sides of the road and then there was only the road and the trees and the early gray that was, now, genuinely early gray and not merely the dark pretending.

She stood there a moment longer. The window glass was cold against her breath.

Then she turned back to the kitchen.

The prescription bottle was on the table where she had left it — brown glass, the white paper label with the doctor's careful handwriting, the name of the medication she had not told her daughter about, had not told anyone about, because there was nothing to tell that would not become a conversation she did not have time for. She had time for one conversation. She had had it.

She picked up the bottle. It was lighter than it looked. She carried it to the cabinet above the flour tin — the high shelf where she kept the things she used rarely, the extra salt packets, an old tin that now held flour, a jar of blackberry jam she had put up in August and was saving for no particular occasion. She set the prescription bottle behind the flour tin, where the label faced the wall. Someone would find it eventually. After. They would not know what to make of it and then they would understand and by then it would not matter, because the thing that needed to be done before that would already be done.

She closed the cabinet.

She stood for a moment with her hand on the cabinet door, not pushing it open again, just resting her palm against the painted wood. The wood was cool. The kitchen was warm — the wood stove had held its heat through the night, the way a good stove does, the way old things do when they have been built for exactly this purpose and nothing else.

She went back to the table.

She sat down in her chair — her chair, the one with the slightly shorter left leg that she had shimmed with a folded piece of cardboard twenty years ago and never replaced because the cardboard worked and there was no reason to do a thing twice when once was enough. She folded her hands on the table. The pine was scarred under her palms, knife marks and bread marks and the ring from the coffee pot that had been there since before her daughter was born.

The light at the window was real light now. Not much of it. The pale, provisional light of a December morning that is not committing to anything yet, that is simply making itself available. It came through the single pane and lay across the table in a thin stripe and touched the two cold cups and the edge of the flour tin's shelf and the worn place on the rug where the colors had gone to gray.

Ida looked at the window.

She thought about Nora walking down the road in the dark green coat. She thought about the names — Saya, Matta, Tessa, the others she had carried for forty years, the ones she had almost not said aloud tonight, the ones she had said. She thought about her own grandmother in the kitchen that smelled of woodsmoke and old coffee, saying *here, take this.* She thought about the mound in the ground in Charles County, Maryland, which she had walked past twice as a girl and would not see again, which was there nonetheless, which had been there for longer than anyone in her family had been alive, which would be there after.

She said one name aloud.

"Saya."

She said it quietly, to no one. To the kitchen. To the early light on the table. To the cold cups and the scarred pine and the worn rug and the stove that was still warm. It was not a prayer exactly. It was something older than prayer — the act of saying a name in a room so that the name exists in the air of the room, so that the room has held it, so that it is not only inside one person anymore.

She had been holding it for forty-five years. Since she was nineteen and her grandmother had said it in a kitchen not unlike this one. She had held it carefully, the way you hold something fragile, and the holding had been right, the holding had been necessary, but the holding was finished now.

The story was outside her body for the first time since she was nineteen years old.

She felt the difference. It was not relief exactly. It was the feeling of setting down a lamp you have carried for a long time — your arm does not hurt less immediately, but you become aware that it had been hurting.

She folded her hands.

She closed her eyes.

The kitchen held its warmth around her. The light moved slightly as the clouds outside shifted — she could tell this through her closed eyelids, the faint change in the color of the dark behind her eyes. Somewhere outside, the first bird began. A single note, then silence, then the note again, tentative, checking whether morning was actually happening.

Nora had the story now.

Whether Nora would hold it — whether she would carry it for sixty years the way Ida had, or whether she would do what Ida's own daughter had done, which was to receive it and then set it aside in the way a person sets aside a thing they believe they will return to and then does not return to — this Ida did not know. She had seen both. She had watched her daughter take the story and then grow busy with children and difficulty and the years of difficulty after, and when Ida had gone to her daughter at the end and asked, her daughter had said *yes, yes, I know it, I know the names* — but the names she gave were not all the names, and the story she gave was thinner than the story Ida had been given, worn down by the years of not being told.

That was the danger. Not forgetting outright. Thinning.

Ida had not thinned it. She had kept it whole, had said the names to herself in the dark when she could not sleep, had traced the chain — Saya to Saya's daughter to the daughters after, forward through the years to Matta, to Tessa, forward to herself — had traced it the way a person traces a scar to remember where it came from.

Now Nora had it.

Nora was twenty years old and did not yet know what she had. This was all right. Ida had not known either, at nineteen, sitting in that other kitchen with her grandmother's voice going on past midnight. She had thought she understood. She had not understood for years. Understanding came later, came with carrying, came with the weight of the thing accumulating in you over time until one day you set it down in front of someone else and felt your arms remember what it had been like before.

Nora would understand. Or she would not, and she would pass it on anyway, imperfectly, the way all things were passed on, and the shape of it would survive even if the edges changed.

*It doesn't move forward perfectly,* Ida had said. *It moves forward.*

She believed this. She believed it the way she believed in the mound — not because she had seen it but because the ground was the ground and the ground kept what it was given and the keeping was not a miracle, it was simply what the ground did, what it had always done, what it would do long after the people who put things into it were themselves in it.

The bird outside said its note again. Then another bird answered from further off in the pines. The morning was happening whether it had decided to or not.

Ida sat at the table with her hands folded and her eyes closed and the early light coming through the window and the stove warm at her back, and she was very still, and the kitchen was very still around her, and the name she had said aloud hung in the air of the room the way a name does when it has been said with intention, fading slowly, the way all sounds fade, but not yet gone.
