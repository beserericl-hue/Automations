The child slept on her side with her knees pulled up and one fist pressed against her cheek, and Yanu sat very still and watched her.

The morning fire had burned down to a low pulse of orange under grey ash. The smoke went straight up in the cold air — no wind yet, just the early quiet before the birds found their voices. Yanu's breath showed in small clouds. Her hands were folded in her lap, the knuckles swollen as they always were in the cold months now, the skin dry and split at the base of her right thumb where she had caught it on the hide-scraper two days before.

She had come out before the child woke. She had not planned to sit here. She had meant to go to the water.

The child's name was Taya.

Yanu had known it for three days. Her son Sena — the eldest, who had his father's way of deciding things without announcing them — had come to her at the end of the evening meal with his wife beside him and said it plainly, the way he said most things: *We have named her Taya.* Not asking. Not explaining. His eyes had held the question of whether Yanu would speak against it, and Yanu had not spoken, and Sena had nodded once and gone back to his fire.

Three days. Yanu had not gone near the child.

The girl shifted in her sleep, the small fist unclenching and reaching for nothing, then curling again. She was wrapped in a length of undyed wool that had once been Sena's wife's, soft from years of use, its edges fraying at one corner where the weave had given way. Her cheeks were flushed from the fire's residual warmth. Her hair was fine and dark, pressed flat on one side from sleeping.

A crow called from somewhere past the ridge. Another answered, further off.

Yanu had been old enough, when Taya died, to understand that grief was not a wound that healed. It was more like a stone you learned to carry — you forgot the weight sometimes, for whole days, and then you shifted your load and there it was again, unchanged. She had carried it for what felt now like most of her life, though she knew that was not true. She had had years before Taya, and she had had years since. The stone was part of her shape now. She did not resent it.

But the name.

She had not heard it spoken aloud since the morning she had pressed her palms to the mound's flank and said it herself, the cold earth under her hands, her breath ragged. After that she had said it only inwardly, in the way you keep a fire going in a closed space — carefully, with attention to the air. She had not thought she would ever hear it come from outside her own mouth again.

And now her son's daughter carried it through the world in her sleeping body, breathing it in and out without knowing.

The child made a small sound — not distress, just the random sounds of sleeping — and her legs straightened briefly and then curled again. Her wool wrapping had slipped from her shoulder. Yanu looked at the exposed shoulder, the skin smooth and very pale in the early light, and felt something move in her chest that she could not name precisely. Not grief. Not quite joy. Something that had no word she knew for it, or if it had one, she had not needed it before.

She thought: *You don't know what you carry.*

The child's eyes were still closed. Her mouth was slightly open.

Yanu looked at her for a long time. The crow called again, closer now, and the child's brow furrowed briefly in sleep and then smoothed. The ash in the fire ring settled with a soft sound. Somewhere behind Yanu's house, one of the boys — Piri, from the sound of it, the slow waking — was dragging his sleeping mat across the threshold.

She should go to the water. She should start the grain. The day had its requirements and they did not wait.

She did not move.

The sun had come up enough now to clear the ridge to the east, and a bar of pale light fell across the patch of ground where the child slept. It reached her face. The child's eyes moved under their lids — some interior weather — and then stilled.

Yanu thought of the mound. She thought of the way the earth had felt under her hands that first morning, the cold of it, the particular smell of cut turf and chalk-pale subsoil, the sound of her own voice saying the name into the ground. She had not known, then, that anyone would ever hear it again. She had not known that the name would travel.

She had not asked for this. She understood that. Her son had made the choice, and it was Sena's right to make it, and Yanu would not take that from him. But she had not asked for it, and there was something in that — something she was still working out — that felt important. The name had not waited for her permission. It had gone back into the world on its own terms, the way water finds its level, and now it was here in this sleeping child's chest, rising and falling with her breath.

The bar of sunlight moved slowly across the child's face. Her cheek flushed warmer where it touched.

Yanu uncurled her hands from her lap. The joints ached as they always did in the cold — a dull, familiar protest. She pressed her palms flat against her own thighs for a moment, feeling the rough weave of her tunic, and then she stood, slowly, the way she stood now, with her weight shifting forward before her knees would agree to straighten.

She stood and looked at the child.

*Taya,* she thought, and did not say aloud.

The child breathed. The crow called a third time, and this time there was no answer. The smoke from the dead fire went straight up into the pale sky and disappeared.

Yanu turned toward the path to the water.

She would come back. She would speak to the child when the child was awake. She did not know yet what she would say, but she would find the words — she always found them, eventually, the way you find a path you have not walked in years: the feet remember before the mind does.

But not yet. Not this morning.

This morning she let the name rest in the sunlight with the child, undisturbed.

Sena brought her at midmorning.

Yanu was at the quern when she heard them — her son's step, which she knew as well as her own, and beneath it the quick irregular patter of small feet that still had not learned to fall quietly. She did not look up at once. She pressed the upper stone through one more turn, feeling the grain give under it, the coarse flour falling warm against her fingers. Then she sat back on her heels and looked.

Sena came around the side of the house with the child on his hip, then set her down when they reached the flat ground near the fire ring. The girl stood for a moment in the way children that age stand — slightly stunned by the transition, weight uncertain — and then found her footing and looked around with the absolute, uncommitted attention of someone for whom everything is still equally interesting.

She was perhaps two winters old. Small, even for that age. She had Sena's brow and her mother's wide-set eyes, and her hair was loose because someone had given up trying to braid it. Her wool wrap was fastened at the shoulder with a plain iron pin, and she had already found something on the ground — a curl of birch bark with the white outer layer half-peeled — and was turning it in both hands with complete absorption.

"She woke early," Sena said. "I thought you might want the morning."

Yanu looked at her son. His face was careful in the way it sometimes was when he had decided something and was waiting to see how it landed.

"Sit," Yanu said. "There's grain left."

"I can't. Kato has the others and he'll lose someone." Sena touched the child's head briefly, a habitual gesture, the way you check a knot you've already checked. "An hour. I'll come back."

He was gone before Yanu could answer, back around the side of the house and away, his step fading into the ordinary noise of the settlement.

Yanu looked at the child.

The child had not looked up. She was still working the piece of bark, turning it in her hands with the focused patience that small children bring to objects that have caught them. Her cheeks were flushed from the walk, and a thin string of dried porridge had made its way to her chin and been ignored there.

"Taya," Yanu said.

The child looked up.

Not because she knew what the name meant. Not because she felt its weight or understood that it had been carried here from somewhere far back, passed through grief and ground and the long chain of mornings between then and now. She looked up because someone had spoken, and she was two years old, and that was enough reason.

Her eyes were direct and dark, and for a moment — just a moment, the length of a breath — Yanu felt the ground shift slightly under her, the way it had felt that first morning at the mound, when she had pressed her palms to the cold earth and the cold earth had pressed back.

Then the child looked down again at her piece of bark.

Yanu set the upper quern stone aside and wiped her hands on her tunic. She crossed the ground between them — four steps, five — and lowered herself onto the flat stone near the fire ring. Her knees complained. She ignored them. She sat and looked at the child at close range, and the child, feeling the nearness, glanced up again with that same uncommitted attention.

"Come here," Yanu said.

The child considered this. She took a step toward Yanu, then stopped, turned to look at something behind her — a crow had landed near the woodpile, black and deliberate — and then, apparently deciding the crow could wait, walked the rest of the way and stood in front of Yanu with the bark still in her fist.

Yanu put her hands under the child's arms and lifted her onto her lap. The girl was heavier than she expected — children always were — and warm through her wool wrap, warm the way small animals are warm, with a heat that seems to come from deeper than the skin. She smelled of porridge and woodsmoke and the particular sweetness of a child's unwashed hair.

She squirmed once, establishing her position, and then settled.

Yanu held her. The child's back was against Yanu's chest, and Yanu could feel her breathing — the small regular rise and fall of it, the ribs moving under her hands. The bark was still being turned, turned, turned.

The crow hopped closer to the woodpile and then thought better of it and lifted away.

Yanu looked out past the fire ring toward the ridge to the east, where the sky was a pale, washed blue, the kind of sky that comes after rain and before more rain, the light thin and clear. She could see the line of the ridge against it. She could not see the mound from here — it was on the far side of the ridge, another half-morning's walk — but she knew the direction of it the way she knew the direction of the river: not by looking, but by something in the body that had learned it long ago and did not need to be reminded.

She said: "You are named for someone who was here before you."

The child turned the bark.

"She was here," Yanu said. "On this ground. She walked this path. She knew this ridge."

The child had found a loose edge on the bark and was peeling it back with her thumbnail, intent.

Yanu did not mind. She had not expected understanding. She was not speaking to the child's understanding. She was speaking to the name itself — to the thing the name carried, which was older than the child and older than Yanu and would, if the name kept traveling, outlast them both.

The pendant was at her chest, as it always was. The trader had made it for her in an afternoon, without being asked, without being told — he had seen the empty cord at her throat and understood something about absence that did not require words between them. The copper had warmed to her skin over the years until she rarely felt it anymore — it was part of her body's weight, like the stone she carried for Taya, like the ache in her knuckles. She wore it because the trader had made it and she had nothing else from that time that she could hold.

The child reached for it.

Both hands, without warning, the bark dropped and forgotten. Her fingers closed on the pendant with the absolute certainty of someone who has seen a thing and decided it belongs to them now.

Yanu let her.

The child pulled the pendant toward her mouth — Yanu moved it slightly aside, gently, without taking it back — and then simply held it, both hands wrapped around the copper disc, feeling its edges with her thumbs. Her brow furrowed slightly, the way it does when a thing is more interesting than expected.

Yanu looked at the small hands wrapped around the pendant. The knuckles were dimpled, the fingers short and very clean at the tips where the skin was new. She thought of the trader's hands, the quick sure movements, the bone rod and the punch tool. She thought of Taya's hands, which she had not let herself think about in some time. She thought of her own hands pressing the cold earth of the mound on that first morning, the pale chalk subsoil under the dark turf, the smell of it — damp and metallic and particular, a smell she had never found anywhere else.

She said: "She is still here. She is in the earth."

The child looked up at her. The pendant was still in both hands. Her expression was the expression of a child who has heard a sound without knowing its source — a brief, open attention, undefended.

Yanu looked back at her.

The child's eyes moved to the pendant again. She turned it once, twice, then released it and reached instead for the leather cord it hung from, pulling it experimentally toward her own face.

"No," Yanu said, mildly, and moved the cord out of reach.

The child accepted this without protest. She looked around, remembered the bark, spotted it on the ground, and immediately wanted down.

She squirmed. She pushed against Yanu's arm with both hands. She made the sound that meant *now*, not distress but pure intention.

Yanu set her down.

The child dropped to her hands and knees, retrieved the bark, stood, and walked away — not running yet, but with the purposeful tilt of someone who has somewhere to be, heading toward the woodpile and whatever had caught her eye beyond it. Her wrap trailed slightly at the hem. She did not look back.

Yanu watched her go.

The pendant had swung back against her chest and settled. She pressed it once with the flat of her hand — not consciously, the way you press a bruise to confirm it is still there — and then let her hand fall.

The child had reached the woodpile. She was examining something in the gap between two logs — a beetle, perhaps, or a spider. She crouched with her hands on her knees and her face very close, entirely absorbed, the bark already dropped again and forgotten.

Yanu sat on the flat stone and watched her.

She had not explained the pendant. She had not explained the mound, or the trader, or the chain of names, or the morning she had knelt on the wet chalk hillside with her breath showing in the cold air and said a name into the ground that had never been said anywhere but inside her own chest for a full winter. The child was two years old. None of it was hers to carry yet.

But the name was hers. The name had already found her, the way names do — not by explanation but by use, by the daily ordinary fact of being called it, by hearing it in her father's voice and her grandmother's voice until it became indistinguishable from herself.

The child stood up suddenly from her crouch and turned, looking for something else. She found a stone, picked it up, considered it, dropped it. She moved along the woodpile with her hands trailing the rough-cut logs.

*You don't know what you carry,* Yanu thought again. *You will.*

A lark went up from somewhere beyond the ridge, its call rising and rising, the way it always did, as if it could not help itself, as if the sky kept pulling it higher than it meant to go. The child stopped and looked up, following the sound with her eyes though there was nothing to see.

Her face was open. Her hands hung at her sides.

She looked, for just that moment, like someone listening.

The fire had burned down to a good bed of coals by the time Ranu came and sat across from her.

He did not speak at once. He settled himself on the split-log bench with the care of a man whose knees had begun to make their opinions known, and he looked at the coals for a while, and then he looked at Yanu, and she could see he was carrying something he wanted to set down in the right place.

She waited.

"I heard you," he said. "With the child."

"I know."

He picked up a stick and turned it in his hands — not to use it, just to have something in his hands. "It should have words around it," he said. "The giving of the name again. Something to mark it."

Yanu looked at the coals. A small tongue of flame found a pocket of unburned wood and rose briefly, then settled. "No."

"Even a few words. Something to say this name has been held and is now — "

"Ranu."

He stopped.

"The name doesn't need ceremony," she said. "It needs to be used. It needs to be said in the morning when she wakes and at night when she won't sleep and when she falls in the mud and when she does something worth praising. It needs to be worn smooth." She paused. "Ceremony would make it a monument. I don't want a monument. I want a name."

Ranu was quiet. He turned the stick once more and then set it down beside the fire. After a moment he reached for a split log from the stack behind him and laid it carefully across the coals, adjusting it without being asked, without comment, the way he always had — she had never once had to ask him to tend a fire.

The wood caught slowly. The smell of it rose, sharp and green at the edges where the bark was still damp from the afternoon's rain.

They sat.

Above the ridge the sky had cleared to a dark, cold blue, the kind that comes after a day of shifting cloud and brief showers, and the first stars were showing. The air smelled of wet wool and woodsmoke and the particular chalk-and-earth smell that always drifted down from the direction of the mound when the wind came from the north. Yanu had stopped noticing it years ago. Tonight she noticed it.

Somewhere in the roundhouse behind her she could hear Kato talking in his sleep — a low, continuous murmur, rising and falling, the words just below sense. Piri had gone in early, wrapped in his mat like a grub, already unreachable. Sena had gone to Okka's fire. The settlement had quieted to its nighttime sounds: a dog somewhere, a child briefly crying and then not, the distant low of cattle.

Ranu said nothing for a long time. He watched the fire. His face in the firelight was the face she had known for twenty years — the lines deeper now, the hair at his temples gone to grey, but the same face, the same quality of attention, the way he looked at things as if he were already deciding how to describe them.

She was glad of him. She had not always told him so. She thought perhaps she should.

She did not.

After a long time he said: "She will ask who the first Taya was."

"Yes," Yanu said.

He looked at her across the fire. "What will you tell her?"

Yanu thought of the child's hands on the pendant. She thought of the mound in early spring, the cowslips coming up through the grass on its southern face, the pale chalk showing in the freshly turned earth at the margins where someone had pressed an offering in. She thought of the morning she had laid her palms flat on the cold wet ground and said a name she had been carrying all winter, and how the ground had not answered, and how that had been all right, and how she had understood in that moment that the answer was not the point.

"Everything," she said. "When she is ready to hold it."

Ranu nodded. He reached for the stick again, not to speak, just to have it. He turned it once in his hands and set it back down.

The fire burned. The new log had caught fully now, and the light had widened, and the warmth reached Yanu's face and the backs of her hands where they rested on her knees. Her knuckles ached with the damp, the way they always did in the wet months. She flexed them once and let them be.

She thought: *she will ask, and I will tell her. I will tell her about the mound and the morning and the cold ground and the name. I will tell her about the pendant and the trader's hands and what he made in an afternoon without being asked. I will tell her about the woman who wore this name before her — the look she had when she was deciding something, the small serious line between her brows. I will tell her that the name is not a weight. It is a cord. It goes back and it goes forward and she is somewhere in the middle of it, which is where all of us are.*

She would not tell the child any of this tonight. Tonight the child was asleep with her fist against her cheek, her breath even, her name already in her, already becoming her. Tonight was enough.

Ranu shifted on the bench. He had the look of a man composing something — she could always tell, the slight stillness around his mouth, the eyes going inward. She waited for him to say it.

He didn't. Whatever it was, he kept it.

She was glad of that too.

The fire settled, and the coals deepened to orange, and the chalk-and-earth smell came again on the wind, and Yanu sat with her hands on her knees and her back straight and the pendant warm against her chest, and she did not speak, and neither did he, and it was enough.

The cold came early that year.

By the time the last of the autumn rains had finished, Yanu could feel it in her chest — not a sharp pain, nothing that announced itself, only a heaviness that sat below her ribs and would not shift. She said nothing about it to Ranu. She said nothing to Sena, who had enough to manage with the grain stores and the cattle fence that kept pulling loose on the north side. She told herself it was the damp, the kind of damp that settled into old joints and old lungs when a woman had ground grain in the cold for too many winters, and that it would ease when the weather turned.

The weather did not turn. Or rather it turned the wrong way — toward frost, toward grey mornings with ice on the water bucket, toward the particular biting wind that came off the ridge and found every gap in the roundhouse walls. The thatch needed attention at the east side and Sena patched it, and that helped, but the cold had already made itself at home in Yanu's chest and was not interested in leaving.

She still worked. She ground grain in the mornings, slower than she had, resting her weight differently on the quern stone so the motion pulled less across her back. She scraped hides when there were hides to scrape, though her hands ached and she had to stop and flex them over the fire more often than she liked. She told herself she was being careful, not diminished. She was not always sure of the difference.

Ranu watched her. He said nothing direct — he was not a man who said direct things about illness, having learned somewhere in his life that naming a thing too plainly could fix it in place — but he tended the fire without being asked, which he always did, and he brought water without being asked, which he did not always do, and she understood from this that he saw what she had not told him.

The children knew less. Kato, who noticed everything and reported it at length, seemed to have decided that his mother's slowness was a seasonal matter, like the cattle's reluctance in frost, and did not press her on it. Piri slept later and later as the cold deepened and was rarely the first to notice anything. Sena knew, she thought — he had his father's quality of seeing without appearing to look — but he kept his knowing quiet, the way he kept most things.

Wasi knew in the way small children know things: not as information but as weather, a change in the air around the adults she loved. She came to Yanu more often in those weeks, launching herself without warning onto Yanu's lap and pressing her dark eyes up close, as if proximity were the same as understanding. Yanu held her and did not explain. There was nothing to explain that the child could hold yet.

The baby Taya — Sena's daughter, six months old now, round and serious and given to long, considering looks at whatever she was pointed toward — came with Sena's wife most mornings. She would set her in the crook of Yanu's arm while she worked, and Yanu would hold the weight of her and feel the child's warmth through the wool wrapping, the small hands flexing and releasing at the edge of the cloak, the breath coming in quick, even pulls. This was a different child from the girl who had turned the bark in her fist — that child was walking now, already into everything, already arguing with Kato over the last of the dried berries — but the name was the same, and Yanu held the infant with the same quality of attention she had given the toddler at the quern stone: careful, present, still working out what she felt.

On a morning in the deep cold, with frost still on the grass outside and the breath of the cattle showing in the yard, Yanu sat with the baby in her arm and felt the heaviness in her chest spread, slowly, like water finding its level in low ground. She breathed carefully. The child looked up at her with the particular unfocused attention of an infant who is not yet making sense of faces but is already deciding what she thinks of them.

Yanu said, quietly, so that only the child could have heard: "You are named for someone who was here before you."

The child's eyes moved — not toward the words, toward the warmth of the breath that carried them.

"She is still here," Yanu said. "She is in the earth."

The child reached for the edge of Yanu's cloak with both hands and gripped it and held on.

Sena's wife came in from the yard with a clay pot of water and set it near the fire. She was a good woman — careful, not quick to speak, with Sena's way of doing the necessary thing before it was asked. She looked at her mother-in-law holding the baby and said nothing, which was the right thing.

Yanu said: "I need you to take her to the mound."

She turned from the fire. Her face went still in the way it did when she was deciding how to respond to something she had not expected. "Now?"

"Not now. When the ground is soft enough. When the frost has gone."

"What do I do when I'm there?"

"Press her hands flat to the earth. The way I've always done it. Both palms, flat."

She nodded slowly. She understood the gesture; she had watched it for years.

"And say a name," Yanu said. "Only the first one. The one that belongs to the ground."

Sena's wife was quiet for a moment. The fire crackled. Outside, one of the cattle shifted and the fence post knocked against its neighbor.

"I don't know which one that is," she said. Not defensive — honest. She was always honest.

"I'll tell you," Yanu said.

She told her that evening, when the children were in, when the fire had settled and Ranu was at Sena's end of the house checking the fence timber they'd brought in to dry. She told her the name carefully, the way it had been told to her — the pause before the second syllable, the weight of it, the way the meaning shifted with the pause from a common word to something that belonged only to one person and one place.

She said it back. She got it right on the second try.

"That's it," Yanu said.

"Who was she?"

"The first one. She started it." Yanu shifted the baby, who had fallen asleep against her chest, the small face slack and warm. "She buried someone she loved, and she pressed her hands flat to the earth, and she said the name, and that was the beginning."

"Of what?"

Yanu thought about how to answer that. "Of knowing that the name doesn't end when the person does."

She looked at her mother-in-law for a long moment. Then she looked at her daughter asleep in Yanu's arms. Her expression was one Yanu recognized: the look of a person receiving something they are not yet sure they are large enough to hold, but who is going to try.

"I'll take her," she said. "When the frost is gone."

The frost did not go quickly. The cold held through what should have been the first mild weeks, and the ground around the mound stayed hard and pale, and Yanu did not go to it. She had not gone since the autumn. She had not missed it, exactly — she had carried it with her the way you carry a place you know well, in the body rather than the mind — but she was aware of its absence from her mornings in a way she had not expected. She had thought she would not mind. She found she did not mind, precisely, but she noticed.

She got worse in the middle cold. Not sharply, not in a way that announced itself as a crisis, but the heaviness in her chest thickened and she could not walk far without stopping, and she slept more than she had, and the grain grinding became something Sena or his wife did while she sat by the fire and told them when they were getting the angle wrong. She was not graceful about this. She had never been good at being still.

Ranu sat with her. He sat with her the way he had always tended fires — without being asked, without comment, with a quality of attention that was its own kind of speech. He brought her water and he brought her broth and he sat on the bench beside her and sometimes he talked and sometimes he didn't, and she was grateful for both in equal measure.

One evening when the children were asleep and the fire had burned low, he said: "Do you want me to take you to the mound?"

"No," she said.

He nodded. He did not ask again.

She thought about the mound in those last weeks. Not obsessively — she was not a woman given to obsession — but the way you think about a place that has been part of your daily life for a long time and is now slightly beyond reach. She thought about the cowslips that would come up on the southern face when the cold finally broke, the pale yellow of them against the dark grass. She thought about the chalk-and-earth smell that came down on the north wind. She thought about the morning she had first pressed her palms flat and said the name into the cold ground, the way the ground had not answered, the way that had been enough.

She did not ask to be buried there.

She had never asked for that. The thought had not crossed her mind, or rather it had crossed her mind once and she had set it aside without difficulty. The mound was Taya's. It had always been Taya's. The pendant was there — or rather, the pendant was at her chest, and it would go into the ground with her, near the mound but not in it, because she was not the first one and had never claimed to be. The first one lay in the deep layer, in the dark, with the chalk and the long silence of the undisturbed earth. Yanu had built something — not with her hands, not with timber and turf, but with the small repeated act of pressing palms to earth and saying a name — and the thing she had built was not hers to be buried in. It was for the ones who came after. It was for the child sleeping in her daughter-in-law's arms with the old name new in her mouth.

She died before the first warm month, on a morning when the frost was still on the grass and the breath of the cattle still showed in the yard. Ranu was beside her. He had been beside her all night, sitting on the bench with his back against the wall and his hands in his lap, not sleeping, or if sleeping then lightly, the way old men sleep when they are keeping watch. She was aware of him in the last hours the way she had always been aware of him — as a presence at the edge of her attention, reliable as a fire tended without being asked.

She did not speak at the end. There was nothing she needed to say that had not already been said, or that could not be carried by the people who would carry it.

The last thing she was aware of was the smell of the house — woodsmoke, lanolin, the faint chalk-and-earth that had followed her in from outside on her cloak, years of it worked into the walls — and Ranu's breathing, steady in the dark, and the weight of the pendant against her chest, warm from her own warmth, and she thought, without urgency, without grief: *She will take her. She will press the hands flat. She will say the name. The name will go into the ground and come back up again. That is how it works. That is how it has always worked.*

Then she was not thinking anything.

---

They buried her near the mound, not in it. A shallow grave on the eastern margin, where the ground was soft enough to work, in the shadow of the old rise. Sena and Ranu dug it together with the iron mattock, the blade dark with rust at its edges, the wooden haft smooth from use. The soil came up in layers — dark humus over pale chalk subsoil, the chalk catching the thin morning light and gleaming briefly before the next shovelful covered it. A rook called twice from the ridge and went silent. The wind came from the north, carrying the chalk-and-earth smell, and the wet wool smell of the people gathered there, and the faint green sharpness of the cut turf.

They laid Yanu in the ground with the pendant at her chest. The copper had been warm against her skin for so many years that it still held her heat when Sena's wife folded the cloak over her. She left it there. It belonged to Yanu in a way nothing else did — the trader had made it for her, for her particular grief, and it had never belonged to the chain in the way the mound belonged to the chain. The mound held the first one. The pendant had held Yanu. Both would go into the earth now, each to its proper depth.

Sena's wife held the baby Taya through the whole of it. The child was awake, eyes moving over the faces of the people around her without comprehension, fingers working at the edge of the cloak.

When it was done, she stood at the edge of the grave for a moment. Then she crouched and pressed her free hand flat to the fresh earth — palm down, fingers spread — and said the name. The first one. The one that belonged to the ground.

The baby looked down at her mother's hand on the earth. She reached toward it with both of hers, the gesture absolute and certain, the way she reached for everything bright or warm or close.

The frost broke three days later. The cowslips came up on the southern face of the mound, pale yellow against the dark grass, the same as every year.

---

Ranu pressed both hands flat to the earth above her.

He had to lower himself slowly, his knees finding the soft ground at the grave's edge, and Sena put a hand under his elbow without being asked. Ranu did not look up to acknowledge it. He was looking at the earth. His palms spread wide, fingers pressed into the dark soil, the chalk-pale subsoil showing at the grave's cut edge where the spade had gone through.

He said her name.

He said it the way she had taught him, without ceremony, without the words of approach or departure he had once proposed and she had refused. Just the name, spoken into the ground. His voice was steady. He had been practicing steadiness for forty years in her company and it had finally taken hold.

The rook called again from the ridge. Nobody moved.

Sena's wife stood at the head of the grave with the baby Taya against her shoulder, the child's face turned outward toward the mound's eastern face, the old grass-covered rise dark with morning damp. The child was watching Ranu's hands on the earth with the focused, purposeless attention of the very young — not understanding, only seeing. Her own fingers worked at the neck of the cloak, grasping and releasing, grasping and releasing.

Kato stood to his left with his eyes fixed on the middle distance, the way he stood when he did not want anyone to see his face. Piri was beside him, still half-wrapped in his sleeping cloak, his breath showing in the cold air. Sena had not let go of Ranu's elbow.

Ranu lifted his hands from the earth and looked at the marks his palms had left in the soft soil — two pale impressions, already beginning to blur at the edges as the ground settled back.

He said nothing else. There was nothing else required.

He let Sena help him up.

---

The cowslips were already showing on the southern face — four of them, then seven, pale yellow against the dark wet grass, the same as every year. The child Taya would not remember this morning. She was too young for memory, too young for names, too young for anything except warmth and the sound of voices and the smell of her mother's wool cloak and the cold air on her cheeks that made her eyes water and her small fingers flex and grasp.

But her name was in the ground now.

Not the name Ranu had spoken at the grave's edge — that was Yanu's name, going into the earth beside the mound, close enough that the ground would know it, not so close that it displaced anyone. Yanu's name was new in the soil, shallow, still finding its depth. The pendant was with her, warm from her own warmth, its copper holding the last of her heat in the cold earth.

The name the child carried was older.

It had been in the ground since before Ranu was born, since before his mother was born, since before anyone now living could count back to. It was in the deep layer, in the dark, with the chalk and the long silence of the undisturbed earth. And now it was also here, in this child's body, in the breath her father had given her name with, in the sound that had gone into the air on the morning of her birth and would go into the ground again someday, and out of the ground again after that, and forward.

Three names, Yanu had thought in those last weeks, lying on the bench with the pendant warm against her chest and the frost on the outside of the wall and Ranu's breathing steady in the dark.

Three depths.

The first Taya, deepest — the one who had been here before all of them, whose name required the pause, whose memory lay in the chalk and would lie there long after the mound's grass grew over and the cowslips seeded and the people who knew what it held were themselves held in the ground. She was the bottom of it. She was the reason there was a bottom.

Yanu herself, middle — not in the mound, never in the mound, that was not her place and she had never wanted it to be — but near it, on the eastern margin where the ground was soft and the shadow of the old rise fell in the mornings. Close enough. The ground would know her. The ground knew everyone who had pressed their palms to it and said a name into it for thirty years. And the pendant was with her, as it had always been, as it should be.

And the child, shallowest, newest, not yet in the ground at all but carrying the name above it — Taya, again, the name given back to the air, the name that meant *she is still here, she is in the earth, she was here before you and she does not leave.*

The child did not understand this.

She would.

---

Sena's wife straightened up from the grave's edge. She shifted the baby to her other hip, the child's weight settling against her, and looked at the mound's eastern face — the old grass, the chalk showing at a rabbit's scrape near the base, the cowslips coming up on the far side where the ground caught the morning light.

Ranu was standing with Sena's hand still at his elbow, looking at the same thing.

"She never asked to be in it," she said.

"No," Ranu said.

"She said it was Taya's."

Ranu was quiet for a moment. The wind came off the ridge carrying the chalk-and-earth smell, the wet wool smell of the gathered people, the faint green sharpness of the cut turf. A lapwing called somewhere over the valley and the sound came and went.

"It was always Taya's," he said. "She understood that from the beginning. Before I did."

She looked down at the child in her arms. The baby Taya had stopped reaching for the cloak. She was looking at the mound — not at the grave, not at Ranu's hands, but at the old rise itself, the dark grass, the pale chalk at the base where the rabbit had scraped.

Her eyes did not move.

She was too young for understanding. She was too young for anything except the warmth of her mother's body against the cold and the sound of voices saying words she did not yet have shapes for.

But she was looking at the mound.

She felt the weight of her — the solid, particular weight of a child who is alive and present and entirely herself — and thought: *she will come back here. She will press her hands flat. She will say the names.*

The child reached toward the mound with both hands, fingers spread wide, the gesture absolute.

She did not stop her. There was nothing to stop. The child's hands found only air.

That was all right. The ground would be there when she was ready for it. The ground was not going anywhere.

---

They walked back to the settlement in a loose group, Ranu between Sena and Kato, the two of them matching his pace without making a show of it. Piri fell in behind, still trailing his sleeping cloak, his breath showing in the cold. The frost was still on the north-facing grass but the south-facing slopes were beginning to soften, the earth darkening as it thawed.

Sena's wife walked last, the baby on her hip, and did not look back.

She did not need to. She knew what was there: the mound, old and grass-covered and patient, with the cowslips coming up on the southern face. The fresh grave on the eastern margin, the pale chalk of the turned soil already settling. The double impression of Ranu's palms in the earth, blurring at the edges. And below the grave, deeper still, Yanu with the pendant warm against her chest — or cooling now, the copper finding the temperature of the earth around it, becoming part of it.

And below all of that, deeper than the grave, deeper than the chalk, in the dark where the first name had been spoken into the ground before anyone now living could count back to: the beginning of it.

The child on her hip made a sound — not a word, not a name, just a sound, the kind a child makes when something has caught her attention and she has not yet learned to say what it is.

She looked down at her.

The baby was looking back over her shoulder at the mound.

"Yes," she said.

The child looked at her, then back at the mound, then at her again, with the absolute seriousness of the very young.

"You will," she said. "When you're ready."

The child considered this for a moment, with Yanu's dark eyes in a face that was entirely her own.

Then she turned forward, into the morning, into the smell of the hearth fires coming up from the settlement below, into the world that was waiting for her to grow into it.

The frost broke three days later.
