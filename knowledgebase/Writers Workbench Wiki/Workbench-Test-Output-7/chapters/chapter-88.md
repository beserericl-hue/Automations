The ruling loaded in stages.

First the header: *Maryland Historical Trust Commission on Indigenous Cultural Properties, Final Determination, Case No. 2024-ICP-07.* Then the body text resolving into columns. Then the appendices, each one a separate PDF that her browser queued and rendered in turn. Kimi watched the progress bar the way you watch a wound to see if it is still bleeding. Her laptop sat on the kitchen table. The overhead light was off. The monitor threw its cool blue across her hands and the grain of the wood and the half-empty mug of tea she had forgotten about.

Outside, a truck downshifted on the street. The refrigerator clicked on.

She had been waiting for this since March. Since before March, since the first hearing date got pushed and then pushed again, and she had built the archive folder in the meantime to have something to do with her hands. She had labeled it *18CH-447 / Commission Proceedings* and set up the naming convention herself, the way she had taught herself everything about this: by looking at how other people had done it, and then doing it more carefully. *2024-03-14_HearingTranscript_v1.pdf. 2024-04-02_TayakTestimony_Submitted.pdf.* Date first, always. Description. Version. She had read three archival guides and a Dublin Core metadata tutorial and had written a README she updated every time she added a file.

No one had asked her to do any of this. That was the part she still could not explain to people.

She opened the folder. Twelve entries already. The hearing transcript. Nora's affidavit, scanned at 600 DPI because she had read that TIFF was better for long-term preservation and then converted to PDF/A because PDF/A was what courts and repositories could actually open. Wren's field drawings, three of them, signed *W. Calvert. 18CH-447* in the lower right corner. The 1687 Calvert patent boundary description, which Wren had sent as a photograph and which Kimi had reshot on a neutral gray background with a color card and a ruler because the original had a thumb in the corner.

She had done all of it like that. Like it mattered whether a thumb was in the corner.

She created a new entry. The naming convention said: date, description, version. She typed: *2024-11-19_CommissionRuling_Final.pdf.* Then she stopped and looked at it. *Final.* That was the word. She had been typing *Submitted* and *Draft* and *v1* and *v2* for eight months and now she was typing *Final.*

She dragged the ruling PDF into the folder. The progress bar ran. The file was 847 pages, she had checked, had scrolled to the end before she did anything else, needing to see the number, and it took long enough that she picked up the mug and remembered the tea was cold. She set it back down.

The upload finished. The file sat in the folder with the others, the same size text, the same font. It looked like every other entry. That was the right thing. That was what an archive was supposed to look like: uniform, each thing given its correct weight. But she had expected to feel more than this.

She opened the ruling and went to the findings of fact. Page 31. She had read it twice already on her phone while the ruling was still loading on the laptop, standing with one hand on the counter, reading the language the commission had used: *unbroken ancestral presence*, *continuous occupation*, *the oral testimony of Nora Moyaone, corroborated by material evidence recovered from Site 18CH-447, constitutes*

She closed the PDF. She knew what it said.

She opened the README and added a line at the bottom:

*2024-11-19: Commission ruling uploaded. Case No. 2024-ICP-07. 847 pages. PDF/A format. SHA-256 hash: [to be added]. Second backup pending, upload to off-site cloud once verified locally.*

She had read about checksums in the digital preservation guide. The idea was that you ran a hash of the file when you first received it, and then you ran it again later, and if the numbers matched, the file had not changed, had not degraded, had not been touched. The file was what it said it was. She had downloaded sha256sum to do this and had been running checksums on every document since April, which she had not told anyone because she did not know how to explain why it mattered to her that the numbers matched.

She ran the checksum now. The tool churned for a moment. The number appeared, sixty-four hexadecimal characters meaning nothing to anyone who did not know what it was for, and she pasted it into the README.

She sat back.

The refrigerator cycled off. The room was quieter. On the screen, the folder held thirteen entries now, each one named and dated and hashed, each one pointing at something real that had happened: a hearing, a testimony, a field drawing, a ruling. The archive did not know it was an archive. It was just files. But she had built it so that anyone, fifty years from now, could open the folder and understand what had happened here and in what order and with what evidence. That understanding would not depend on anyone remembering to tell them.

She thought about Nora, who had been remembering to tell people for sixty-two years.

She saved the README. She closed the laptop halfway, then opened it again and checked that the file was still there.

It was still there.

She picked up the cold mug and went to put the kettle on. Through the kitchen window the street was dark and ordinary. She stood there waiting for the water to boil, not sure yet what came next., -

She called Nora at 11:47. Not because she had planned to. She was standing at the kettle, watching the indicator light, and she thought about the archive and what it was missing, and then she was already dialing.

Nora picked up on the second ring.

"Kimi."

"Were you asleep?"

"No."

Kimi did not ask what she had been doing. She said, "I uploaded the ruling. It's in the archive. All of it, the findings, the appendices, the artifact inventory. It's there."

"Good," Nora said. Just that.

The kettle clicked off. Kimi did not move.

"I want to ask you something," she said. "And you can say no."

"Ask."

"I want to make a video. Not about the case, or it will be, but that's not what it's for." She stopped. She had not rehearsed this. "I want to put the photographs in it. The artifacts. The pendant. The gorget. All of them. And I want to put your voice over them." She pressed her free hand flat on the counter. "Reading the names."

The line was quiet for a moment.

"All of them," Nora said.

"All of them. From the beginning. Yanu. Taya. Everyone in between."

Another silence. Not hesitation. Kimi had learned to tell the difference. Nora was deciding something, but she had already decided it.

"All right," Nora said.

"Now?"

"Yes."

Kimi set the phone down and got her second device, the old one she used for recording because its microphone was better than the new one's, and propped it against the mug with the screen facing up. She opened the voice memo app and hit record. The red dot pulsed. She picked the phone back up.

"I'm ready," she said.

There was a breath on the line. Then Nora began., -

She started with Yanu.

The name came out careful and slow, two syllables with a weight on the first that Kimi had not expected, and then a pause before the next name. Kimi understood without being told that the pauses were part of it. This was not a list but a ceremony, each name given its own moment of air before the next one followed.

*Yanu. Sena's wife. Ranu. Sena.*

Kimi did not write anything down. She stood in the kitchen with the phone pressed to her ear and her free hand flat on the counter and she did not move.

*Taya.*

Nora said it with a pause before the second syllable, the way Kimi had heard Tayak describe it, the pause that changed the meaning, that turned a common word into a name belonging only to one person and one place, and Kimi felt something shift in her chest, something that had no word she could reach for quickly.

The names kept coming.

Some of them Kimi recognized from the archive: Okwi, Matta, Tessa, Waya, names she had typed into metadata fields and README entries, names she had read in Nora's affidavit at 600 DPI. But hearing them was different. Reading a name was a different act from hearing it said aloud in the dark by a woman who had been carrying it for sixty-two years. The name *Okwi* on a page was a historical fact. The name *Okwi* in Nora's voice, at 11:52 at night, was something else.

*Saya.*

Kimi's hand pressed harder on the counter without her noticing.

The names she did not recognize were the hardest to hear. They came from the deep part of the chain, the centuries before the colonial documents, and some of them had sounds that English did not prepare a mouth for: a consonant cluster that Nora navigated with the care of someone who had practiced it ten thousand times, a vowel that sat at the back of the throat. Kimi could not have repeated them. But she heard them, and she understood that they were real, that each one had been carried forward by a person who had learned it from a person who had learned it from a person, all the way back to a woman by a river who had pressed her palms flat on a small rise of earth and said a child's name into the ground.

The recording device's red dot pulsed against the side of the mug.

Nora did not rush. She did not explain. She said each name and let it stand.

*Waya. Kaya. Sokan. Ahanu.*

The refrigerator cycled on. Kimi did not hear it.

*Renata.*

There were names near the bottom of the chain that Kimi would not find in any document. They predated the colonial period by centuries, some of them by a millennium. They would not appear in a deed book or a court folio or an NSF proposal. They existed only in this: a woman's voice in the dark, saying them one at a time into a phone, across forty miles, while Kimi stood in a kitchen with her hand flat on a counter and did not breathe.

*Pell. Kessik. Litta.*

Kimi thought about the archive. She thought about the README and the checksums and the sixty-four hexadecimal characters that proved a file had not changed. She thought about how she had built the archive so that anyone, fifty years from now, could open it and understand what had happened. This, what was happening right now on the recording device propped against the mug, this was the thing the archive existed to hold. Not the hearing transcript. Not the commission ruling. This.

*Ida. Nora's mother. Nora.*

Nora paused there. A longer pause than the others.

Then she said, quietly: *Tayak.*

And then she stopped.

The line was silent. Kimi could hear Nora breathing. She could hear her own breathing. Through the kitchen window the street was dark. A car passed slowly, headlights sweeping across the ceiling, and was gone.

The silence went on for a long time.

Kimi reached over and stopped the recording. The red dot went still.

"Thank you," she said. Her voice came out wrong, too thin, too careful.

"You know them now too," Nora said.

Kimi did not answer.

She pressed her lips together and looked at the ceiling and breathed through her nose, slow and even, the way you breathe when you are trying to keep your face from doing something you do not want a person on the phone to hear. Her eyes were wet. She did not wipe them.

On the counter, the recording device sat with its screen dark, holding forty-three names in a voice memo file that she would transfer to the archive before she slept. She would label it *2024-11-19_NoraMoyaone_NamesRecitation_v1.m4a*, would run a checksum on it, would back it up to two locations, would attach an access note reading *Community use, please contact archive keeper before public distribution* because she had read about data sovereignty and she understood that some things were not hers to give away freely, even things she had been given.

She knew that. She had read the guides.

But right now she was not thinking about the archive.

She was thinking about a woman named Yanu who had pressed her palms flat on a small rise of earth two thousand years ago and said a name into the ground, and how that name had traveled forward through fire, through drought, through colonial courts and constables and a man who walked past his sister on the road without stopping, and had arrived here, in this kitchen, in Nora's voice, in the dark.

She was thinking that she had heard it.

"Kimi," Nora said.

"I'm here," Kimi said. Steadier than she expected.

"Good," Nora said. Then, gently: "Go to sleep.", -

The photographs were Tayak's. Tayak had sent them three weeks ago, a folder of seventy-eight images, field documentation mostly, not intended for this, but Kimi had looked through them twice and understood what they were. The trench in early morning light, the walls of it precise and dark, the strata visible as horizontal bands of color: pale clay, then the darker fill, then the orange-brown of undisturbed subsoil. Tayak's hands in nitrile gloves, brush moving across a surface. The pendant in its conservation tray, lying on gray foam, a small scale ruler beside it. The mound's surface from above, taken from a ladder probably, the grass still holding dew, the site tape orange against the green. A photograph of nothing, apparently, just the creek through the tree line, the water carrying light, that Kimi had looked at for a long time before she understood it was the view from the trench's edge, the view Tayak had been looking at every morning for eleven months.

She had set the audio to begin there. Nora's voice, the first name: *Yanu.* Then cut to the trench. Then the mound from above. Then Tayak's hands. She had not used any music. She had not used any title card. She had considered a lower-third, the name of the site, the date, something, and then deleted it. The names were enough. The names were the whole thing.

She had graded the footage flat and warm, pulled the highlights down so the artifacts sat in shadow at their edges, the way objects look when they have been underground a long time and have only recently come into the light. No transitions between the photographs. Just cuts, clean and direct, one image to the next, the way you turn the pages of something that matters.

By four in the morning she had a cut she did not want to change. Eleven minutes and forty seconds. Nora's voice reading every name from Yanu to Tayak, each one given its own breath, its own small silence after, while the photographs moved underneath: the artifacts, the earth, the hands, the creek, the light. The last image was the pendant again, wide this time, the whole conservation tray, the scale ruler, the gray foam, the accession number on a small paper label. The audio ended. Twenty-six thousand.

While she watched, it jumped again, four hundred, five hundred, a number that arrived all at once and then held. She did not refresh the page. She let it run.

Outside, the sky was the color of the creek at first light, pale gold going to blue, the kind of light that did not last. She thought about a woman pressing her palms flat on a small rise of earth above a river, two thousand years ago, saying a name into the ground because she did not know what else to do with it.

She thought: *it traveled.*

She closed the metadata window. She left the view counter open. She sat back in her chair and let the morning come in.

She picked up her phone and called Tayak.

It rang twice. Then Tayak's voice, awake already, she was always awake, flat and careful the way it got when she was managing something. "Kimi."

"Did you see it?"

"I saw it at five." A pause. "You should have called me first."

"I know." Kimi waited.

"Nora gave her permission."

"Yes. Last night. She said yes before I finished asking."

The line was quiet for a moment. Kimi could hear, faintly, the sound of wind through a phone held near a window or a door, Tayak outside, or standing where the frame seal had gone. Then: "The ruling is in the archive?"

"Filed and checksummed. Two backups."

"Dana Osei."

"I haven't responded. I wanted to ask you first."

Another silence, longer. Kimi watched the view counter: twenty-seven thousand, then a jump, then twenty-eight.

"Not yet," Tayak said. "But not never. Give me until this afternoon."

"Okay."

"And Kimi." A pause. "The video is." She stopped. When she came back her voice was different, lower, the management gone out of it. "Tell me you're keeping the audio file somewhere it can't disappear."

"Two locations," Kimi said. "Cloud bucket and physical drive. Checksum verified at five-thirty. The numbers matched."

A breath on the line. "Good."

"Tayak."

"Yeah."

"Nora said, at the end, after she finished, she said I know them now too. The names."

The line was quiet.

"Do you?" Tayak said.

"I think so. Most of them." Kimi looked at the phonetic guide open in the corner of her screen, the vertical bars marking pauses, the syllable breaks on the difficult clusters. "I got lost in the middle centuries. The ones before the colonial record."

"Those are the hardest."

"I know." She paused. "I want to learn them properly. All of them."

Tayak did not answer immediately. When she did, her voice had something in it that Kimi could not quite name. "Come to the site this week. Bring Nora if she's willing. There's something I want to show you."

"What?"

"The trench. The chamber. Where it started." A pause. "You've been building the archive for eight months and you've never seen the ground."

Kimi looked at the view counter. Twenty-nine thousand. The morning light was coming in through the kitchen window now, pale and clear, the kind that arrived before the cold did.

"I'll ask her," she said.

"Good," Tayak said. Then, quieter: "The names traveled, Kimi. You did that."

She pressed her lips together. Breathed through her nose. Did not say anything.

"I'll call you this afternoon," Tayak said, and was gone.

Kimi set the phone down. She sat for a moment in the early morning with the archive folder open and the view counter climbing and the phonetic guide on the screen, the vertical bars holding the space where the pauses went.

Then she opened a new document and began, from the beginning, to write the names down.
