The candle had burned down to a nub the width of his thumb, and Okwi had not moved in an hour.

He sat at the camp table — two planks laid across a pair of split logs — with the petition in his hands and the oilskin folded on the wood beside him. The oilskin smelled of linseed and something sharper underneath, like pine resin, and when he had spread it out earlier to check the folds, it had crackled in the quiet house like something alive.

Outside, the frogs had gone silent. That meant the hour before first light, the coldest part of the night, when the air pressed down on everything and even the insects stopped.

He read the petition.

He had read it before. He had held the candle steady while Saya copied it at this same table two nights before she went to the assembly, her hand moving across the laid paper in the careful, slightly uneven script her father had taught her. He had watched the iron gall ink go from wet-black to brown as it dried, and he had not spoken because she needed quiet when she worked. He knew every word. He had been present when Paka suggested *therein* and Tomah said *humbly beseeching* and Saya had written those words in without changing her expression, though afterward she had told Okwi that *humbly beseeching* was a phrase that made her feel like a dog lying on its back.

He read it anyway.

*To the Honourable the President and Council of the Province of Maryland, the humble Petition of the Piscataway People, being the ancient possessors of the lands herein described—*

Her hand. That was what he was reading. Not the words — the words were Paka's and Tomah's and the dead judge's and the clerk's, all the voices she had gathered and pressed flat onto paper. What he was reading was the particular way she formed the letter *h*, the slight lean of her capital *P*, the place on the third line where she had pressed too hard and the nib had caught and left a small spur of ink he could feel with his fingernail if he ran it across the page.

He ran his fingernail across the page.

The candle flame shuddered. He put his hand around it without thinking, and the heat pressed against his palm, and he held it there a moment longer than was comfortable before he let go.

She had died in the previous winter, when the fever came in the cold months and did not leave. It had come the way fevers always came — first the sweating, then the chills, then the long days of heat and confusion when she did not know where she was and called for her father, who had been dead six years. He had sat beside her through all of it, and he had not prayed because he did not know which prayers were still his to speak. The ones his mother had taught him felt too far away. The ones the mission men offered felt like someone else's coat, cut wrong across the shoulders. So he had sat and held her hand and given her water when she could take it, and on the seventh day she had stopped.

The petition had come back from the assembly in a letter. One of the clerk's boys had brought it to the winter camp, a folded sheet sealed with a blob of red wax that had cracked in the cold. Okwi had opened it. He had read it twice. He had put it in the fire.

He had not told anyone what it said. He did not need to. Everyone knew.

Now he looked at the second copy — the one Saya had made for *someone who will find it later*, she had said, *because that is how these things work, Okwi, the first copy goes to them and we keep the second somewhere they cannot reach it* — and he thought: she was right that they needed a copy in the ground. She had been right about most things. She had been wrong about the assembly, but she had known she might be wrong about the assembly, and she had made the second copy anyway, which meant she had been right about that too.

He turned the last page over. Her handprint was there, pressed in iron gall ink, the lines of her palm still visible if he held the page to the candle.

He held the page to the candle.

The paper was thin enough that the chain lines showed through — fine parallel ridges in the laid paper, the bones of the thing. Her handprint sat over them like a shadow.

He had hoped, sitting here, that the document would feel like her. That holding it would give him something — warmth, or the sense of her presence, or at least the particular quality of attention she had brought to everything she did, that focused, unsentimental care that had made him trust her before he had known her well enough to love her.

It felt like paper.

He set it down.

The candle flame steadied. In the sleeping room, their daughter turned in her sleep and made a small sound and went quiet. She was four years old. She could write her own name in the dirt with a stick, because Saya had taught her. That was the thing Saya had been most pleased about in the last year — not the petition, not the journey to the assembly, not the two copies. Their daughter writing her name in the dirt with a stick.

Okwi looked at the oilskin.

It was folded in a square, stiff and slightly tacky where the linseed had pooled at the creases. He had prepared it himself, soaking a length of linen in oil and hanging it to dry in the sun until it was rigid enough to keep water out. He had done it carefully. He had thought about what he was preparing it for, and he had been careful.

He picked up the petition — all three pages, the handprint page last — and he began to fold it.

He did not know if anyone would find it. He did not know if the ground would keep it. He knew what the ground did to paper, eventually — the wet, the rot, the slow erasure. He had dug enough pits to know what the soil did to things that were not stone or fired clay or metal. Paper was not any of those things.

But the oilskin might buy it time. And time was the only argument he had left.

He folded the petition into the oilskin, pressing each crease flat with his thumb. The oilskin crackled softly as he worked, and the smell of linseed rose in the cold air, and underneath it the faint smell of the petition itself — ink and rag paper and the particular dry smell of something that had been handled many times by careful hands.

He wrapped it once. He pressed the folds tight. He set it on the table beside the candle.

Then he stood, went to the small shelf above the sleeping room door, and took down the cedar box.

He had not looked at it since the fever. He had put it there himself, the morning after she died, because he could not leave it where she had kept it — beside the sleeping mat, within reach — and he could not bring himself to give it away, and so he had put it on the shelf with the other things he did not know what to do with, and then he had not looked at it.

It was small enough to hold in one palm — a trading-post box, not his work, not hers, the wood cut thin and fitted with a bone peg for a clasp. The cedar smell came off it even now, faint but distinct, the way cedar holds its smell long after everything else in a room has gone over to smoke and damp. Saya had kept the bead in it when she was not wearing it. He had assumed, without asking, that the box itself had been her mother's.

He opened it.

Inside: the blue glass bead, resting in a small hollow the wood had worn to fit it. He had taken it from her wrist when she died. He had worn it himself for a month, then stopped, because wearing it felt like a claim he was not sure he had the right to make, and he had put it back in the box, and there it had stayed.

He took the bead out and held it in the last of the candle's light. It was blue — a deep, particular blue, the color of deep water or a clear sky at the moment before it goes dark — and the surface was not perfectly smooth: there was a small pit on one side, a place where the glass had not fired evenly, and he could feel it under his thumb the way you feel a scar you have touched so many times it has become familiar. He turned it. The candlelight caught it and threw a small point of light across the table.

He knew what she had said about it once — that her mother had brought it south along the Potomac with a trading party, carried it the whole way in a knotted cloth, and that it was not the bead that mattered but the fact that her mother had chosen it, from among all the things a trading party carries, as the one thing worth keeping close. Saya had said this without sentiment, the way she said most things, as though the fact itself was enough without any weight added to it. He had understood. He had not asked more.

He set the bead in the center of the folded petition, on top of the oilskin. The bead sat there small and very blue against the pale linen. He looked at them together for a moment — the bead and the petition — and thought that they were not the same kind of thing, and that they were, now, going to the same place, and that Saya would have had something clear and unsentimental to say about that, and that he did not.

He began to fold the oilskin around them both.

The oilskin crackled as he worked. He folded the near edge over first, then the far edge over that, pressing each crease flat with the heel of his hand the way Saya had shown him — not with the fingers, which left uneven pressure, but with the heel, which laid the crease down hard and smooth. The oilskin was stiff and slightly tacky at the creases where the linseed had pooled, and it resisted folding the way a thing resists when it has set itself into a shape. He pressed it flat. The smell of oil and resin rose in the cold air.

He folded the sides in and pressed those creases flat too.

Then he took the cord.

It was a length of hemp cord, thin but strong, the kind used for binding bundles of dried leaf. Saya had taught him the knot — not as ceremony, just as the most secure way to bind something you needed to stay bound. He had watched her do it three times before he understood the sequence: tight, then tighter, then a half-hitch drawn down hard so the cord bit into itself and would not slip. He had practiced it afterward, alone, because he did not want to ask her to show him again. He had not thought, practicing it, that he would use it for this.

He wound the cord around the length of the package. Tight.

He wound it around the width. Tighter.

He tied the knot — the half-hitch, pulled down hard, the cord biting into the oilskin with a faint squeak of resistance — and he held it for a moment to make sure it had set. It had set. He ran his thumb along the cord where it crossed the first fold. The package was solid. It did not give.

He sat with it in his hands.

He was not a ceremonial man. He had never been. Saya had known this about him and had not tried to change it. There were men in the community who knew the words for moments like this — the right words for a burial, for a sending, for the placing of something important in the ground — and he was not one of them. He had always found the words came out wrong when he tried them, formal and hollow, like wearing someone else's clothes. He did not try them now.

The candle was burning low. Tallow, not oil — the last of the good candles, the ones Saya had made the previous autumn before she fell sick. He watched the flame for a moment. It did not waver.

He thought about praying. He thought about it the way you think about a door you know is there but have never opened — aware of it, not going through it. Whatever was on the other side of that door, he did not think it needed his words. He thought it needed the bead in the ground and the paper beside it and the oilskin keeping the water out for as long as it could, and that was what he was going to give it.

He stood up. The package was lighter than it looked. He tucked it under his arm.

The candle guttered as he moved, and he stopped, and the flame steadied, and he blew it out.

In the sleeping room, his daughter breathed slowly in the dark.

He stood for a moment in the extinguished smell of the candle — hot wax and a thread of smoke — and listened to her breathe. Four years old. She could write her name in the dirt. She did not know yet what the petition was, or what the assembly had done with it, or that her mother had walked two days to stand in front of men who had already decided. She would know eventually. He would tell her, or someone would. He did not know yet how to tell it in a way that was true without being only the failure. He would have to think about that.

He went to the door and lifted the bar and went out into the night.

The cold hit him across the face and chest like a flat hand. He stood for a moment in the doorway, letting his eyes find the dark.

There was no moon. The sky was clear and dense with stars — the Milky Way a pale smear above the tree line, the bright winter planets low in the south. The field stretched away from the house in shades of black, the mound a darker mass at its far edge, barely distinguishable from the tree line behind it. He had walked this ground since he was a boy. He did not need a torch.

He pulled the door closed behind him. The bar settled into its bracket with a soft knock. He stood still until he was sure the sound had not carried into the sleeping room, and then he started across the field.

The grass was February-stiff beneath his feet, each blade edged with frost that crunched under his sandals. The cold came up through the leather soles. He could feel the packed earth hardening as the night deepened — the slight give of the topsoil gone, the ground pressing back solid and indifferent. He held the bundle under his left arm, tucked against his ribs. The oilskin was warm where it had been against his body in the house. In the cold air, the smell of linseed oil and resin sharpened. He could hear the faint squeak of the cord where it crossed the folded edge, a small sound with each step, like something trying to speak.

Halfway across the field he stopped. He had heard something — or thought he had. A sound from the house, a child's sound, thin and indistinct. He turned and looked back. The house was dark. No light moved behind the shuttered window. He waited. Nothing. The field was full of its own sounds: the dry rustle of last year's stalks, the distant bark of a dog from somewhere past the eastern boundary, the high, continuous singing of insects in the grass that never entirely stopped even in February, even at this hour. He turned back toward the mound and kept walking.

The ground rose. It always surprised him, even now — how gradual the rise was, how you only knew you were climbing when your calves began to feel it. The mound was not dramatic from the side. From a distance it looked like a natural swelling in the field, the kind of thing a man who did not know the ground might walk past without noticing. Saya had called it *the old place* in a tone that meant: older than you know, older than I know, older than anyone left to ask. She had not said it with reverence exactly. She had said it the way you name a thing that requires no decoration.

He reached the top. The wind was stronger here, coming off the tree line from the north, and he turned his back to it. The mound's surface was uneven under his feet — patches of coarser grass where the soil was thin over old deposits, softer ground where organic matter had built up in the hollows. He had stood here before, years ago, with Saya. She had pointed out the slight depression near the center where the surface layer had settled over something below, and she had said: *people put things here for a long time before us, and we are not the last.* He had not thought much about it then. He thought about it now.

He set the bundle down at his feet and unslung the hoe from his shoulder. It was a short-handled iron hoe, the blade hammered thin from years of sharpening, the wooden shaft worn smooth and slightly dark from his hands and his father's hands before him. He had brought it wrapped in a length of cloth so it would not knock against the doorframe on the way out. He unwrapped it now and set the cloth aside.

He chose a spot slightly off the center of the mound — Saya's depression, the place where the ground had already begun its own slow sinking. He set the blade against the soil and pushed down with his foot.

The iron skidded. The ground was harder than he had expected. February had taken the moisture out of the top layer and left it compacted, almost stone-like at the surface. He repositioned the blade, set his weight on it, and pushed again. This time it bit — a small bite, half the width of his hand, no more. He worked it loose and set it aside.

He had not calculated this. He had thought: break the surface, place the bundle, cover it over. He had not thought about the cold and what it did to February ground.

He worked the hoe in short strokes, breaking the surface crust in sections, then scraping the loosened material away. The blade rang faintly against a potsherd buried near the surface — a small, sharp sound in the quiet — and he stopped and worked around it with his fingers. The sherd was thick, cord-marked on one face, cold and gritty in his hand. He set it aside on the pile of loosened soil. Below the surface crust the earth was looser, darker, and it came away more easily, releasing a smell of wet soil and old ash and something else beneath that — something organic and faint, the smell of things that had been in the ground a long time.

He worked the hoe until he had broken through the surface crust and cleared a shallow bed in the looser earth just beneath it — wide enough for the bundle, deep enough that a frost or a heavy rain would not shift the soil back and expose it, but no more than that. The surface layer was where things waited to be found. He was not digging a grave. He was making a place at the skin of the mound, where the old deposits began, where the ground itself would hold the record.

He set the hoe down and picked up the bundle. The oilskin was cold now, the linseed smell muted by the frost. He held it for a moment over the shallow bed. The petition was in there, folded flat, the handprint face-down. The bead was in the center of it, small and blue and cold. He had not looked at them again after wrapping them. He did not need to.

He placed the bundle in the bed of broken earth — not dropped, not pressed, but placed, the way you set down something you want to stay where you put it. He adjusted it once so the cord-bound edge faced up and the flat face lay against the soil. Then he knelt over it and said, aloud, to the mound and the dark field and the cold air and no one in particular:

*Someone will find this.*

He did not know if he believed it. He said it the way you say a thing that you need to be true, knowing the need is not the same as the truth. The words left his mouth and the mound did not answer and the stars kept their positions and the field was silent. He stayed kneeling for a moment after, listening to the silence, and felt nothing ceremonial at all — no shift in the air, no sense of completion. He had spoken the only true thing he could find, and it had gone into the cold and dissolved, and that was all there was.

He used both hands to push the loosened soil back over the bundle, working from the edges inward. The dark earth fell over the oilskin in layers — the finer particles first, sifting down around the edges, then the larger clumps, which he broke apart with his fingers before pressing them down. He replaced the potsherd where he had found it, in roughly the same position, because it had been there before him and should be there after. He pressed the surface flat with the heel of his hand — not the fingers, which left uneven pressure, but the heel — and when it was flat he pressed it again, harder, until the soil was compact and the surface matched the surrounding ground as nearly as he could make it.

He sat back on his heels. The mound was quiet. The stars had moved while he worked — the winter planets lower in the south, the Milky Way beginning to thin toward the west. He did not know how long he had been here. Long enough for the sweat to dry cold against his back.

He picked up the hoe and the cloth and stood. His knees ached from kneeling on the cold ground. He brushed the soil from his hands against his trousers, but his hands were still black, and the smell of the earth stayed with him.

He looked at the place where he had dug. He could not see it in the dark. He could only see the mound as it had been before he came — the darker mass against the sky, the slight depression near the center, the same ground it had always been. There was nothing to show that anything had changed.

He turned and walked back toward the house.

He walked the same path he had come by, following the line of the field's edge where the grass was shorter and his feet knew the ground. The cold had settled deeper since he'd left the house — the kind of cold that comes in the hour before dawn, when the night has given up all the warmth it was holding and the air goes still and final. His breath came in small clouds that dissolved before they reached his shoulders.

He was thinking about what to tell his daughter.

She was four. She could write her name in English letters — Saya had taught her, tracing them in the dirt with a stick, making her do it again until the letters stood upright and even. She could not read the petition. She did not know what a petition was. She had been asleep when he left and she would be asleep when he returned, her face turned toward the banked coals, her breathing the slow, even breathing of a child who does not yet know how much there is to fear.

He would tell her when she was older.

He would tell her: there is a document in the mound. There is a bead beside it. The document is your mother's work — her words, her hand pressed flat on the last page in ink. The bead was your grandmother's, brought south along the Potomac with a trading party, and your mother wore it at her throat until the fever took her, and I wore it after, and now it is in the ground with the paper because I did not know what else to do with the things that were hers. He would tell her all of this. He would find the right words by then. He was not a man who found words quickly, but he found them eventually, and she would be old enough to hold the weight of it.

He would tell her when she was older.

The field gate was where he had left it, the latch set but not locked. He lifted it carefully — the iron was cold enough to sting his palm — and passed through and set it back. The house was dark. No lamp in the window, no sound from inside. He stood for a moment in the yard, looking at the door.

He went inside.

The coals in the hearth were banked under a grey crust of ash, still orange at the center when he crouched and looked. He did not add wood. He did not want the light. He could hear his daughter breathing from the sleeping room — the slow, even pull and release of it, unchanged. He stood in the dark and listened to her breathe and felt the cold coming off his own skin and the smell of the mound still on his hands, the wet-ash smell, the smell of old organic things disturbed and then covered again.

He went to the basin and poured water from the clay jar and washed his hands. The water was cold enough to ache. He worked the soil from under his fingernails with the edge of his thumbnail and washed until the smell was mostly gone, though not entirely — the earth had been in the creases of his knuckles and it left a faint stain there, the color of old tea, that the cold water would not shift.

He dried his hands on the cloth by the basin and sat down on the mat.

The mat was woven from river grass, tight and even, the kind of work that took patience he had never had for it. Saya had made it the first winter, her fingers moving without looking, talking the whole time about something — he could not remember what. He had not been listening carefully enough. He had thought there would be more winters. He had thought he would have time to listen better.

He lay down on the mat and looked at the ceiling.

The thatch was dark above him, the smoke from months of cooking fires worked into every reed, the smell of it sweet and close and familiar in a way that was almost unbearable. He could hear the roof frame settling in the cold, the slow creak of it, the sound the house made when it thought no one was awake to hear.

He was not going to sleep. He knew this already.

He lay on his back with his hands at his sides and the cold coming up through the mat from the packed earth floor and he thought about the mound as it was now — the surface flat, the potsherd back in place, nothing to show for it. He thought about the bundle just below the surface, in the shallow bed of broken earth, the oilskin going slowly cold, the iron-gall ink on the laid paper fading from brown-black to something paler over years he would not live to see. He thought about the bead — small and blue, slightly pitted, cold and heavy for its size — lying in the center of the folded petition, in the dark, alone.

He had said the only true thing he could find, and it had felt like nothing, and now he was lying on a mat in the dark listening to his daughter breathe and the house creak and the night go slowly toward morning, and he was thinking: she will ask me, someday, where the bead is. She will ask because she knew her mother wore it. She will have noticed it was gone from his neck. Children noticed everything.

He would tell her when she was older.

He would tell her the whole of it — the petition, the assembly, the man in the large chair who had said *the matter will be considered* and meant nothing by it, and Saya walking two days to stand in that room and press her hand flat on the last page of a document that no one with power to act on it had read with any intention of acting. He would tell her that her mother had done all of this without complaint, without sentiment, without asking him whether it was worth it, because Saya had never wasted time on questions whose answers she already knew. He would tell her that the document was in the mound because the mound was the oldest thing on this ground and the oldest things lasted longest, and because he had not known what else to do with it, and because he had needed to put it somewhere that was not the fire.

He would tell her.

He lay in the dark and the stars outside moved toward morning and the cold deepened and the coals in the hearth went from orange to grey without his seeing it happen, and his daughter breathed in the next room, slow and even, and he did not sleep, and he did not tell her.

Not that night.

Not the next year, when she was five, because five was still too young.

Not the year after, when she was six, because there was never a moment that felt right, and the right moment kept moving forward in front of him like the horizon, always the same distance away.

He did not know, lying there in the dark with the smell of the mound still faint on his knuckles, that the moment would keep moving forward until it was out of reach entirely. He did not know that the silence he was choosing — *not yet, not yet, when she is older* — was its own kind of burial. That it would go into the ground with him when he died, and that his daughter would grow up knowing only that her mother had made something important and that it was gone, and not knowing where, and not knowing to ask, because he had never given her the question to carry.

He did not know any of this.

He lay on the mat and looked at the dark ceiling and listened to his daughter breathe and thought: *I will tell her when she is ready.*

Outside, the first bird called — a single note, flat and clear, from somewhere at the field's edge. Then silence. Then another bird, farther off, answering.

The night was ending.

He had not slept. He had not felt anything ceremonial. The bundle was in the ground, and the ground was unchanged, and his daughter was breathing, and Saya was still dead, and none of it was different from what it had been before he walked to the mound and back.

He got up.

He went to the hearth and crouched and blew on the coals until they caught, and fed small sticks to the flame until it was steady, and hung the clay pot over it with water for the morning. His hands moved through the familiar work without thought. The fire grew. The room lightened.

In the sleeping room, his daughter turned on her mat and made a small sound — not waking, just shifting — and he went still and listened until her breathing steadied again.

He stood in the firelight with his back to the hearth and looked at the door.

The candle had burned to a stub.

He had not noticed it go. He had come back from the mound and sat at the camp table and the candle was there, a finger's length of tallow with a small, steady flame, and now it was nearly nothing — a bead of light trembling on a wick that had almost no more wick to be.

Saya's quill lay on the table where she had left it. Not where he had put it. Where she had left it, months ago, the night she finished the second copy of the petition. He had not moved it. He had worked around it, eaten around it, lit the candle around it, and the quill had stayed where it was because moving it would have required him to decide what to do with it, and he had not been ready to decide that.

He picked it up now.

The shaft was thin and slightly curved, the feather stripped to a working length, the tip cut to a fine point that had gone slightly dry and splayed. She had cut it herself. She had a way with the knife — quick, confident, the cut always at the right angle — that he had never been able to match. He had watched her do it a dozen times and had never understood how she knew where to stop.

He held the quill and looked at the paper on the table.

There was nothing on it. He had put it there himself, weeks ago, thinking he might write something. He had not written anything. He did not know what he would have written or to whom, and the paper had stayed blank, and now in the last of the candlelight it was the color of old bone, its chain lines faintly visible, the surface slightly rough under his thumb when he pressed it.

He did not write anything now either.

He held the quill and looked at the paper and the candle shrank and the flame bent once, twice, and then went out.

The dark was immediate and total. The coals in the hearth had gone grey while he was at the mound. No moonlight reached the window. He sat in the complete dark and held the quill and could not see his own hands.

She knew.

The thought arrived without preamble, the way the truest things always did — not building, not announced, just suddenly present.

She had known the petition would fail.

He sat with it. He turned it over the way you turn a stone, looking for what is underneath.

She had known before she left. He understood that now. He had watched her copy the petition by candlelight, her hand steady, her face without expression, and he had thought she was focused, concentrated, holding herself together for the work. But it was not that. She had been past the question of whether it would work. She had already answered that question and set it aside, and what he had read as focus was something else — something he did not have a word for in any language he spoke. The willingness to do the necessary thing after you have understood that it will not save you.

She had walked two days.

She had stood in that room with the man in the large chair and the man with the well-fed face and the young clerk with ink on his fingers, and she had pressed her hand flat on the last page, and she had already known.

He sat in the dark and held the quill and did not know what to do with that.

He had spent the months since her death — since the fever took her in the cold of the previous winter, the sweating and the chills and then the stillness — he had spent those months angry at the assembly, at the man in the large chair, at the petition itself, at the distance between what Saya had built and what the world had been willing to give her for it. The anger had been useful. It had kept him moving. It had gotten him through the planting and the harvest and the first cold months and the night at the mound.

But she had known. She had known and she had gone anyway, and she had not told him, and now he was sitting in the dark with her quill in his hand and the anger had nowhere to go.

He was not angry at her. He could not be. He understood why she had not told him — she had known he would have tried to argue her out of going, and she had not had time for that argument, and she had been right not to have time for it. But understanding it did not make the dark any less dark.

The quill was smooth between his fingers. He turned it slowly, feeling the slight resistance where the stripped shaft caught the pad of his thumb.

She had made something that would outlast her. She had known it would fail and she had made it anyway, and then she had died, and he had put it in the ground because he did not know what else to do with it, and now he was sitting in the dark asking himself what it meant to make something you know will fail.

He did not have an answer.

He sat until the dark began to thin — not light yet, just the faint, grey loosening that comes before light, the moment when shapes return without color. The table. The door. His own hands.

He was still holding the quill.

From the sleeping room: a small sound. Not crying. Just a shift, a murmur, the sound of a child turning on her mat and finding the edge of waking.

Then: *Baba.*

He set the quill down on the blank paper. He pushed back from the table.

She called again, louder — *Baba* — and he heard in it the specific pitch that meant she was not frightened, only awake, only reaching for him the way she reached for him every morning, because every morning he was there.

He went to her.

She was sitting up on her mat with her cloth pulled around her shoulders and her hair pressed flat on one side from sleep. She looked up at him in the grey pre-dawn light with her mother's eyes — direct, already focused, already asking — and held up her arms.

He lifted her. She was warm and heavy with sleep, and she pressed her face against his neck, and he stood in the doorway between the rooms and held her and felt her breathing slow back toward calm.

She would ask him, someday, about her mother. She would ask what Saya had been like, and what she had done, and whether it had mattered. He would have to find the words for it. He would have to find a way to give her the question to carry, because the question was the only thing he had left to give — not the answer, which he did not have, but the question itself: *whether it is enough to make the necessary thing, knowing it will fail, knowing someone else will have to carry it forward, not knowing if anyone will.*

He did not know if he could find those words. He did not know if he was the right person to give them to her. Saya would have known how. Saya would have found the exact words and given them at the exact moment, and his daughter would have carried them without knowing she was carrying them, the way you carry the things that are given to you before you understand what they are.

He was not Saya.

But he was here. He was here and she was breathing against his neck and outside the first birds were starting — one, then another, then several, the morning filling with sound the way mornings did, indifferent to everything that had happened in the dark.

He did not know if anyone would find what he had buried.

He did not know if it would be enough.

He stood in the doorway with his daughter in his arms and the day beginning outside and those two questions sitting in him like stones, heavy and unresolved, and he understood — not with hope, not with certainty, but with the particular exhausted clarity that comes at the end of a sleepless night — that they would stay unanswered. That he would carry them for the rest of his life without resolution, the way you carry the weight of the things you have done in the dark when no one was watching, not knowing if they mattered, not knowing if anyone would ever come to find out.

His daughter lifted her head and looked at him.

"Hungry," she said.

"Yes," he said. "Me too."

He carried her to the hearth.
