The mound rose out of the morning fog like a held breath.

Tessa stood at its eastern edge where the ground softened into the creek bank, her moccasins dark with dew, and looked up at the crest she could not quite see through the gray. Somewhere above her, a crow called once and fell silent. The river smell was thick — mud and rotting leaves and the cold mineral edge of water moving fast after three days of rain.

She had been here before dawn so that no one would see her come.

Matta had been in the ground eleven days. She had not visited the mound since the burial, and now that she was here she understood why she had stayed away: she was afraid. Not of the dead — she had never been afraid of the dead — but of herself. Of the gap where his voice had been.

She closed her eyes and began.

*Eastern face, first depth: Okwi, who carried the petition. A woman. Laid on her right side, head toward the water.*

She had the words. Good. She moved them forward.

*Beside Okwi: the bead, cobalt, her mother's mother's mother's. Placed at the left hand.*

She had that too. She breathed.

The fog shifted and a wedge of pale light fell across the mound's slope, catching the wet grass so that each blade held its own small light. Tessa opened her eyes and looked at it. Matta had taught her to look at the mound as a living thing — not dead ground but accumulated attention, every layer the result of someone deciding that this place mattered enough to carry clay and river stone up a slope and press it into the earth with their hands.

*Second depth, center: a man whose name the chain does not hold. Before Okwi's time. Wrapped in bark.*

She had that.

*Third depth—*

She stopped. Her chest tightened.

Third depth. She had rehearsed this alone in the dark of the sleeping room three nights running, lying on her back with her eyes open, moving through the map the way Matta had moved through it — unhurried, each name given its full weight before the next. But now, standing at the actual ground with the actual fog and the crow's single call still hanging in the air, the third depth came up blank.

Not entirely blank. She had the direction — head toward the high ground, south-facing. She had the object — a pipe bowl, dark-fired, left at the right shoulder. But the name. The name.

*A woman*, she thought. *Older than Okwi. Before the petition. Before the English came in numbers.*

She pressed her palm flat against the slope of the mound, feeling the wet grass bend under her hand, the soil cool and slightly yielding beneath. Matta had done this. She had watched him do it a hundred times, palm flat, eyes closed, as if the ground would remind him of what he had put there.

It did not work for her. She was not Matta. She was twenty years old and she had been taught this map the way you are taught a song — by hearing it, by repeating it, by being corrected when you got a syllable wrong. Now there was no one to correct her.

*The woman at the third depth*, she told herself. *Think.*

A sound reached her from the direction of the path — footsteps, someone moving through the undergrowth without particular care, branches pushed aside and released. Tessa lifted her hand from the mound and stood straight. Her heartbeat was loud in her ears.

It was only a deer. It came out of the tree line twenty yards south, saw her, and stood very still for a moment with its ears forward. Then it turned and walked back into the trees with the unhurried dignity of something that had decided she was not worth alarm.

She let out a slow breath.

She was alone. She was the only person alive who knew what was in this ground.

The thought arrived without drama, and it was worse for that. Matta had known. Matta had held the map in his head for forty years, adding to it, checking it against the stories that came to him through the chain — through his granddaughter, through the women who had told her, through the long backward line of voices going all the way to the first burial. He had checked his memory against theirs and theirs against his, and in the checking the map had stayed sharp.

Now there was only Tessa, and no one to check against.

She turned back to the mound and pressed her palm to it again. The wet grass. The cool earth. She made herself be still.

*The woman at the third depth.* She had a name that Matta had said was old even in the chain's earliest tellings, old enough that its meaning had been partly lost. He had said it carefully, the way you say a word in a language you are not certain of. He had said: *this is the name we were given for her; we do not know if it is the name she called herself.*

And then he had said the name.

Tessa waited. The fog was lifting now, pulling back from the mound's crest so that she could see the top — a rounded shape against the pale sky, grass-covered, unremarkable to anyone who did not know what it held. A crow landed at the crest and walked a few steps and flew off again.

The name came back.

Not all at once. It came the way a fire catches — a small thing first, then the rest following. She had it. She had the whole name, and with it the rest of the third depth fell into place: the direction, the object, the depth below the surface in hand-lengths, the way Matta had described the clay around that layer as darker than the others, almost black, as if the ground itself had marked the spot.

She exhaled and began again from the beginning, moving through the map from east to west, top to bottom, name by name.

By the time she reached the deepest layer — the copper pendant, the first burial, the woman whose name the chain held as the beginning — the sun had cleared the tree line and the fog was gone. Her feet were wet through to the skin. Her legs ached from standing still on the slope.

She did not move.

She finished the map. Every name. Every depth. Every object placed at every shoulder or hip or hand.

Then she stood quietly for a moment with her palm still against the mound's side.

She did not know if she had it right. That was the truth of it, and she was old enough now to look at the truth without flinching. She might have one name slightly wrong. She might have a depth off by a hand-length. Matta was not here to tell her. His granddaughter, who had told Matta, was long dead. The chain went back further than Tessa could see, voice to voice to voice, and somewhere in that long passage things had certainly been lost or shifted — Matta himself had said so, had said *we carry what we can carry and we name what we can name and we are honest about the difference.*

She would carry what she could carry.

She would come back tomorrow at dawn, and the day after, and she would recite the map again until the names were as automatic as breathing, until she could say them in her sleep, until the day came — if it came — when there was someone she could trust enough to teach.

She lifted her hand from the mound. The impression of her palm remained in the wet grass for a moment, then the grass straightened.

She turned and walked back toward the tree line, her moccasins leaving dark prints in the dew-heavy ground. She did not look back. She had learned that from Matta too: you do not look back at the mound when you leave. You carry it with you instead.

Behind her, the crow returned to the crest and called once, sharply, into the brightening air.

The clay bench was Matta's — a split log set on two forked stakes driven into the earth floor of the small outbuilding he had used for his work. He had smoothed the top surface with a river stone until it was almost glassy, and in twelve years of use it had taken on a faint grey sheen from the clay dust worked into the grain. Tessa had not touched it since the morning they buried him.

She set her basket down beside it now and stood for a moment looking at the bench the way you look at something you have been avoiding. The clay she had dug from the stream bank two days before sat in a damp bundle wrapped in deerskin, already kneaded once to pull out the largest stones and the roots. Matta had always kneaded twice. She unwrapped it and began working it again with the heels of her hands, pressing and folding, pressing and folding, feeling for the small hard places that would crack a bowl in the fire.

Outside, a crow was making noise in the oaks. The light through the outbuilding's single gap in the wall — not a window, just a gap where a log had never been fitted — was thin and white, the kind of light that gave no warmth.

She had watched Matta make pipes her whole life. She knew the shape of his hands on the clay, the way he pinched up the walls with two fingers and a thumb, turning the bowl slowly. She knew he tempered the clay with crushed shell — she had the shell, ground fine between two stones the way he had shown her — and she knew he fired the finished bowls in the coals at the edge of the hearth, not in the center, where the heat was too sudden. She knew all of this the way she knew the map: by watching and listening and holding it in her memory. What she did not know was whether knowing was the same as doing.

She shaped the first bowl slowly. It took her longer than it had taken Matta — she could feel herself being too careful, pressing too lightly, and then correcting by pressing too hard. The walls came out uneven. She smoothed them with a wet finger, working around the rim, and the shape steadied into something that looked, from a certain angle, like what it was supposed to be. She set it on a flat piece of bark to dry and did not let herself look at it again.

The second she made faster, trying to trust her hands. It came out rounder than the first, the walls more even. She pressed a small line of incised marks around the base the way Matta had done — not decoration exactly, more like a signature, a way of saying *this bowl was made with intention* — using the sharpened tip of a deer rib. When she was done she set it beside the first and looked at them both. They looked like pipe bowls. Whether they were pipe bowls — whether they would hold smoke without cracking, whether the clay and shell had been worked together in the right proportion, whether the walls were thick enough or too thick — she did not know. There was no one to ask.

She mixed a small amount of the powdered shell into the remaining clay and worked it through with her fingers, feeling for the gritty resistance that meant the temper was distributed. Matta had said: *you will know when it feels right.* She had been twelve years old when he said it and she had nodded as if she understood. She had not understood. She understood now that *right* was a thing you learned by firing bowls that cracked, and she was not yet past that part of the learning.

She shaped the third bowl and set it with the others and spent the rest of the morning on her other work — the corn that needed sorting, the firewood that needed splitting at the far end of the yard, the small repairs to the bark panels on the storage shed that she had been putting off since the first cold rain. She did not think about the bowls. Matta had told her not to watch clay dry. *It does not dry faster for being watched,* he had said, *and watching makes you want to interfere.* She left them alone.

In the late afternoon she built up the hearth fire and let it burn down to coals, then raked the coals to the edges and set the three bowls in the cleared center on a flat stone, close enough to the heat to dry thoroughly but not close enough for the temperature to rise too fast. Matta had said the firing had to be slow at the start. She had heard him say it a hundred times. She sat back on her heels and watched the coals pulse orange in the dimming light and tried not to listen for the sound she did not want to hear.

She heard it twice.

The first crack came from the left-hand bowl — the uneven one — about an hour into the firing, a small sharp sound like a knuckle popping. She did not move. There was no point in moving. When the coals had cooled enough to touch she reached in and lifted the bowl and felt the crack running from the base up one side, splitting the wall cleanly. She set it down outside the door. The clay was still warm.

The second crack came from the rounder bowl near midnight, while she was half-asleep by the fire. She heard it the way you hear things when you are not quite awake — the sound arriving before she could decide whether she had heard it. She sat up and waited and did not go to check until morning. When she did, the bowl had split at the base, a long diagonal fracture that opened the bottom into two pieces. She sat looking at it for a while with her hands on her knees.

The third bowl was whole.

She lifted it carefully, turning it in the light from the gap in the wall. The clay had fired to a pale grey-brown, darker near the base where the heat had been highest, and the incised marks around the rim had come through clean. The walls were thick — thicker than Matta's bowls, she thought, though she was not certain, because she was comparing it to a memory and memories of proportion were unreliable. She pressed her thumb against the side. It did not flex. It felt solid, the way fired clay should feel, with a faint roughness from the shell temper.

She did not know if it was right.

That was still the truth of it, and she sat with the bowl in her hands and let the truth settle. Matta would have looked at it and said something — would have turned it over, pressed the walls, held it up to check the rim was level — and his face would have told her before his words did. She had read his face her whole life without knowing she was doing it, and now the face was gone and she was reading nothing but her own uncertainty, which was not a reliable text.

She set the bowl on the bench.

It was a pipe bowl. It had been made from clay dug at the stream bank, tempered with crushed shell, shaped by hand, fired in hearth coals. It had not cracked. Whether it was a *good* pipe bowl — whether Matta would have approved it for use in the ceremonies, whether the proportions were right, whether the walls would hold up over years of use and smoke and handling — she could not know. What she knew was that she had made it, and that it was the only one she had, and that the ceremonies required a bowl, and that the ceremonies would come whether she was ready or not.

She picked up the two broken ones from outside the door and carried them to the midden at the edge of the yard and set them down in the leaves and dirt with the other broken things. Then she came back and stood in the doorway of the outbuilding and looked at the bench, at the single grey-brown bowl sitting in the thin light, at the smooth surface Matta had worked to a shine with a river stone over twelve years of use.

*You will know when it feels right.*

She had not known. She had made three bowls and two had cracked and one had not, and she did not know if the one that held had held because she had done something correctly or because of luck or because the clay from that particular section of the stream bank had a quality she had not chosen deliberately. She might never know. The bowl sat there regardless, solid and imperfect and hers.

She picked it up and carried it inside and wrapped it in a piece of clean deerskin and set it on the shelf above the hearth where Matta had kept his finished work. Then she sat down by the fire and looked at the shelf for a long time.

The crow was back in the oaks. She could hear it calling, the same sharp note repeated at uneven intervals, the sound carrying easily through the thin walls of the outbuilding and the bare November trees. Somewhere to the south, faint and intermittent, came the sound of an axe — someone splitting wood at one of the English farms along the lower road. The sound stopped and started and stopped again.

She had gotten one bowl from three attempts. Matta, in his first year, had gotten how many? She did not know. He had never said. She had never thought to ask, because she had never imagined she would need to know. There were a hundred things she had never thought to ask, and now the asking was over, and she would spend the rest of her life learning what she had not thought to learn when she had the chance.

She put more wood on the fire.

Tomorrow she would dig more clay.

The ceremony fell in early spring, when the ground had thawed enough to walk without breaking through ice at every step but the mud still held the shape of her moccasin prints behind her. Tessa had watched the timing the way Matta had taught her — not by counting days but by the red-winged blackbirds returning to the marsh below the creek, and by the smell of the soil when she pushed her fingers into it at the mound's base, that particular cold-wet-rot smell that meant the earth was moving again after winter.

She came alone before first light, carrying the pipe bowl wrapped in deerskin and a small pouch of tobacco she had kept through the winter in a twist of bark. The trail through the oaks was soft underfoot, the leaf mold spongy and dark. Somewhere behind her a crow called twice and went silent. She did not look back.

The mound rose out of the grey half-dark the way it always did — not suddenly but gradually, its shape becoming distinct from the surrounding trees as she came closer, the way a face becomes distinct when you have been looking at it long enough. She stopped at the base and stood still for a moment. The sky above the ridge was the color of ash, lightening at the edges.

She set the deerskin bundle down at the southern foot of the mound, the place Matta had always set things, and she unwrapped the bowl and set it on the flat stone he had positioned there years ago for exactly this purpose. The stone was half-buried in leaf mold now. She cleared the leaves away with her hands, pushing them back until the stone was clean.

Then she straightened and began.

The perimeter walk went sun-wise. Matta had said this so many times that she heard his voice now as she turned east, his dry unhurried voice that never seemed to come from anywhere in particular, just arrived in the air near her. *Sun-wise. Always sun-wise. The names go with the sun.*

She walked and she spoke the names.

The names were not a list. They were a map — each name placed at a specific point in the circuit, corresponding to where that person's remains lay within the mound, and the order of the names followed the order of the burials, oldest first, working inward. Matta had spent three winters teaching her the map, testing her at odd moments — in the middle of grinding corn, in the middle of crossing the creek — asking her to recite from a given name forward or backward without warning, so that the sequence would not live only in the calm of ceremony but in the muscle of habit.

She had thought she knew it.

She spoke the oldest names first, the names she had always found easiest because they were the most worn, the most practiced, the ones Matta himself had recited so many times that they had a groove in her memory like a path through grass. Yanu. Ranu. The daughter whose name had come down only as a sound, two syllables, the second one uncertain. She spoke it the way Matta had spoken it, with the second syllable rising slightly, acknowledging the uncertainty rather than papering over it with false confidence.

She walked the eastern face of the mound, where the soil was darker from the runoff of a small spring above. The oaks were putting out the first pale buds, barely visible in the dim light, and the ground at the mound's base was thick with last year's leaves, not yet broken down. She brushed them aside with her foot as she walked, not wanting to slip.

She came around to the north face and the names moved with her — the later names now, the ones from the generations closer to her own time, the ones she had known less time and therefore trusted less. This was where Matta had always slowed down, she remembered. He had moved through the oldest names with a kind of ease, but here in the middle of the sequence he had always walked more deliberately, as though the ground were different.

She was speaking the name of a woman three generations before Matta's grandmother when she heard herself say it wrong.

She stopped.

The name she had just spoken — she had placed it two positions too early. She had spoken it in the slot that belonged to the woman's mother, and the woman's mother's name had not yet been spoken, and now she was standing on the north face of the mound with the sequence broken.

Her first thought was: *Matta would have caught it immediately.*

Her second thought was: *No one is here to catch it.*

Her third thought, arriving so quickly behind the second that they were almost the same thought: *That is not the point. The point is whether the names are right.*

She stopped walking. She stood on the north face of the mound with the tobacco pouch in her left hand and the cold air coming off the creek below and she made herself be still.

She could keep going. She could tell herself the error was small, that the woman whose name she had displaced would understand, that the intent was what mattered. She had heard people say things like that. She did not believe them. The map was the map. The names were where they were because that was where the people were, and the order was the order because the order was the truth of the sequence, and if she smoothed over the error with good intention she would be doing something that looked like the ceremony and was not the ceremony.

She backed up.

Not one name. She went back four names, to the last point where she was certain — the name of the man who had come to the mound from the north, the one whose name Matta had always said with a slight pause before it, as though bracing — and she began again from there.

Her voice shook.

She had not expected that. She had thought the shaking would be in her hands, the way it had been when she fired the bowl, but it was in her voice — a fine trembling that she could hear and could not stop, the way you cannot stop a hand from trembling by watching it. She kept speaking. She placed the names in their correct order, the mother before the daughter, the sequence restored, and she walked the north face again and came around to the west.

The sky had lightened to a pale yellow-grey above the ridge. A pair of crows moved through the bare oaks to her left, their wingbeats audible in the quiet.

She finished the circuit at the southern stone where she had begun. Her legs were steady. Her voice was still shaking faintly when she spoke the last name — Matta, placed here not because she had chosen it but because he was the most recent, and the most recent went last, closest to the living — and the shaking did not stop when she finished speaking. It continued for a moment after the last syllable, like a branch still moving after the wind has passed.

She crouched at the stone and filled the bowl with a pinch of tobacco from the pouch — not much, because she had not much to spare, and Matta had always said a small offering made with care was worth more than a large one made carelessly — and she lit it from the flint and steel she carried in her belt pouch, cupping her hands around the bowl to protect the coal from the morning air. The tobacco caught, and she watched the smoke rise, thin and pale, breaking apart almost immediately in the light wind off the creek.

She did not know if she had done it correctly. She had made an error and corrected it, and the correction had been made alone, with no one to confirm that the correction was right and the error truly identified. What if she had backed up to the wrong point? What if the error was earlier than she thought, and she had corrected one mistake while carrying another forward?

She could not know. That was the fact of it. She could not know, and she could not ask, and she could not go back to the moment before Matta was gone and ask him to test her one more time.

The smoke from the bowl thinned and went out. She sat back on her heels and looked at the mound in the growing light — the rounded shape of it, the pale grass of last year still visible through the new growth at its base, the way the soil at the top was darker than the sides, holding moisture from the winter. She had walked its perimeter since she was old enough to follow Matta without being carried, and she knew its shape the way she knew the shape of her own hands.

She wrapped the bowl back in the deerskin and tied it carefully.

She had made an error. She had caught it. She had gone back and corrected it. The names were in their right order now, or she believed they were — and belief was not certainty, but it was what she had, and it was what the ceremony required her to carry forward from this morning into the next season and the next, alone, with the shaking in her voice and the tobacco smoke already gone into the air above the ridge.

She picked up the bundle and stood.

The blackbirds were calling from the marsh below. She could hear them clearly now — the liquid, three-note call, the one that meant spring had come as far as it was willing to come — and she stood at the base of the mound and listened to them for a moment before she turned and walked back through the oaks toward home.

He was waiting at her fire.

She saw him before she reached the clearing — the set of his shoulders, the coat he wore now, dark wool with pewter buttons, the kind a man bought from a colonial trader when he wanted to be read a certain way at a distance. He was standing with his back to her, looking at the coals, and she knew from the way he stood that he had been there long enough to grow impatient.

She did not slow her pace.

"Joseph," she said, because that was what he answered to now.

He turned. His face was the same face — the same jaw, the same line of the brow — but he had cut his hair short at the sides in the English fashion, and the absence of it changed him in a way she had not yet grown used to, though she had had two years to try.

"You were at the mound," he said.

"Yes."

"Before dawn."

"The ceremony doesn't wait for convenient light."

He looked at the bundle under her arm — the deerskin-wrapped pipe bowl — and his expression did not change, which meant it had already changed before she arrived and settled into the shape he intended to show her.

"Sit," she said. "I'll put water on."

"I'm not staying."

She set the bundle down on the flat stone beside the fire pit and crouched to add wood to the coals anyway. The coals caught the new wood slowly — the night had been damp — and she fed it small pieces first, the dry ones she kept under the bark overhang. He watched her do this.

"There are men coming," he said. "English soldiers. A road through the western hills — they're cutting it now, toward the Ohio. You can hear the axes some mornings if the wind is right."

"I've heard them."

"Then you know what comes after the axes."

She looked up at him. "I know what comes after the axes."

"Then stop." He said it quietly, which was worse than if he had said it loudly. "Stop going to the mound. Stop doing the ceremony. Stop keeping accounts of who is buried where and what their names were. Someone will notice, Tessa. Someone will ask what you're doing out there before dawn, alone, with a pipe and a pouch of tobacco, walking in circles around a pile of dirt."

"It is not a pile of dirt."

"To them it is."

She fed another piece of wood to the fire. "Then it matters what it is to me."

"It matters what they decide it is." He crouched on the other side of the fire, and for a moment he looked like himself again — like the boy who had sat across from her at a hundred fires, who had known the names she was learning now before he chose to set them down. "You are twenty years old. You are alone out here. Matta is gone, and there is no one left who does what you do, and if someone reports you to the wrong magistrate —"

"Reports me for what? Walking on land we have walked for a hundred years?"

"For practicing heathen rites." He said the words cleanly, without flinching, which told her he had rehearsed them. "That is what they will call it. That is the word they use."

The fire had caught properly now. She sat back and looked at him across it.

"Is that what you call it?" she said.

A muscle moved in his jaw. "I call it dangerous."

"You call it heathen. I heard you."

"I am trying to keep you alive."

"You are trying to keep yourself comfortable." She said it without heat, which was the only way to say a thing like that and have it land. "You have made a calculation. I understand the calculation. You decided that the safest thing was to become legible to them — to take their name, wear their coat, sit in their church and say their words. And maybe you are right. Maybe that is how you survive." She picked up the deerskin bundle and held it in her lap. "But you are not surviving. You are disappearing. And you want me to disappear with you so that you do not have to watch."

He stood up. "You are stubborn."

"Yes."

"You are going to get yourself killed."

"Perhaps."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the only honest one." She looked up at him — the pewter buttons, the cut hair, the face that was still her brother's face underneath all of it. "If I stop going to the mound, the names are lost. If the names are lost, the land is lost — not because someone takes it, but because we forget we were ever there. The land does not know we loved it if we stop saying so."

"The land doesn't know anything. It's dirt."

"Then why are they cutting a road through it?"

He had no answer for that. She had known he would have no answer for that, and she felt no satisfaction in it — only the particular tiredness of winning an argument with someone you love.

"They are going to win," he said. "The English. Or the French. One of them. And whichever one wins, they will not ask us which names we kept or what ceremonies we performed. They will ask how useful we are."

"And I am asking how much of yourself you will sell to be useful."

The word landed. She watched it land.

He pulled his coat straight — a gesture she recognized, the one he used when he was about to leave a conversation he had lost — and he looked at her for a moment with something that was not quite anger and not quite grief and was probably both.

"I won't come back," he said. "If you keep doing this, I won't come back."

She looked at the fire. "I know."

He left. She listened to his footsteps through the wet leaves, the sound of them growing smaller and then gone, and then there was only the fire and the blackbirds starting up again from the marsh, and the distant, irregular sound she had been hearing for three weeks now on mornings when the wind came from the west — the sound of axes in the western hills, working through timber that had stood for longer than any of them had been alive, making a road for men who did not know whose ground they were crossing and would not have stopped if they had known.

She set the bundle on her knees and untied the deerskin. The pipe bowl sat in her palm, cool and smooth, the clay the color of river silt in shade. She turned it once, checking the surface the way Matta had taught her — running her thumb along the rim, feeling for any crack she might have missed in the firing.

It was sound.

She wrapped it again and tied the cord. She would not see her brother for three years. She did not know that yet, but she knew something like it — the way you know a winter will be hard before the first snow falls, from the way the animals are moving and the way the light sits on the water in the afternoons.

She fed the last of the small wood to the fire and sat with her hands around her knees, listening to the axes.

---

Three years is not a long time if you count it in seasons. It is very long if you count it in fires.

She counted it in fires.

She fired the pipe bowls in the same hollow where Matta had fired them, in a pit she had dug deeper than his to shelter the coals from the wind that came off the river in autumn. She had learned the depth by ruining two bowls the first winter — pulling them too soon, finding hairline cracks along the rim where the cold air had reached the clay before it was ready. After that she waited longer. She learned to read the color of the coals rather than the time she had been sitting with them, and the bowls came out sound.

She made six that first year. Four the second. Seven the third, because by then her hands knew the clay the way her feet knew the path to the mound — without thought, without hesitation, the knowledge living in the muscle rather than the mind.

The mound she visited in every season. She did not dig. She walked the perimeter the way Matta had walked it, beginning at the eastern face where the ground rose first, moving with the sun, her lips moving with the names. Matta's mother's mother's name. The names before that, the ones that had come down through Matta to her, each one attached to a marker in the ground — the root-heave at the north face, the place where a beech had fallen and left a low depression, the flat stone at the south that sat slightly proud of the earth as though it were trying to surface. She touched the stone each time. She did not know why Matta had touched it. She touched it because he had.

In summer the mound smelled of warm grass and something underneath the grass — a darkness, a depth, a coolness rising through the heat. In autumn it smelled of leaf mold and the particular sweetness of hickory nuts that had fallen and begun to rot in the undergrowth. In winter the ground was hard and the names came out in small clouds of breath, and she recited them faster to keep her feet moving, and the crows watched her from the bare oaks with their heads tilted at the angle of creatures who understand more than they let on.

In spring the mound was soft underfoot, and once she sank to her ankle in a low place on the western face and had to pull herself free with both hands, and she stood there in the mud and laughed — a short, startled sound that surprised her — and then she finished the circuit and said the names and went home.

She did not see her brother.

She heard about him. A woman who came to trade dried corn for a length of stroud cloth mentioned that a man matching his description had been seen at the Anglican mission three miles south of the settlement, that he had read scripture aloud to the congregation and that the minister had praised his English. She said this without particular feeling, the way you report weather. Tessa thanked her for the corn and did not ask anything further.

Another time, a man passing through on his way north said he had worked alongside a Piscataway man who went by the name Joseph, who was skilled with timber framing and had helped build a granary for a colonial family near the creek. Good man, the traveler said. Quiet. Knows his business. Tessa said that was good to hear. The traveler moved on.

She thought about him on the long evenings in the second winter, when the fire was the only warm thing and the wind pushed through the gaps in the wall she kept meaning to fill. She thought about what he had said — *they will ask how useful we are* — and she thought about what she had said in return, and she still believed what she had said, and she still felt the particular tiredness of it, the way a true thing can be exhausting to carry.

The axes had stopped in the autumn of the first year, when the cold came hard and early. They started again the following spring, closer. By the second summer she could hear them on still mornings without needing the wind to carry the sound, and once she climbed to the high ground east of the mound and looked west through the trees and saw the pale gash of raw timber where the road was coming through, the cut wood bright as bone against the dark of the standing forest. She stood and looked at it for a long time. Then she went back down and finished the circuit and said the names.

The road was for soldiers. She understood that. The news that came through in the summer of the second year — passed from mouth to mouth along the trading paths, arriving in fragments that she assembled like shards — was that the English general with the red coats had marched his army west toward the French fort at the forks of the Ohio, and that the army had been met in the forest and broken. She heard numbers that seemed impossible: hundreds dead, officers cut down, the general himself wounded and dying. She heard that the Shawnee and Delaware had fought alongside the French. She heard that the road the English had cut through the forest had not saved them.

She thought about that for a long time. She thought about what her brother had said — *whichever one wins, they will not ask us which names we kept* — and she thought that he was probably right about that too, and that it did not change what she was doing, and that this was the difficulty of the situation: two true things that did not resolve each other, that simply sat side by side like stones in a wall, each one necessary, neither one sufficient.

She fired a bowl that autumn that came out darker than the others — the clay had more iron in it from the bank where she had dug, or the fire had burned hotter, she was not sure — and the color was nearly black, the surface smooth and close-grained, and when she held it to the light it had a faint sheen like river water in shadow. She kept it separate from the others. She did not know yet what it was for.

---

The third year she saw him on the road.

It was late in the season — the leaves mostly down, the air carrying the first real cold, the kind that settles in the low places near water and does not lift until midmorning. She was coming back from the mound, the deerskin bundle under her arm, her moccasins wet from the grass. He was coming from the direction of the settlement, walking with his head down, his breath making small clouds.

She saw him before he saw her. She had time to take in the coat — heavier now, better quality, dark wool with brass buttons that caught the flat light — and the way he walked, which was the same as it had always been, the slight forward lean, the long stride. The walk was his. Everything else was a decision he had made.

Then he looked up and saw her.

She watched his face. She watched the recognition move through it, and then something after the recognition — a quick, complicated thing, there and gone in less than a breath — and then he looked away.

Not away to the side. Down. He looked at the road in front of him and kept walking.

She kept walking too. They passed each other at perhaps four feet of distance. She could hear his footsteps on the packed earth. She could see, in the edge of her vision, the brass buttons, the cut of the coat across his shoulders. She did not turn her head.

When she could no longer hear his footsteps she stopped and stood still for a moment, the bundle under her arm, the cold air on her face.

She had expected to feel the tiredness again — the particular tiredness of winning an argument with someone you love. But what she felt was different. It was quieter than that. It was the feeling of a thing that has been decided without anyone deciding it, the way a river finds its channel not through intention but through the slow, patient pressure of water against earth over years.

He had looked away first. She did not know what that meant to him. She knew what it meant to her: that the argument was over, not because either of them had won it, but because it had run its full length and come to rest. He had made his calculation. She had made hers. They had both been right about some things and wrong about others, and the world had not resolved the question for them — the English were still here, the French were still here, the road was still being cut through the old timber — and she was still walking the mound's perimeter in every season, and he was still sitting in the church and reading their words back to them, and both of those things were true and neither of them was finished.

She shifted the bundle to her other arm and started walking again.

The mound was behind her now. She had said all the names. The bowl she had wrapped in the deerskin — the dark one, the one she had kept separate — was sound. She had checked the rim with her thumb before she left, the way Matta had taught her, and it had no cracks.

She did not know yet who it was for. She thought she would know when the time came, the way you know a winter will be hard before the first snow, from the way the light sits on the water.

The road ahead of her curved into the trees. Somewhere to the west, too far to hear, the axes were working again in timber that had stood longer than any of them had been alive. She walked toward home, and the cold settled in around her, and the crows lifted from the oaks along the creek and crossed the pale sky, calling to each other in the language they had always used, which no one had ever needed to teach them and no one had ever managed to take away.
