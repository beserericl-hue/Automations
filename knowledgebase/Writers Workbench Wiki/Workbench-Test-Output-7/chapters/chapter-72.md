The voicemail ran forty-three seconds. Wren had timed it on the second listen.

*Ms. Calvert, this is Lester Pruitt calling from Holloway and Breck in Prince Frederick. We've been retained in connection with a quiet title inquiry touching on the 1687 Calvert land patent in Charles County, and I understand you may have some familiarity both with that document and with the excavation currently underway at the high ground site near Mattawoman Creek. I'd welcome a brief conversation at your earliest convenience. My direct number is, *

She lowered the phone. Through the windshield, the site road ran straight between two stands of loblolly pine, the trunks dark with last night's damp, and at its far end she could see the orange survey flags snapping in the November wind. The trench was down there. Tayak was down there. The thirty-day clock had been running for nine days.

Wren had parked at the edge of the field road where the ruts from Dawes's truck had dried into ridges hard enough to jar her axle. She had pulled over the moment she recognized the 301 area code, because she had been expecting it since her mother called three weeks ago to say a firm in Prince Frederick had been asking questions about her grandmother's estate papers. She had wanted to be stopped, not moving, when it came.

She called back.

"Pruitt." He answered on the second ring, which meant he had been waiting.

"This is Wren Calvert. You left me a voicemail."

"Ms. Calvert." The warmth in his voice was immediate and practiced. It was the warmth of a man who had learned that warmth costs nothing and closes distance fast. "Thank you for returning my call. I appreciate it. I wanted to reach out personally, before this becomes, "

"Mr. Pruitt." She kept her voice level. "I'm going to save us some time. I know what the inquiry is. I know who your client is. And I know what you're hoping I'll say."

A brief pause. "I'm not sure I follow."

"You want to know whether I'll confirm the patent's chain of title and whether my presence on the excavation was connected to the Calvert family's interest in the land. You want me to say I was there to protect something."

"Ms. Calvert, I simply want to understand, "

"I wasn't." She watched a red-tailed hawk cross the gap between the pine stands, low and unhurried, and drop out of sight behind the tree line. "I took the field position because I'm an archaeologist and the work needed doing. The patent is real. The boundary description is real. I found it in my grandmother's estate papers and I understood what it meant. That's exactly why I'm not going to help you."

The warmth went out of his voice the way heat goes out of a stone after dark. Not suddenly, but completely.

"I see. And what is it you intend to do?"

"I intend to provide my field documentation to the tribal legal team. Site records, stratigraphic notes, artifact logs. Everything I've produced in four months on site."

"That documentation was produced in the course of your employment. There may be questions about who holds the right to, "

"I've already spoken to the project PI about data-sharing." That was not entirely true. She had drafted the email to Tayak twice and not sent it. But she would. "The records are contemporaneous, signed, and dated. They'll stand."

"Ms. Calvert." His voice had gone to something carefully neutral, the voice of a man reading from a script he had used before. "I want to be direct with you. If you provide documentation that is later used to cloud the Calvert title, you may be creating a personal liability. That's not a threat. It's a professional courtesy."

Wren looked down at her hands on the steering wheel. Her knuckles were chapped from four months of nitrile gloves and cold water, the skin at her right index finger cracked at the joint where she gripped her trowel. She had spent four months in that trench. She had found a glass bead and a folded document and a shell gorget and a copper pendant and a dozen other things she had drawn and logged and catalogued with the same careful handwriting she had been using since her first field season. She had found a partial name in a water-damaged folio, *Sioſ, *, the rest eaten by time, and had written *possibly female* in the margin of her notebook in pencil. She had known then that she was not going to pretend she didn't know what she knew.

"I understand," she said.

"I'd encourage you to consult an attorney before you, "

"I understand." She ended the call.

The hawk was gone. The orange flags snapped once, twice, in a gust that rocked the truck on its springs and then died. The pine trees stood very still after it.

She sat for a moment with the phone face-down on her thigh. The mineral smell of the trench came through the truck's heating vents, or she imagined it did. Damp clay and old root-rot and something underneath both that she had no name for, something that had been in the ground long enough to become part of the ground's own smell.

Then she picked up the phone and called her mother.

Her mother answered on the fourth ring with the particular silence that meant she had been watching the screen and deciding.

"He called you," her mother said. It was not a question.

"I just got off with him."

"And?"

"I told him I wasn't going to help. And there's something I need to tell you." Wren's voice was steady. "I should have said it two years ago. I should have said it the day I found the estate papers."

The silence on the other end was long enough that Wren could hear the television in the background, a news program, the specific cadence of someone speaking about something urgent in a measured voice.

"Wren." Her mother's voice had the quality it always had when she was about to say something she had been holding for weeks. "That land has been in this family for, "

"It hasn't." She said it quietly, without heat. "That's the point. It was never ours to begin with. Grandmother knew it. She read the boundary description and she set it aside and didn't tell anyone. I found it in the estate box. I've known for two years, and I took the field position knowing, and I told myself I was there for the work. That was true and it wasn't all of it."

"She set it aside because there was nothing to be done about it."

"There is now." Her voice had gone very quiet. "Since the day grandmother read that boundary description and set it aside, and I was eight years old and I watched her do it and I didn't understand what I was seeing. Then I grew up and I understood and I still didn't say anything. That's twenty years of knowing and not saying. I'm trying to stop."

"You're going to give away everything your grandmother, "

"Mom. I'm not giving anything away. I'm a field archaeologist. I'm giving them my field notes. That's all I have to give."

"Pruitt's firm will come after you."

"Maybe."

"You'll lose your position."

"Maybe."

"You don't have the right to make that decision for this family."

Something moved in Wren's chest, a pressure she recognized from the trench. It was the feeling just before the trowel found something, when the soil changed texture under the blade and she knew to slow down. She had been carrying this for two years. She had carried it through the application for the field position, through the first week on site when she had told herself she was there for the work, through the morning she found the cobalt bead and knew she was lying to herself about why she was there.

"The 1687 patent uses the mound as a survey marker," Wren said. "The boundary description names the Piscataway settlement at the high ground. Whoever drew that patent knew exactly what was there and granted it anyway." She kept her voice level. "Grandmother found that. She read it and she set it aside and she said nothing. I found it after she died. And I've been doing the same thing she did. That's what I'm stopping."

"That is the most self-righteous, "

"It's not righteous. It's just late."

"You are ungrateful," her mother said. "You have always been ungrateful. And when this is over and you have nothing, don't call me."

The line went dead.

Wren sat with the phone in her hand. Her hand was shaking, a fine, shallow tremor that came not from cold but from the body's decision to metabolize something it hadn't been given time to process. She watched her own fingers against the black rectangle of the phone and thought, with the detached clarity that sometimes comes after the worst part of a thing: *there it is.*

The field was empty. The orange flags at the site perimeter were just visible through the loblollies, their plastic tails snapping in a gust that rocked the truck once and then stilled.

She had known this call was coming for two years. She had imagined it in various forms, her mother's voice, the specific vocabulary her mother used for disappointment, the way the word *ungrateful* would land. She had imagined it would feel like something breaking. Instead it felt like a survey stake being driven: sharp, brief, and then simply there, marking a position that had always been there, now just visible.

She set the phone face-down on the passenger seat.

Her field notebook was wedged between the seat and the center console, its Rite-in-the-Rain 393F cover swollen slightly at the spine from four months of damp mornings. She pulled it out. The cover was stiff with dried clay at the bottom corner where she had set it down in the trench without thinking. She opened it to the last entry, the attorney's call, logged in her own shorthand, time-stamped, signed, and looked at the list of artifact entries above it.

Glass bead. Colonial document. Ceramic pipe bowl. Shell gorget. Copper pendant. A dozen smaller finds, each with its context number, its depth below datum, its measured coordinates, its sketch.

She had drawn all of them in the field, quick working sketches, enough to establish form and position. But she had not drawn them properly. Not to scale, with profile and plan views, with cross-hatching on the broken surfaces, with the north arrow and the scale bar and the stratum number that would make the drawings stand in a repatriation file or in whatever room this evidence eventually reached.

She would do that tonight.

She got out of the truck. The air smelled of damp loblolly bark and turned earth and, underneath both, the mineral cold of the trench. Her boots found the familiar ruts in the field road. The orange flags pointed the way they always pointed.

She walked toward the trench., -

The site was quiet in the way it got in the late afternoon, after the field team had packed out and before the last of the light left the sky. The total station still stood on its tripod at the north marker, its yellow casing catching the low sun. Someone had coiled the measuring tape and hung it on the stake the way she had shown them. Delia, probably. Delia was careful.

Wren stopped at the trench edge and looked down.

Four months. She had stood at this edge so many times that the ground here was worn smooth under her boots, a slight depression where she always planted her left foot before stepping in. The trench was four meters long, a meter and a half wide at the widest point, and it went down in steps, the careful stratigraphy she and Tayak had cut level by level, each stratum marked with a hand-lettered aluminum tag on a wire flag. The flags were still in place. The string lines were still in place. The photo scales were still in place, lying on the second step beside the trowel rest she had built from a scrap of lumber so the blades wouldn't nick the exposed surface.

She pulled on her nitrile gloves out of habit, the snap of the cuff against her wrist, and then stopped.

She looked at her hands. The gloves were blue, powderless, size medium. She had gone through four boxes of them since September.

She peeled them off and put them in her jacket pocket.

She climbed down into the trench.

The floor was cool under her boots, the compacted soil slightly slick where the day's foot traffic had polished it. She could feel the texture through her soles, the way the earth changed from the loose disturbed fill near the ladder to the firmer, undisturbed zone at the south end where the deepest layer was still sealed. She knew this floor the way she knew the layout of her apartment. She knew where the roots crossed under it and where the soil went from loam to clay and where there was a slight depression near the east wall that she had flagged as a possible post hole and then ruled out and then flagged again.

She sat down on the second step, the one at stratum three depth, and set her field notebook on her knees.

The Rite-in-the-Rain paper was slightly damp at the edges from the morning. They had had a light rain before seven and she had covered the trench with the tarp, but not before a little water had run in along the east wall. The pages were wavy at the corners. She opened to the front.

The first artifact entry was dated September 14th. *18CH-447-001. Glass bead. Blue, translucent, cylindrical, 7mm L x 4mm D. Stratum 1, Unit B-2, 12cm below datum. In situ, vertical orientation, long axis north-south.* Her working sketch was in the margin, quick and functional, not much more than a circle with a notation for the perforation. Enough to establish that she had seen it. Not enough for anything else.

She turned the page. The colonial document. The ceramic pipe bowl. The shell gorget, which had taken her three sketches to get right because the incised design on the face was more complex than she had expected and she kept losing the line in the shadow. The copper pendant. A dozen smaller entries, a scraper, two projectile points, a fragment of cord-marked ceramic, burned faunal bone, a bone awl she had almost missed because it was the same color as the surrounding matrix.

She had drawn all of them. None of them properly.

She looked at the trench walls, the careful horizontal lines of the strata, each one a different color and texture in the low light. The pale sandy fill of stratum one. The darker loam of stratum two. The dense clay-rich zone of stratum three with its faint orange mottling from iron. The deep brown of stratum four where the gorget had been. The almost-black of stratum five where the pendant had lain face-up in the earth for seventeen hundred years.

She thought about the attorney's voice. The careful script of a man who had used it before.

She thought about her mother's voice.

She thought about the woman whose partial name was in the 1691 folio, *Sioſ, *, water damage taking the rest, who had stood in a county court and argued that the ground was unavailable for patent because it already held the dead.

She opened her pencil case and took out the 2H. She sharpened it with her utility knife over the plastic bag she kept for shavings, four careful strokes, until the point was fine enough to draw a line she could hold to scale. The graphite shavings fell into the bag, dark against the clear plastic.

She turned to a fresh page.

At the top she wrote: *18CH-447-001. Glass bead. Scale 2:1. Plan view.* Then she drew the north arrow, small and precise, and the scale bar, and the stratum number, and the context number, and the date.

Then she began to draw the bead.

She had not held it since the day she had logged it. But she had looked at it for a long time before she bagged it, turning it in the light, trying to understand what it was before she understood what it was. The cobalt blue. The slightly uneven cylindrical form. The perforation that was just off-center. The faint spiral of the cane-wound glass visible if you caught the light right, the mark of a Dutch glassworker's rod pressed into the molten gather and twisted. She had written *possibly female* in pencil and then looked at that notation for a long time before she closed the notebook.

She drew from memory and from the log entry and from the photograph she had taken that morning in September, which was on her phone and which she pulled up now with her left hand while her right hand moved the pencil. The plan view first, then the profile, then the cross-section showing the perforation. She cross-hatched the broken end, there was a small chip at the south terminus, pre-depositional, and labeled it. She measured the drawing against the scale bar. She wrote the dimensions in millimeters in the margin.

Twelve minutes. She checked her watch.

She turned the page.

The colonial document was harder because it was flat and complex. The written text was not her job to reproduce, but the physical form of it, the dimensions, the fold lines, the condition of the edges, the water staining at the lower left corner, all of that needed drawing. She did the plan view and then a detail of the seal impression, which was partial and had been partially illegible even when fresh. She labeled the fold lines with arrows. She noted the water staining in a margin note: *brown, irregular, lower left quadrant, pre-depositional.*

She had found this document. She had been the one with the trowel when it came up. She had set it on the photo scale and called Tayak over and they had both looked at it without speaking for a moment, and then Tayak had said, very quietly, *get the camera*, and Wren had gotten the camera.

She had not told Tayak then what she already knew about the Calvert patent. She had told herself she was waiting for the right moment. She had been telling herself that for two months before she finally said it.

She turned the page.

The pipe bowl was her favorite drawing of the lot. She had done it well even in the field, because the cord-marked decoration on the exterior gave her something to work with, a pattern she could render in fine parallel lines that actually looked like what they were. She did it again now, more carefully, getting the curvature of the bowl right, the angle of the stem socket, the slight asymmetry of the rim that suggested it had been finished by hand rather than in a mold. She drew the cord-marking in a detail circle at twice the main scale.

She had been in the trench for forty minutes. The light was going. She could feel it in the way the trench walls were losing their color differentiation, the strata blurring from distinct bands into a general darkness, the orange mottling in stratum three fading to gray.

She climbed out, went to the equipment trailer, and came back with the LED work light on its telescoping stand and the small generator that ran it. She set the light at the trench edge, angled down, and started the generator. It caught on the third pull. The light came on, white, flat, slightly harsh, and the trench filled with it, the shadows jumping to the walls and then settling.

Moths came almost immediately. A pale one landed on the open page of her notebook and she lifted the corner of the page to move it. It flew off and came back. She drew around it.

The shell gorget.

She had drawn this one three times in the field and still hadn't gotten the incised design right. She looked at the photograph on her phone, the gorget in situ, face-up, the design catching the raking light of the reflector she had held at a low angle to bring it out. The design was a series of concentric lines around a central perforation, with four radial lines extending outward and a repeating notched border. The form was documented in mid-Atlantic Late Woodland contexts, the kind of gorget that moved along the same waterways that moved copper and shell and the knowledge of how to use both. She had not written that in the field log because the field log was not the place for that kind of inference. But she was thinking it now.

She drew the plan view. She drew the profile, the gorget was slightly cupped, not flat, a gentle curve she had measured at three millimeters of relief across the diameter. She drew the cross-section. She drew a detail of the incised design at three times scale, the lines careful and fine, the notched border rendered in short precise strokes.

She had been in the trench for an hour and twenty minutes. She had drawn four artifacts. She had twenty-seven more to go.

She sharpened the pencil again.

The copper pendant.

She had not photographed it herself. By the time it came up she had stepped back and let Tayak do the documentation, because it was Tayak's site and Tayak's find and Wren had understood that there were things you stepped back from. But she had looked at it. She had been two feet away when it came out of the earth, and she had seen the way the light caught the patina, that particular green-brown of ancient copper, not uniform, darker in the recesses where the soil had been in contact longest, lighter on the raised surfaces. She had seen the form of it: a small pendant, roughly oval, with a slight ridge along the central axis and a suspension loop at the top that had been worked separately and attached. Hammered into shape by someone's hands. Made for someone specific.

She drew it now from the photographs on her phone and from what she remembered of the actual object. She drew the plan view and the profile and the detail of the suspension loop. She drew the patina distribution in a margin note, using a simple notation system she had developed for herself: *D* for dark, *L* for light, a rough map of the surface.

The pendant had been face-up in the earth. Someone had placed it that way. She had written that in the field log, *orientation deliberate, face-up, long axis east-west*, and she had not written what she thought it meant, because the field log was not the place for that either.

She was thinking it now.

She turned the page.

The scraper. The first projectile point, a small triangular point, late Woodland, chert, the base slightly concave. The second projectile point, larger, earlier, the flaking pattern different, a style she associated with the Middle Woodland but hadn't said so in the log. The cord-marked ceramic fragment, rim sherd, the cord impressions still clear on the exterior. The bone awl, almost certainly worked deer bone, the tip missing, the shaft polished from use.

She drew them all.

Her neck ached. She rolled it once, felt the vertebrae shift, and went back to the page. The graphite smudge on the heel of her right hand was spreading up toward her wrist. She had a small streak of it on her left forearm where she had rested it on the page. The moths were thick around the light now, a soft percussion of wings against the LED housing, and somewhere beyond the perimeter of the work light a fox or a raccoon was moving through the dry leaves at the tree line. She could hear it stop and start, stop and start, investigating something.

The burned faunal bone. The red ochre fragment, small, irregular, the surface stained deep red-brown, the hematite still vivid under the LED light in a way that made her think of ceremony rather than accident. A second ceramic sherd, body sherd this time, a different paste than the first, tempered with what looked like crushed shell. A fragment of worked copper, small, possibly a scrap rather than a finished object, found in the same stratum as the pendant but two meters north.

She drew the copper fragment carefully. It was small enough that she drew it at four times scale, and she still had to use the pencil point very fine to get the edge profile right.

At some point she became aware that she was cold. The temperature had dropped. She could feel it in her fingers, the joints slightly stiff, and in the way the damp soil smell had intensified, the mineral cold of the trench rising around her. She pulled her jacket tighter and kept drawing.

She had not thought about her mother's voice in two hours. She noticed this the way you notice a sound has stopped, not during the silence but after, when you realize the absence.

The bone awl's detail. A fragment of what might be shell bead, too degraded to be certain, logged as *possible shell bead, identification tentative*. A small biface, unfinished, the flaking on one face complete and the other barely started, as if the maker had been interrupted or had changed their mind.

She thought about the maker's hands. She thought about the fact that the maker had been interrupted, or had changed their mind, and had set the biface down in a particular place at a particular time, and it had stayed there for a very long time, and then her trowel had found it.

She drew the biface. Plan view, profile, detail of the flaking on the finished face. She wrote the dimensions. She wrote the context number.

She turned the page.

The last entry was the burned faunal bone from stratum five, the deepest context reached, recovered in the same level as the pendant. She had logged it as *burned, long bone fragment, large mammal, possibly deer, identification tentative*. She drew it now, the irregular form, the calcined white at one end where the burning had been most intense, the darker zone at the other end where it had been less complete. She drew the cross-section. She labeled the calcined zone.

She put the pencil down.

Her hands were cold and stiff and the smear of graphite on her right hand had worked its way into the lines of her knuckles. She looked at the open notebook, the last drawing still slightly wet-looking in the LED light, the pencil lines fine and dark against the cream-colored paper.

Thirty-one artifacts. She counted the pages. Thirty-one drawings, each with its north arrow and scale bar and context number and stratum number and date. Each one signed at the bottom right corner: *W. Calvert. 18CH-447.*

She had held each one. Not in her hands, most of them were in bags in the equipment trailer or in the conservation suite in College Park. The generator was still running, a low steady hum at the trench edge. The moths circled the light. The fox, or raccoon, she still wasn't sure, had moved off into the trees and the leaf-sound had stopped.

She sat in the trench for a moment longer, the notebook in her lap, her cold hands flat on the cover.

Tomorrow she would present her documentation. Tomorrow whatever was going to happen would begin to happen, and she would not be able to stop it or slow it or shape it, because it was not hers to shape.

But tonight she had drawn every artifact in this trench, in sequence, from the glass bead to the deepest layer. She had done it for herself, so that she had held each one. So that when she handed the notebook over, and she would hand it over, that was already decided, it had been decided the moment she peeled off the nitrile gloves, she would know what she was giving.

She climbed out of the trench.

The night air was cold and clear, the stars very bright above the loblolly canopy. The orange flags at the site perimeter were invisible in the dark but she knew where they were. She picked up the notebook and walked back to the truck.

She had signed each drawing at the bottom right corner. *W. Calvert. 18CH-447.*

The name sat in her chest the way it had been sitting there since the attorney's call. Calvert. The same name on the 1687 patent. The same name on the boundary description that used the mound as a survey marker, that said, in the careful language of colonial record-keeping, *the Piscataway burial ground at the high ground adjacent to Mattawoman Creek*, saying it as a landmark, as a fixed point, as something the surveyors had known was there and used anyway.

She had not chosen her name. She had not chosen her grandmother's estate papers or the photocopy her mother had found in them or the phone call from Pruitt's firm asking about successors in interest. She had not chosen any of the things that had led her to this field position, though she had chosen to take it, and she had chosen, four months ago, not to say anything, and she had chosen, last night, to say everything.

What she had not yet chosen was what happened next.

She picked up her phone. She opened the draft she had written twice and not sent, addressed to Tayak, subject line *documentation and disclosure, 18CH-447*, and she read it through one more time. It said what she needed it to say. It said she had field notes, artifact drawings, and chain-of-title documentation relating to the 1687 Calvert patent. It said she understood the conflict of interest and was prepared to make a formal disclosure. It said she wanted to speak before anything went further. It said she had the estate papers and would provide them to whoever the tribal legal team directed.

She sent it.

Then she sat in the truck cab with the heater running and watched the sky go gray and then pale and then the first hard line of orange appear above the eastern tree line, and the loblolly pines come fully back into the world, and the orange flags at the site perimeter become visible one by one in the early light. The site she had been on for four months. The ground she had been standing on without fully standing on it. She was standing on it now.

Her phone buzzed.

Not Tayak. It was too early for a reply. It was her mother. A text, sent at 5:49 AM, which meant her mother had been awake, which meant her mother had been waiting.

*I hope you know what you're doing.*

Wren looked at the text for a long moment. Then she looked at the site, the trench, the orange flags, the pale morning light coming flat across the mowed grass between the equipment trailer and the tree line.

She typed back: *I do now.*

She put the phone in her jacket pocket. She got out of the truck. The air was cold and smelled of damp grass and the mineral residue of the trench, and somewhere in the tree line a bird was making the first sound of the morning, a single repeated note, patient and unhurried, as if it had all the time in the world to say what it needed to say.

She walked to the trench and stood at its edge and looked down into it. The work light was off. In the early gray light the trench floor was dark and the soil walls showed their layers clearly, the pale sandy stratum one, the denser brown of stratum two, the red-tinged clay of the deeper levels, and at the base the almost-black of stratum five where the pendant had lain. She could read the stratigraphy from here. She had been reading it for four months.

The notebook was under her arm. Her cold hands were in her jacket pockets. The frost was melting off the turf at the mound's crown, the pale blades darkening as the sun reached them, and the water ran down in thin lines along the mound's face and soaked into the earth at its base, and the earth took it the way it took everything, without hurry, without acknowledgment, keeping what it was given.

The bird in the tree line kept going. That single repeated note, patient as a metronome.

Wren stood there until her hand stopped aching. It took a while. Then she opened the notebook to the first page, uncapped her pencil, and wrote the date.
