The longhouse smelled of last night's fire and damp bark. The cold hearth at the center still held a grey crust of ash, and the smoke-hole above it let in a pale column of morning light that fell on nothing.

Pell found a place against the back wall where one of the roof-posts threw a shadow, and he stood there with his arms loose at his sides, watching people file in.

He had not meant to come.

His wife Nima had said, at dawn, while he was sharpening his knife on the whetstone: *You should go.* Not a command. She had her way of saying things that were not commands but sat in the room after she said them, like a stone set in a doorway. Then she had laughed at his expression, a quick sound, and gone back to her work. So he had gone. He was here. He was not going to agree with anything.

The longhouse was not large. It was Kessik's, the one her father had built and that Kessik had taken over in the way she took over most things, quietly and completely, until one day you looked and it was simply hers. A dozen roof-posts down each side, the walls wattle plastered with grey clay, a smoke-hole in the bark above the dead hearth. People brought their own light: a clay lamp burning animal fat, a pine torch jammed into a bracket near the door that threw jumping orange across the faces nearest it.

The old people came first. That was how it always was.

Pell watched them settle. His mother moved with the care she used now, the care she had not needed five years ago, lowering herself onto the bench along the left wall with one hand braced on the plank. She wore a long shell-bead necklace at her throat, the whelk beads worn smooth where her fingers had touched them ten thousand times, the shells clicking softly as she sat. Beside her, an old woman who had walked with a stick since the winter before last. Behind them, an elder who sat with eyes already closed in the way that might be sleep and probably was not. They arranged themselves without speaking much. They had been in rooms together for forty years and more. They did not need to discuss where to sit.

The younger families came after, filling the center space, standing mostly. A man with his wife. A woman with her youngest on her hip, the child already asleep against her shoulder. Other households, pressing close enough that their arms touched.

Pell counted. Twenty-three people, perhaps twenty-five. Nearly everyone still here.

Kessik came in last, which was not modesty but method. She had learned early that the room would look at whoever came last, and she used that. She wore a plain deerskin tunic belted with leather, her hair pinned back with a bone pin, and she carried a stone hoe across her forearm with the blade forward, the handle resting in the crook of her elbow. She set it down in the center of the floor near the cold hearth where everyone could see it, and then she stood and waited until the talking died.

She did not call for quiet. She simply waited, and the quiet came.

"You know why we're here," she said.

Pell had heard her speak in rooms before. He knew what she could do with a sentence. He kept his face still.

"The mound," she said. "The one on the high ground. Our grandfather's grandfather's grandfather is up there. Some of you knew that already. Some of you heard it from someone who heard it from someone else. What I want to say tonight is that we should build it higher."

A pause. Not for effect, Pell thought, or not only for effect. She was listening to the room.

"The families who left," she said. "The ones who went north, who went east. They're gone. Their houses are falling. In twenty years, thirty, there won't be anything here that says we were. Except the mound. The mound is older than anyone's grandfather. It will be here after our grandchildren's grandchildren are gone. If we build it higher, put our names in it, not just the dead but the living who did the work, then something stays."

She stopped. She looked at the room.

Pell looked at the room too.

That was when he saw it.

He had expected what he usually saw at these gatherings: the polite attention of people who had come because it was expected, who would nod and ask two careful questions and go home and do nothing. He had expected the younger ones to be distracted, the older ones to be tired. He had expected to be the only person in the room who was going to say what everyone was actually thinking, which was: *this is a great deal of labor for a thing that will not feed anyone.*

But the old people's faces were wrong.

His mother was watching Kessik with an expression Pell had not seen on her in years. Not polite. Not patient. Something older than that. A look like a person who has been waiting in a doorway for a long time and has finally heard footsteps on the path.

The old woman with the stick had opened her eyes. She was leaning forward very slightly, her chin lifted.

The elder who had seemed asleep had his hands flat on his knees and his eyes on Kessik's face with a steadiness that was not the steadiness of a man at rest.

Pell looked from face to face. The older man near the door whose name he could not place, who had come from upriver two winters ago when his own settlement's stores ran out. He had his mouth slightly open, the way a man looks when he is hearing something said aloud that he has been carrying in silence.

They already knew this, Pell thought. Not the plan. Not Kessik's plan specifically. But the thing underneath it. The thing she was pointing at. They had been carrying it, and they had not said it, and now someone was saying it, and their faces were the faces of people who had been waiting for someone to say it.

He felt something shift in his chest, small and unwelcome. He did not name it.

A man near the fire raised his hand. "How much work are we talking?"

"Enough," Kessik said. "Not more than we can do. I'll tell you what I think the work is, and you can tell me if I'm wrong."

She began to lay it out. The soil, the turf, the pack baskets, the time. She had thought it through. She always thought things through before she spoke. That was the difference between them. Pell had always considered it a weakness in her, a kind of excessive caution, but standing here watching her he admitted to himself that it was not a weakness.

He did not stay for the questions. He had told himself he would not, and he kept to that.

He pushed off from the wall and went out through the low door into the night air, which was cold and smelled of mud and woodsmoke from the cookfires banked down for the night. The sky was clear, the stars very bright, the ground under his feet soft from three days of rain.

He walked home.

He did not think about his mother's face. He did not think about it for the rest of that evening, or the following morning, or most of the day after.

But it was there, behind everything else he thought about, the way a stone is behind water. The water moves. The stone does not., -

The first morning he dug out the drainage channel along the eastern edge of his field, where the ground had been pooling since the rain. The work was straightforward and he was good at it. He moved the stone-bladed hoe in short, even strokes, and the wet soil came up dark and heavy and smelled of old roots. He thought about the channel. He thought about the angle of the cut and whether it would hold through the next rain. He did not think about the mound.

The second time he thought about the mound he was on his knees pulling dock weed from the bean rows, and the thought arrived without invitation: *the baskets alone would take three men a full season to weave.* He pulled the dock weed. Its roots went deep and he had to work his fingers down to find the break point, otherwise it came back. He knew this about dock weed. He had been pulling it since he was old enough to walk a row.

The baskets, he thought. The baskets were only the beginning. You needed baskets to carry the soil, and you needed the soil, and you needed men to cut the turf and men to carry the baskets and men to lay the layers and tamp them down correctly. Kessik had said she knew what the work was. He wondered if she knew about the tamping. You could not simply pile earth. He had seen what happened when you simply piled earth. It slumped. It ran in rain. It settled wrong. His father had taught him this when they raised the grain store platform together, the summer Pell was fourteen. You cut your turf in blocks and you laid it grass-down and you tamped each layer before you added the next, and then you waited and tamped again, and the work was slow and heavy and it was not the kind of work that kept young men's attention past the third day.

He sat back on his heels and looked down the bean row. The plants were doing well. He had put good effort into this ground.

The young men, he thought. He ran through them in his mind the way you run your thumb along a blade to check for gaps. The man near the fire who had asked about the work, willing but easily distracted by any conversation that came near him. The two brothers from the river-side houses, strong enough, but they had their own fields and their own obligations and they would be there for the first week and then they would be less there and then they would not be there. Kotan, who was nineteen and thought that wanting to do a thing was the same as doing it. He could not build a mound on Kotan's wanting.

He pulled another dock weed and threw it to the edge of the row.

The season was wrong, too. He had not said this at the meeting because he had not stayed for the questions, but it was true and someone should say it. You did not begin earthworks in the wet season if you could help it. The ground was waterlogged now from the rains, which meant the soil would not compact properly and the lower layers would shift when they dried. Any man who had ever dug a post hole in spring and come back in summer to find it leaning knew this. Kessik had thought things through, he reminded himself. Perhaps she had thought about the season. Perhaps she had a reason for starting now rather than waiting for the dry months.

He did not find this reassuring. He found it irritating, which was different.

That evening he sat by his own fire and sharpened the hoe blade on the whetstone, working the stone in slow circles, and his wife Nima sat across from him carding wool, and the sound of the whetstone and the sound of the carding filled the house and neither of them spoke for a long time.

"You're thinking very loudly," Nima said.

"I'm sharpening a hoe."

"You sharpened it yesterday."

He looked at the blade. She was not wrong. She pressed her lips together in the way she had, not quite a smile but the shape of one held back.

"The mound," she said. It was not a question.

"The work is too great," he said. "The season is wrong. The young men will lose interest halfway through and Kessik will be standing on a half-built mound in the rain wondering where everyone went, and I will have to go and help her finish it, and I will have lost a season's work on my own ground for the sake of a pile of dirt that no one will see for a hundred years."

Nima pulled a long tangle of wool free and set it aside. "Is that all of it?"

"That's enough of it."

She did not say anything. She had a way of not saying anything that could tip toward laughter as easily as toward argument, and he was never entirely sure which way it would fall.

"The old people's faces," he said, after a while.

"Yes."

"My mother's face."

"Yes."

He put the whetstone down. The hoe blade was as sharp as it was going to get., -

The third day he was splitting wood behind the house, working through a stack of ash he had cut in the autumn. The axe was good and the ash split clean along the grain, and he fell into the rhythm of it, lift, set, swing, the crack and the two halves falling apart, and he was not thinking about anything except the next log when the thought came at him from a direction he had not been defending.

*What would it mean for his children to stand on something their grandparents built.*

He set the axe head down on the block and stood there.

He had two children. His daughter Efa was four. His son Bran was not yet two. They would not remember this season. They would not remember the rain or the wet ground or the meetings or the arguments. They would not remember Kessik standing at the cold hearth waiting for the room to go quiet. What they would have, if the mound was built, was the mound. Not a story about the mound. Not someone else's account of what their father had said in a room one night. The mound itself, under their feet, made partly by his hands and partly by his children's hands and their children's hands after that, going up and up, and under it the names of everyone who had done the work.

He thought about the families who had left. Their houses falling. The lashings rotted, the hearthstones going cold. In thirty years there would be nothing. He had known this without wanting to know it, the way you know a tooth is going bad before you admit it.

He picked up the axe and split the next log.

*The thing underneath it.* He had thought that, watching the old people's faces. The thing she was pointing at. They had been carrying it.

He had been carrying it too. He just had not wanted to be the one to put it down in the open where everyone could see.

He finished the stack. He stacked the split wood against the wall under the overhang, where it would stay dry. He washed his hands in the bark bucket by the door and dried them on his thighs and stood for a moment looking at the line of the high ground above the settlement, where the mound sat against the grey sky, older than anyone's grandfather, holding whatever it held.

Then he walked to Kessik's house., -

The evening came in fast, the way it did in the wet season. The light went from grey to dark without the long amber pause that summer brought. Pell was carrying water from the stream when he heard the children behind him.

His daughter Efa was four, which meant she went everywhere at a half-run, arms out for balance on the uneven ground. His son Bran was not yet two and was being carried on Efa's back in the way she had decided was correct, which was not quite correct, and Bran was gripping her hair with both fists and looking entirely satisfied.

"Down," Pell said, and took Bran and settled him on his own hip. Bran grabbed his ear instead.

"Where are you going?" Efa said.

"To set the water by the house."

"Then where?"

He looked at her. She had Nima's directness, which was useful and also relentless. "Up to the high ground," he said, because he had been going there every evening for two days now and he saw no reason to lie about it.

"We'll come," Efa said, in the tone that was not a request.

He set the water jar inside the door. Nima was at the fire, turning flat cornbread on the hearthstone, and she looked up at him and at the children and back at him without saying anything. He lifted his chin toward the high ground. She nodded once and turned the bread.

She had understood the mound's purpose before he did. He had known this, somewhere, since the assembly. The way she had said *you should go* that morning without explaining why, the way she had not needed to explain. He had been catching up ever since., -

The path up was steep enough that he had to take Efa's hand on the last section, where the turf was wet and the roots of the old oak made the footing unreliable in the low light. Bran rode his hip and pointed at things, a crow, a puddle, his own foot, with the grave authority of someone cataloguing a new country.

The mound sat at the top of the rise the way it always had, which is to say it sat there as if it had always been there and always would be, and the distinction between the mound and the ground around it was not sharp but gradual, the way a sleeping animal's side rises without a clear line between body and earth. The grass over it was the same grass as the grass around it, just older. Denser. The roots going down further.

Pell stood at the edge of it and let Bran down to stand on the turf. Bran sat down immediately and put his hand flat on the ground.

"What is it for?" Efa said.

He had known she would ask. She asked this about everything, the whetstone, the loom weights, the way the maize was stored in the pit and not in the house. Everything had a reason and she wanted to know it.

"The dead sleep there," he said.

It was the answer his mother had given him, and her mother had given her, and he supposed it went back further than that, the same words passed down the same way the mound itself had been passed down, unchanged, sufficient.

Efa looked at the mound. She was not frightened of it. She had been up here before, with Nima, and she treated it the way she treated the old oak, with a respectful lack of ceremony, as something that had been here longer than she had and would be here longer than she would and did not require her opinion.

"Which dead?" she said.

"The ones who lived here before us."

"Before you?"

"Before my grandfather's grandfather." He thought about it. "Before that, even."

She considered this with the focused attention she brought to problems she was not sure she could solve. Bran was still sitting on the turf with his hand flat on the ground, looking at his own fingers.

"When you're dead," Efa said, "will you sleep here?"

"I don't know."

She looked at him. This was not an acceptable answer and her face said so.

"Perhaps," he said.

"Who will put you there?"

"Someone will."

"Who?"

He opened his mouth and then closed it. The question had a shape he had not expected. He had thought she was asking about the ritual, who would wrap him, who would speak the names, who would know what to do. He could have answered that. But she was looking at him with Nima's eyes in the low light, and what she was asking was something else, something underneath the ritual question. *Will there be someone, when the time comes, who knows this ground well enough to put you in it correctly.*

He did not answer.

The wind came up from the river valley and moved through the oak behind them, a low sound, not quite speech. Bran looked up at it. Then he looked back at his hand on the turf and pressed it down harder, as if testing whether the ground would hold.

Pell had stood on this ground his whole life. He had played on the slope of it as a boy, had sheltered under the oak in rain, had watched his mother come up here in the early mornings when she thought no one was watching. He had never thought about its weight before. He had thought about the mound the way you think about a wall that has always been standing, as something given, not something made, not something that required a decision.

But someone had made it. Someone had stood where he was standing now and looked at the same slope and decided that this was the place, and then had picked up a basket and moved the first load of earth, and that decision had made everything after it possible. The names. The burials. The knowledge of who was under the ground and why. All of it resting on that first decision and the hundred decisions after it, the hands that had come back season after season to add another layer, to keep the shape true.

And the families who had left, their houses falling, their hearthstones going cold, their names thinning out in the mouths of people who had not known them, they had not had this. Or they had had it and had left it anyway. He did not know which was worse.

Efa had stopped asking questions. She was standing beside him with her shoulder against his arm, looking at the mound in the last of the light, and her silence had a quality he recognized from Nima. The quality of someone who has asked the important question and is now waiting to see what the answer does.

Bran pulled a handful of grass and looked at it and let it go. The blades fell back against the turf.

Pell thought: *my hands, and their hands after mine.*

He thought: *going up and up.*

He put his hand on Efa's head, briefly, the way his mother used to. She did not pull away.

"Come," he said. "Your mother has bread on the stone."

He picked Bran up and took Efa's hand and they went back down the slope in the dark, the wet turf cold under his feet, the mound behind them holding its shape against the sky., -

The fire had burned down to coals by the time Pell came back inside. Nima was asleep with Bran curled against her back, one of the boy's fists tangled in her hair, and even in sleep her face held that quality of readiness, as though she might at any moment find something worth laughing at. Efa had gone to her mat without being told, which meant she was tired. The walk up to the mound had taken something out of her. Pell stood in the doorway for a moment and watched them breathe.

Then he went back outside.

He sat on the log by the door and looked at the dark. The valley below was black, no moon yet, and the river was audible but not visible, a low sound, water moving over the tidal shallows, the same sound it made in every season regardless of what the people above it were doing. He could smell the damp from the coals inside and the sharper smell of the oak behind the house where the bark had split last winter and the wood was beginning to rot. He had meant to do something about that oak. He had been meaning to for two years.

He was not a man who sat still easily. His hands went to his belt knife and he turned it over once and put it back.

The objections were still there. He had not stopped having them.

The work would be enormous. He had stood at the mound that afternoon and looked at the slope and done the arithmetic in his head the way he did when he estimated the barley yield for a neighbor, without sentiment, just measurement. The mound as it stood was already a thing of some size, but what Kessik was describing was a different thing entirely. More earth. More height. Enough that it could be seen from the river. That meant not weeks but seasons, and seasons meant the planting would suffer, and the planting suffering meant leaner stores, and leaner stores meant a harder winter for the children, and the children were not abstract to him. Bran's ribs had shown last February. He had not forgotten that.

And Kessik. He still did not fully trust Kessik, not her intentions, which seemed genuine enough, but her judgment. She saw the thing she wanted to see and built outward from it. Pell had watched her at the assembly three days ago, the way she had stood in the middle of the longhouse with the firelight on her face and spoken about the ancestors as though they were listening from just outside the door. Some of the old ones had wept. Pell had not wept. He had stood at the back with his arms folded and thought: *this is a woman who believes very hard, and believing hard is not the same as being right.*

He still thought that.

He turned the knife over again. Set it down on the log beside him.

The thing was, and this was the thing he had been not-thinking for three days, the thing he had been walking around the edges of without looking at directly, the thing was that the objections did not touch the question Efa had asked.

*Who will put you there? Who?*

She had meant it practically. She was four years old and she meant everything practically, which was one of the things he loved about her, the same thing that sometimes exhausted him. But the practical version of the question had a practical answer. Nima would outlive him, probably. Or Bran, when he was grown. Someone would know what to do. That was not the problem.

The problem was the version of the question underneath.

He had grown up knowing the mound. He had grown up knowing that the dead were under it and that the dead were his people and that his people had been on this ground for longer than anyone could count back to. That knowledge had weight. It had made him something, not important, not proud exactly, but *located.* He knew where he was in the world and he knew it in his body, the way he knew the slope of the hill and the sound of the river, because the knowledge had been given to him by people who had stood on the same ground and been given it by people before them. It went back. He did not know how far. Far enough that the beginning of it was not available to him, which was itself a kind of richness. The chain going back into the dark, link by link, further than he could see.

His children had that now. Efa and Bran had that.

But Efa had stood on the mound this evening and asked *who will put you there*, and the question had a shape he had not expected because the honest answer, the answer he had not given her, was that he did not know. Not because there would be no one, but because he did not know if the ground would still be theirs. He did not know if the knowledge would still be held. He had watched families leave. He had watched houses fall. He had watched names thin out in the mouths of people who had not known the ones who bore them. The mound was still standing. But standing was not the same as being kept.

Kessik wanted to make it larger. Higher. Visible from the river. Pell had thought this was vanity, Kessik wanting to leave a mark, wanting to be the one who did the large thing. He still thought there was some of that in it. But he had stood at the assembly and watched the old ones weep, and he had watched their faces, not at Kessik but at the mound, at the idea of it, at the thing Kessik was describing, and what he had seen in their faces was not sentiment. It was recognition. The look of people who had been waiting for someone to say a thing they had known needed saying, and who were relieved, finally, that someone had said it.

And his mother's face. He had been not-thinking about his mother's face since the assembly and he thought about it now. She had sat on the bench along the left wall with her shell beads at her throat and she had watched Kessik the way a woman watches a river she has been waiting to cross, not with hope exactly, but with a readiness that had been there for a long time, held in reserve for the moment when the water dropped enough to ford. His mother had been turning this mound over in her mind longer than Kessik had been speaking about it, longer perhaps than Pell had been alive. He had not known this before tonight. He knew it now.

The old ones remembered the northern mounds. He had heard the stories. Mounds that could be seen for a day's travel upriver. Mounds that told you, from a distance, that someone had been here and had been here for a long time and intended to stay. The families who had built those mounds were still here. Their names were still held. Their dead were still known.

He picked up the knife and put it in his belt.

The wind had dropped. The valley was very quiet now, just the river, and somewhere downslope a night bird calling once and then stopping.

He thought about Bran's hand flat on the turf. Testing whether the ground would hold.

He thought: the ground holds what you put into it.

He thought: I am going to be dead for a very long time. Longer than I have been alive. Longer than my father was alive, and his father. And what will remain of me will be a name in someone's mouth, if I am fortunate, and the work of my hands, if the work was worth keeping. And my children will stand on this ground after me, and their children after them, and what they will have, what they will know about where they are and who they come from, depends on what we do now, the people who are alive now, the people who can still pick up a basket and move the first load of earth.

The objections were still there. The work was still enormous. Kessik's judgment was still not something he would bet his life on without watching it more carefully.

He decided anyway.

Not because the objections had been answered. Because the question underneath them was larger than the objections, and he had looked at it directly now, in the dark, without flinching, and he knew what it required of him.

He sat for a moment longer. The coals inside had gone grey. The oak behind the house shifted in a small wind, the rotted wood creaking once, low.

He thought: I will need to do something about that oak.

Then he went inside, lay down beside Nima, and slept., -

The fire had not yet caught when he came down the slope.

Kessik was crouched at the ring of stones outside her door, feeding dry bark into the small flame she had coaxed from the coals. The smoke went straight up in the still air, pale against the dark ridge. She did not look up when she heard his steps on the wet grass, but her shoulders changed, a slight settling, as though she had been expecting weight and was adjusting to receive it.

Pell stopped at the edge of the firelight. The bark caught and the flame jumped and in its new light he could see her face properly: older than she seemed when she was talking, the lines deep on either side of her mouth, her grey-streaked hair still loose from sleep. She had a wooden bowl beside her knee, half-full of water, and she dipped her fingers in it and touched them to her lips in the way the old people did before the first fire of the day. Her skin cloak was worn thin at the hem from years of dragging through just this kind of wet morning grass.

He said: "Tell me where to start."

She looked up at him then. Not surprised. Not pleased either, not in the way of a woman who had won an argument. She looked at him the way you look at a tool you have picked up to test its weight, deciding whether it will do.

The bark popped. A coal shifted. The flame found the larger piece of wood she had laid over it and began to work at the grain.

"The eastern face," she said. "Below the shoulder of the mound. The turf there is thick but soft underneath. The ground gives. We need to cut and roll it back before we can begin moving earth. If we start there, we start clean."

Pell nodded. He had walked that ground. He knew the place she meant, the slope where the mound's old skin had grown over years of undisturbed grass, the roots deep and matted, the whole surface holding together like a woven mat. It would take work to open it without tearing. She was right that it was where to begin.

He turned to go.

"Pell."

He stopped.

"I need two others with you." She had not stood up. She was still feeding the fire, laying a second piece of wood across the first with the careful economy of someone who had done this ten thousand mornings. "Not to watch. To work. People who will come back the second day and the third."

He stood still.

She said: "Two who will not need to be convinced again each morning."

He understood what she was not saying. That she had watched the assembly. That she had seen who wept and who crossed their arms and who stood at the back and waited to see which way the weight would fall before they committed themselves. She was not asking him to bring her believers. She was asking him to bring her workers, people whose hands would be in the ground before their doubts had time to organize themselves into reasons.

He thought of Caran first. Caran had not spoken at the assembly, which meant he had not spoken against it, which was not the same thing as being for it but was a beginning. He was a broad, careful man who did not start tasks he did not intend to finish. He had been digging out the new drainage channel at the north edge of the settlement for three weeks without complaint, working alone in the early mornings before the day's other claims on him. He would understand the work. He might not understand the reason, not yet, but he would understand that the work was real and that someone had decided to do it, and that would be enough to get him to the eastern face on the first morning.

The second was harder.

Kessik was watching him now, still crouched at the fire, her fingers loose around the wooden bowl.

He thought of Nima's brother Ithel, who had stood beside him at the assembly and said nothing but whose face had done its own work. The slight tightening around the eyes when Kessik described the mound's height. The way his chin had lifted when the old ones began to weep. Ithel was twenty-two and had a young son not yet walking and a way of going still when he was deciding something, like a man reading weather. He had not committed himself to anything at the assembly. But he had not left early, which some had.

Pell thought: he will listen to me. Not because he trusts my judgment more than his own, but because I am Nima's husband and he will want to understand what I have decided and why. He will ask questions on the walk up the hill. That was fine. Pell had answers now. He had spent the night making them.

"I know who," he said.

Kessik gave a small nod. She turned back to the fire.

He walked away from her down the slope, his feet dark with dew to the ankle, the mound rising behind him against the pale sky where the ridge was beginning to separate itself from the dark. He did not look back at it. He had looked at it enough in the night. He knew what it was and he knew what it needed and he knew what he was going to do.

The settlement was still quiet below him. A dog moved between two houses, nose down. Smoke was beginning from one roof hole, then another, the first fires of the morning, the small daily proof that people were still here, still waking, still feeding the coals. Somewhere a child made a sound and was hushed.

Caran's house was on the north side, near the drainage work. Ithel was two houses east of Nima's mother.

Pell went to Caran first.

He walked steadily, not hurrying, his hands loose at his sides. The ground was soft under his feet and the air smelled of wet turf and woodsmoke and the faint sour note of the dung heap at the field's edge. Behind him, above him, the mound stood on its hill in the early grey light, waiting to be made larger, waiting to be made visible, waiting for the first load of earth.

He had work to do.
