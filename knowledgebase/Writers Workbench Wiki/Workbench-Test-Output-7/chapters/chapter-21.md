The shoes were the hardest part.

Matta had worn English shoes for eleven years and still could not make his feet forget what they knew. The buckles caught the morning light coming through the cabin door, brass recently polished, each one a small mirror. He stood in the doorway working the left strap through the tongue until it sat flat against the instep. His wife watched him from the fire without speaking. She had learned not to speak during the shoes.

He straightened and looked down at himself. Linen shirt, clean. Waistcoat of dark blue wool, the third button replaced last autumn with one that did not quite match. Breeches, woolen stockings, the coat he had bought secondhand from a merchant in Port Tobacco four years ago, cut for a man with narrower shoulders. The Bible under his arm was not secondhand. He had bought it new, spent more than he should have, because a worn Bible could be read as indifference and a new one could not.

"The fire will need wood before noon," he said.

His wife said, "I know."

He went out., -

The road to Port Tobacco was three miles of packed clay that turned to mud in the low stretches near the creek. Matta walked it at the pace of a man with nowhere urgent to be, which was the pace he had practiced. Hurrying drew attention. Dawdling drew a different kind. He had found the speed that made him invisible on an English road and he kept it.

The morning smelled of wood smoke and the particular damp of October leaves going soft in the ditches. A cart passed him going the same direction, two women in dark wool gowns sitting upright in the bed, their caps bright against the grey sky. The man driving touched his hat brim. Matta touched his. The cart moved on.

He thought about the ordinance.

He had not seen it yet. Thomas Pryor had told him about it three days ago at the millpond, speaking quietly with his back to the road in the way men spoke when they were not certain they should be speaking at all. Pryor was not a bad man. He was a man who understood which way things were moving and liked to give warning before the movement arrived. *Heathen implements*, he had said. *Seized and condemned.* He had looked at Matta with an expression that was partly apology and partly something else, something that wanted Matta to say it was nothing, that he had nothing to worry about, that the ordinance was aimed at other people.

Matta had said, "I appreciate you telling me."

Pryor had seemed relieved.

But three days of knowing a thing second-hand was not the same as reading it. Matta needed the exact words. The exact words were the only thing worth having., -

Port Tobacco on a Sunday morning was quieter than a market day but not quiet. Horses stood at the rail outside the ordinary, patient and steaming in the cold. A family from the tobacco farms east of the creek came up the lane in their Sunday clothes, the father in a decent coat, the mother with her best kerchief folded over her hair, three children in a stair-step behind them. A dog crossed the lane carrying something and was shouted at by a man Matta did not know.

The church stood at the far end of the lane, timber-framed, its whitewash going grey at the corners where the rain came off the roof. The bell had been rung already. Matta could hear the last of it fading as he came around the bend, the sound moving through the cold air like something dissolving.

He did not go to the courthouse first. He wanted to. He had been thinking about the ordinance for three days and he wanted to read the thing himself, to count the words and find where the edges were. But a man who goes to the courthouse before church on a Sunday is a man whose mind is not on the Lord, and Matta could not afford that observation today.

He went to the church., -

The back pew was not marked. It did not need to be. The congregation understood its geography the way they understood which side of the road to walk on. Matta took his seat at the end of the bench nearest the wall, where the cold came through a gap in the timber framing and the tallow candle in the iron sconce above had burned down to a stub. Two other men were already there. Nessit, who came most Sundays. And a younger man Matta did not recognize, wearing moccasins under his breeches and looking at his own hands.

The minister came in.

Reverend Aldgate was a small man who had found that God gave him height. He moved through the church with the certainty of a man who knew where every object in the room was and had decided each one was correctly placed. He had been at Port Tobacco for six years. In that time he had baptized four Indigenous people, which he counted carefully, the way a merchant counts profitable transactions.

Matta was the first.

The service began. *Dearly beloved brethren, the Scripture moveth us in sundry places to acknowledge and confess our manifold sins and wickedness.* The congregation found the words together, a sound like a single large body breathing. Matta knew the responses. He had read the Book of Common Prayer until the pages at the ordinary services had gone soft at the corners from his thumb. He spoke the words at the correct volume. Not loudly, which would seem like performance. Not quietly, which would seem like reluctance.

The psalms were lined out by the clerk, a young man with a reedy voice who read each line before the congregation sang it back. Matta sang. He had a decent voice and he used it without flourish, blending into the sound the way a stone blends into a riverbed. Present, unremarkable, part of the thing.

He felt the eyes.

Not hostile eyes, mostly. The congregation at Port Tobacco had grown accustomed to him over eleven years. What he felt was the particular quality of attention that was not quite looking. The slight adjustment of a head. The brief stillness of a body in the middle of some other motion. He was noticed in the way a thing is noticed when it is almost but not quite where it belongs.

He kept his eyes on the pulpit.

Aldgate's sermon was on the parable of the talents. He preached it with the energy of a man who believed his congregation was not doing enough with what God had given them, which was probably true of most congregations. Matta listened with the part of his mind that tracked English and let the other part run quietly underneath, thinking about the ordinance, thinking about what was in the cabin, thinking about which things could be moved and where.

*Seized and condemned.*

The medicine bundle was the problem. Everything else could be explained or hidden or made to look like something else. The rattle he kept wrapped in sacking at the back of the woodpile. The pipe, the ceremony pipe, not the one he smoked in the yard, was in a clay pot buried under the floor of the smokehouse, which no constable was going to dig up without a specific reason. But the medicine bundle was large and wrapped in painted deerskin and there was no English explanation for it that would satisfy a man who had already decided what it was.

He would need to move it.

The sermon ended. The congregation stood for the final prayers. Matta stood with them, the Bible open in his hands to the correct page, though he did not need the page.

Afterward, in the yard, Aldgate found him.

This was the part Matta had been preparing for since he put on the shoes.

"Matta." The minister's voice carried the warmth of a man who has accomplished something and wants to be seen accomplishing it. He put his hand out and Matta shook it. "A cold morning for it."

"Cold enough," Matta said.

"Your family well?"

"Well enough, Reverend. Thank you."

Aldgate smiled. It was a genuine smile, which was almost worse than a false one would have been. "I am glad to see you faithful," he said. "In these times, it is a comfort." He let the words sit there for a moment, freighted with something he did not say directly. *In these times.*

"I find comfort in it," Matta said.

The minister nodded. His eyes moved briefly to Matta's coat, the shoulders, the button that did not match, and then back to his face with the practiced smoothness of a man who has taught himself not to notice certain things. "There is talk," he said, lowering his voice by a degree, "of a new ordinance from the county. You may have heard."

Matta kept his face the way he kept his pace on the road. "I may have heard something."

"I would not worry unduly." Aldgate's tone was the tone of a man who has not thought very hard about whether the person he is reassuring has cause to worry. "It is aimed at those who persist in, in the old ways. Not at men of faith."

"Of course," Matta said.

"Still." The minister looked at him with the expression of a man who believes he is being kind. "If there were any objects in your household that might be misread by someone less understanding than myself, it would be wise to." He moved his hand in a small gesture that meant *dispose of them* without saying so.

"I'll bear that in mind," Matta said.

Aldgate nodded, satisfied. He moved on to the next cluster of congregants, his hand extended, his voice warming again to its Sunday register.

Matta watched him go.

Then he turned and walked toward the courthouse., -

The ordinance was nailed to the door with two iron tacks, the paper darkened along the upper edge where the rain had run off the courthouse eave. The ink was iron-gall, gone brown at the edges of each letter, and someone had pressed a thumb into the lower corner so that the words there were smudged into illegibility. Matta stood in front of it and read it through once, quickly, the way he read things he did not want anyone to see him reading.

Then he read it again, slowly.

*Whereas divers Indians yet persist in their heathenish superstitions, to the scandal of the Christian religion and the disturbance of the peace of this Province, be it enacted that all implements, objects, and paraphernalia used or kept for the purposes of heathenish rites, powaws, or Indian conjurations, found within any Indian household or upon any Indian person within this county, shall be subject to seizure and condemnation, to be proceeded against as the law directs, the same to be enforced by constables, justices, and such persons as shall be appointed thereunto, on pain of forfeiture and, upon refusal to surrender, commitment to the common gaol.*

He read the word *household* again. He read *found within*.

A family came past him on their way from church, the mother glancing at the notice with the brief, satisfied look of someone who has seen a problem being addressed. The father did not look at it at all. The children looked at Matta.

He folded his hands behind his back and waited until they had passed.

*Paraphernalia.* He turned the word over. It was a large word, designed to be large, designed to hold as much as whoever was doing the holding needed it to hold. A carved figure could be paraphernalia. A bundle of tobacco could be paraphernalia. A pipe. A rattle. A length of painted deerskin with the symbols of his grandmother's grandmother worked into the grain of it in colors that had no English names.

The medicine bundle.

He needed to move it today, before he went home, while he was still thinking clearly. Not to a neighbor's house. That would put the neighbor at risk, and there were few enough people he could ask that kind of thing of. Not into the woods, where it could be found by anyone. It needed to go somewhere that no constable would think to look, somewhere that was neither his household nor upon his person.

He thought of the mound.

He had not been there in three weeks. The ceremony had been the last time, the autumn one, the one his father had taught him and his father's father had taught his father, the one that had no English name and needed none. He had gone alone in the dark and come back before the light changed and his wife had not asked him where he had been because she already knew.

The mound was half a mile east of the cabin, in the high ground above the creek bottom, where the tulip poplars grew thick on the ridge. It was old. Older than anyone's grandfather, older than the oldest story he had been told about it. The colonists who had bought the surrounding land thought it was a natural rise, a quirk of the ground, and he had never told them otherwise.

He looked at the ordinance one more time.

*Seizure and condemnation.*

He thought: *Not this. Not while I am alive to prevent it.*

He turned from the courthouse door and walked back up the lane, past the church, past the ordinary with its patient horses, out onto the road that led home. His pace was the same as it had been coming in. The invisible pace. The pace of a man with nowhere urgent to be.

But his mind was already at the mound, already measuring what could be carried and what could be hidden and how much time he had before the constable with the warrant came to his door.

He had the beginning of a plan by the time the church bell was out of earshot. But a beginning was not enough. He needed the whole of it.

The tobacco merchant's shop stood two doors past the ordinary, its sign, a painted twist of brown leaf, swinging in the cold air off the river. Matta pushed the door open.

The smell hit him first: dried tobacco, sharp and sweet and faintly rotten, layered over tallow and the sourness of a fire that had been burning since before dawn. The merchant, a red-faced man named Coates who had sold Matta tobacco for eleven years without once remembering his name, looked up from the counter.

"Twist," Matta said. "Half a pound."

Coates reached under the counter without a word. This was the transaction they always had, and it had the worn smoothness of a thing done many times. Matta set his coins on the counter, four pence ha'penny, the price having gone up a farthing since September, and watched Coates wrap the tobacco in a scrap of brown paper and tie it with a length of twine.

"Cold enough," Coates said.

"Cold enough," Matta agreed.

He put the tobacco in his coat pocket and went back out into the lane.

The family who had passed him at the courthouse was gone. A cart horse stood at the rail outside the ordinary, its breath making small clouds, its head low and patient. From inside the ordinary came the sound of men's voices, the particular loose sound of men who have come from church and are now doing the thing they came to church to justify.

Matta walked past without slowing.

The ordinance was still there on the courthouse door. He had not expected it to be gone, but some part of him had hoped. He stopped in front of it again, not to read it. He had it now, the whole of it, word by word. He stopped to look at the thing itself. The paper was rag stock, thick and slightly off-white, the kind a clerk would use for a fair copy. The iron-gall ink had dried to a color almost black at the centers of the letters and brown at the edges, the way such ink always aged. Someone had pressed the tack through the heading so that *By His Excellency the Governor* was slightly puckered, the letters pulling apart at the nail hole.

His father had taught him to read documents the way a man reads a river before crossing it. Not for what it shows, but for what it hides. *The current is never where the surface tells you it is.* He had been twelve when his father said that, standing at the edge of the Patuxent with a rolled paper in his hand, some county notice about a road survey. *Read it again. Find the seam.*

The seam in this one was the word *household*.

*Found within any Indian household or upon any Indian person.*

Not *upon any land.* Not *within any mile.* Not *within any building or outbuilding or structure.* The word was *household*. The domestic space, the hearthside, the interior of the home. A thing kept in a mound was not in a household. A thing buried in the ground of the high ridge above the creek was not upon any person. The ordinance, for all its bulk, for all its *heathenish superstitions* and *seizure and condemnation* and the long, self-satisfied weight of its Latin-rooted words, had a hole in it exactly the size of what he needed to move.

He did not let himself feel relieved. Relief was a thing you felt after. He was still in the before.

He read the penalty clause one more time. *On pain of forfeiture and, upon refusal to surrender, commitment to the common gaol.* Twenty shillings, Aldgate had said. Twenty shillings was three months of what a man like Coates paid a hired hand. For Matta it was the winter's supply of cornmeal and salt and the new axe head he needed before February. It was everything that stood between his family and a cold spring.

The constable who would come to his door, he knew which one it would be, a man named Pratt who lived two miles east and who had the look of a man who had waited a long time for a task that suited his temperament, would not come on his own judgment. He would come on sworn complaint of a neighbor. That was the mechanism: *on sworn complaint.* Which meant someone would have to go to Pratt, put their hand on a Bible, and say: *I have seen, in the household of the Indian called Matta, objects used for heathenish rites.*

He thought about his neighbors. He thought about each of them in turn, the way he thought about the congregation each Sunday. Not with hatred, not with love, but with the careful, measuring attention of a man who has learned that the difference between a neighbor and a threat is often a single bad season.

Most of them would not do it. Not from goodness. From the same incuriosity that kept them from asking what he did on the dark mornings when he came back from the high ground with mud on his moccasins and said nothing about where he had been.

But one of them might. That was enough.

He turned away from the courthouse door for the last time and walked up the lane. The cold had sharpened since morning. The sky to the northwest had gone the color of old pewter, and the smell in the air, a particular flat, iron smell, the smell his father had taught him to read before Matta knew the English word for snow, said that the weather would change before nightfall.

He had the tobacco in his pocket. He had the ordinance in his head, every word of it, including the seam. He had the afternoon, which was enough time if he did not waste it.

The road home ran east past the church, past the last of the county-seat buildings, out into the open ground where the fields had been harvested and the stalks stood broken and pale in the cold. He walked it at the same pace he always walked it. The pace of a man with nowhere urgent to be, a man who has been to church and bought his tobacco and is going home to his wife and his fire. The pace that attracted no attention.

But he was already making the list.

The medicine bundle, first. The painted deerskin with the old symbols. The small carved figure his grandmother had pressed into his hands the winter she died, the one she had called by a name that had no English translation and that he had never spoken aloud in any colonial space. The gourd rattle that had been his father's and his father's father's before that, the one that still held seeds from a gourd that no longer grew in this county.

He would not take the pipe bowl. Not yet. The pipe bowl was the one thing he used in both lives, the one that crossed the seam, and moving it would be a kind of surrender he was not ready for. He would keep the pipe bowl and risk the pipe bowl. If Pratt came to his door and found it, he would say it was for tobacco, which was also true, and let Pratt decide what to do with a truth that was also a lie.

The mound was not far. Half a mile east of the cabin, up the slope through the tulip poplars, the ground rising in a long curve that the colonists had never looked at closely enough to read. He would go before the light failed. He would go the way he always went. Alone, in the quiet, without telling anyone where he was going, because the people who needed to know already knew, and the people who did not need to know were better off not knowing.

He turned off the road at the familiar gap in the fence line and began the walk up through the fields toward home, the tobacco twist a small solid weight in his pocket, the plan taking shape with each step., -

The chest was under the sleeping platform, pushed to the back where the wall met the floor joists, and it took him a moment to work it free. The wood had swollen with the cold and the damp, which was useful, which was part of why he had put it there. He set it on the bench by the dying fire and opened it with the key he kept on a cord around his neck, inside his shirt, against his skin.

Two ledgers. He lifted them both out and set them side by side on the bench.

The first was a proper account book, the kind sold at the trading post in La Plata. Marbled boards, sewn signatures, good rag paper that took ink cleanly. He had bought it three years ago from a man named Prewitt who had ordered two and needed only one, and the transaction had cost him four shillings and a pound of cured tobacco, which he had recorded on the first page in the column marked *disbursements.* The ledger was full of such records: tobacco sold to Hargrove in October, six pounds at threepence the pound; a day's labor clearing fence line for the widow Sims in March, one shilling sixpence; a debt of four shillings owed by the younger Colton boy for a cord of split wood, still outstanding. The entries were in English, in his careful hand, the letters formed the way the schoolmaster at the mission had taught him. Upright, even, the kind of hand a magistrate could read without effort and find nothing in but commerce. He had shown this ledger to a constable once, three years ago, when there had been a question about a horse. The constable had looked at two pages, nodded, and left. The ledger had done its work.

The second ledger was smaller, bound in deerskin he had tanned himself, the pages cut from the same rag stock but left unruled. The script in it was not English and not Algonquian written in English letters. It was a notation system he had worked out with his mother in the years before she died, part syllabary, part pictograph, part a set of marks that meant things only if you knew what they meant. Two living people knew what they meant: himself, and Tessa.

Tessa was young still, a girl learning the ceramic craft at his side, her hands not yet certain in the clay, her eye already better than her hands. She had her grandfather's patience, which was the most important thing. And she had something else he had not expected. She asked questions that went sideways, that came at a thing from an angle he had not considered, and when he answered her she listened with her whole body the way children listen before they learn to perform listening. He had begun teaching her the notation two winters ago, starting with the simpler marks, the ones that recorded objects and names. She did not yet know the full account of what the second ledger held. But she was learning, and she would be ready before he needed her to be.

He looked at the second ledger for a long time.

The fire had gone to coals. The room was cold enough now that he could see his breath, faint and brief, dissolving before it reached the ceiling. He did not add wood. He sat with the two ledgers open side by side on the bench and looked at them the way he looked at a piece of work he was about to cut. Measuring twice, because once was how you made a mistake you could not take back.

The ordinance said *household.* He had turned that word in his mind all the way home from Port Tobacco, the way you work a knot with your fingers before you try a knife on it. *Found in the household of any Indian.* The objects in his keeping were here, in this room, under this roof. If a constable came with a warrant, and a constable could come with a warrant, because the ordinance had made the warrant permanent and general and requiring only a neighbor's word to activate, then the objects were here to be found.

He closed the second ledger and held it in both hands.

Every word in a law had a boundary, and the boundary was the only part of the law that mattered, because everything inside the boundary was controlled and everything outside the boundary was not. He had learned this from his father, who had learned it from watching a surveyor drive stakes, and the lesson was the same: a line drawn in one place is not drawn in another, and the difference between the two sides of the line is everything.

His household ended at his walls.

He stood up. He put the second ledger back in the chest, locked it, worked the chest back under the sleeping platform into the cold dark against the joists. Then he stood in the middle of the room and looked at the walls.

The Owens family's rattle. The old woman's bundle. The carved figure. Three objects, and there were others he was already being asked to hold. He had felt the asking in the way people spoke to him after the service that morning, the particular way a man's eyes move when he is about to say something he cannot say in front of witnesses. There would be more.

He could not keep them here.

He thought about the hollow tree at the edge of the field, a dead chestnut struck by lightning two summers ago, the heartwood rotted out to a cavity large enough to put your arm in to the elbow. He thought about the space under the floor of the smokehouse, where the boards were loose and the ground was dry because the smoke had cured it. He thought about the stone root cellar his neighbor Prewitt had abandoned when Prewitt moved his household north, the door still on its hinges, the walls still sound, the location known to Matta because he had helped Prewitt dig it and had never mentioned this to anyone.

Not his household. None of them his household.

The constable could search every room of this house and find the first ledger and the chest with the second ledger locked inside it, and if the constable could not read the notation, and the constable could not read the notation, then the second ledger was a craftsman's tally and nothing more. The objects would not be here. The objects would be in the hollow tree, in the dry earth under the smokehouse floor, in the abandoned root cellar two fields east, distributed across the dark in separate locations that were connected by nothing a constable could see.

He would need to tell Tessa. She was the right person. She had already shown him, in small ways, that she understood which world she was choosing, and she already knew the notation. He had been watching her long enough to know she was ready for more than he had given her. He would bring her to the mound before the ground froze, show her where things were placed, and give her the name for this ground in the language that was not English. A thing held in trust belongs to the people who trusted you with it, and his death would not change the ownership, only the obligation.

He opened the second ledger one more time, on the bench in the last of the firelight, and added three lines in the notation: the hollow tree, described by its position relative to the field's northeast corner; the smokehouse floor, described by the number of boards from the south wall; the Prewitt cellar, described by the landmark only he and Prewitt had used, which was a particular stone in the boundary wall shaped like a sleeping dog. Then he locked the ledger away again and sat for a moment with the key warm in his fist.

It was not a solution. He knew it was not a solution. It was a deferral, a way of buying time, and time was not the same as safety. The ordinance would be enforced by men who were paid to enforce it and who had neighbors willing to speak, and the neighbors who were willing to speak were not all of them moved by religion. Some were moved by the simpler fact that his land was adjacent to theirs and his land was good land and the law had just made it easier to find a reason to take it. He had seen this before. His father had seen it before his father. The mechanism was not new.

But the hollow tree was not his household. The smokehouse floor was not his household. The Prewitt cellar was not his household. And the law, for now, said *household.*

He would work within the word they had given him, and he would work carefully, and he would teach Tessa the locations, and he would keep the second ledger locked, and he would show the first ledger to any constable who asked, and he would attend the service next Sunday and speak the responses in the correct places and sing the psalm in the lined-out fashion the clerk preferred.

He would do all of this. And it would not be enough forever. But forever was not the question. The question was: enough for now, enough for the next season, enough to pass the objects forward to the hands that came after his.

He put the key back inside his shirt. It settled against his sternum, cold for a moment, then warming.

He reads it twice and begins to plan. That was what he had told himself, standing at the courthouse door. Read it twice. Find the seam. Begin.

He had done all three., -

The evening ceremony was not a large thing.

It could not be, not in 1738, not in Charles County with the ordinance still fresh on the courthouse door and the ink not yet fully brown. Six people came, which was the number that could move through the dark without being heard from the road. They came separately, at intervals, through the gap in the fence line and up the slope through the tulip poplars to the high ground above the creek. No lanterns. The quarter moon gave enough light to walk by if you knew the ground, and everyone who came knew the ground.

Matta was there first. He had come ahead of the others to build the fire, a small one, set in the depression at the center of the mound's crown where the earth was bare and the old stones ringed the ash of many previous fires. He used dry cedar, which burned hot and nearly smokeless, and he fed it carefully, keeping the flame low enough that it would not be visible above the tree line from the road below.

The others arrived in ones and twos. Old Nessit, who had been in the back pew that morning and who Matta had watched from the corner of his eye during the sermon. The Owens woman, whose rattle was now in the hollow chestnut, who came in her matchcoat with her head covered and her feet silent on the grass. Two younger men whose names Matta would not record in either ledger, because some things were safer unnamed in writing. And, last, a woman he had not expected: the widow from the farm east of the creek, who had attended the Anglican service that morning in her best kerchief and who now stood at the edge of the firelight in a different kind of stillness entirely.

No one spoke of the ordinance. That was understood.

Matta opened the ceremony in the old form. His voice was low, not whispering but contained, the words shaped for the space between people rather than for a room or a crowd. The language was not English. It was the language his grandmother had given him, the one that had no written form in any ledger he kept, the one that lived only in mouths and in memory and in the particular quality of attention that a person brings when they know they are the vessel for something that cannot be replaced if it is lost.

The fire shifted. Cedar smoke rose and spread and the smell of it was not the smell of tallow candles in an Anglican church. It was older than that and it knew it.

The call-and-response began. Matta led and the others answered, their voices finding the pattern the way hands find familiar work in the dark. The rhythm was a slow one, built on a phrase that repeated and changed and repeated again, and underneath it the Owens woman's feet found the beat on the packed earth, a soft shuffling sound that was barely sound at all. From the creek bottom came the voices of tree frogs, early for the season, and from somewhere east a dog barked once and was silent.

Matta did not think about the ordinance during the ceremony. He had made a practice of this, over the years, of keeping the two lives from bleeding into each other at the moments that mattered most. The Sunday morning belonged to the Book of Common Prayer and the lined-out psalms and the particular quality of Aldgate's attention. This hour, this fire, this ground belonged to something else, something that had no English name because it had not needed one until the English arrived and began naming things that were not theirs to name.

He thought about his grandmother. He thought about the name she had given this place. He thought about the pipe bowl in the earth beneath the middle stone, wrapped in oilcloth, waiting.

The ceremony lasted perhaps an hour. When it was finished, the fire was tamped down and the ash covered and the six people left as they had come, separately, at intervals, through the dark. Matta stayed last. He stood for a moment on the mound's crown with the fire cold beneath him and the stars overhead and the smell of cedar still in his coat.

He thought about Tessa.

He would bring her here. Not to the ceremony, not yet. First to the ground itself, in the daylight, on a day with a reason. He would show her the three stones and their angles. He would give her the name for this place in the language that was not English. He would tell her what was in the earth beneath the middle stone, and he would tell her that the knowing of it was now hers as well as his.

Because he was one man. He had understood this since the afternoon, since the courthouse door with the ordinance nailed to it and the iron-gall ink gone brown at the letter edges. One man could be sick. One man could be taken. One man could be gone before the telling was done. And the objects in trust were not his to lose.

He looked down at the three stones.

They looked like nothing. They looked like stones that had always been there, which they had. They looked like a coincidence of the ground, which they were not. They looked like something a man could walk past a hundred times without seeing, which was exactly what they needed to look like.

He had learned to make things look like what they needed to look like. He had been learning this his whole life. He was very good at it.

He turned and walked back toward the house.

The cold followed him across the field, settling into his coat, into the backs of his hands where the skin had gone rough from the earlier digging. The house was dark except for the coals showing orange through the gap in the door. He stood outside it for a moment, looking at the shape of it against the sky. The low roofline, the chimney, the small window where the oilpaper let through no light at all. His house. His household. The word the ordinance used.

He thought: *How long can a man hold two lives before one of them cracks?*

And then, because he was a craftsman and a craftsman thinks in terms of what the material will bear, he thought: *That is not the question. The question is whether Tessa is ready to hold what I am about to put in her hands.*

She was. He had been watching her long enough to know it.

He went inside. He sat by the coals for a while with his hands open on his knees, feeling the warmth come back into them. Then he went to bed.
