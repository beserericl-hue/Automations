The kitchen smelled of the coffee she'd made at nine and forgotten to drink. It sat on the counter going cold, a dark ring forming at the waterline, and Kimi had not moved from the table in two hours.

Tayak's email was open on the screen. She had read it four times. The attachment — the forty-three-page field assessment, file size 2.7 MB, named with Tayak's characteristic precision: *18CH-447_FieldAssessment_Final_Moyaone.pdf* — sat below the message body like something she wasn't allowed to touch yet.

The authorization line was in the third paragraph. She read it again.

*You have my explicit permission to use this report publicly, in any format you judge appropriate, in connection with the recognition campaign. I am trusting your judgment on timing and framing. Don't make me regret it.*

That last sentence was Tayak all the way through. She could hear her cousin's voice in it — the affection buried under the warning, the trust extended like a hand that was also a test.

Kimi set her phone face-down on the table. She did not want to see the notifications. She'd had forty-seven texts since the press conference, and she had answered none of them, and Dana Osei's message was still sitting there unanswered the way Tayak had told her to leave it, and she was following that instruction because Tayak was right and because following it cost her something, which meant it was probably the correct thing to do.

She opened her laptop and made a new folder on the desktop. She typed the name slowly, one character at a time: *Archive_Final.*

Then she sat back and looked at it.

The folder was empty. The name felt presumptuous, like buying the frame before you had the painting. But she did not change it. Naming it right mattered. The name was a commitment.

She got up, found a yellow legal pad in the drawer beneath the silverware — the one she'd been meaning to move somewhere more sensible for six months — and brought it back to the table. She uncapped a pen and wrote at the top of the first page, pressing hard enough that the letters would not smear: *What I have. What I need.*

She drew a line down the center of the page.

On the left side she wrote *HAVE* and started listing.

The field assessment — Tayak's report, just authorized, forty-three pages, covering the stratigraphy from the surface down to the deepest layer, the copper pendant in stratum five, the cord-marked ceramic in the east transect, the radiocarbon dates, the chain of custody for every artifact. All of it in careful qualified language that would read, to anyone who knew how to read it, as one of the strongest pre-contact presence arguments she had ever seen assembled in a single document.

The 1697 petition. She had the scan Wren had found — high-resolution, the rag paper showing its chain lines clearly, the iron gall ink browned to a dark sepia that looked almost like dried blood on screen. The deckled edges. The partial signatures. She had read it so many times she could recite the opening line from memory: *The humble petition of the Piscataway people, humbly beseeching that the high ground at Mattawoman Creek, being the ancient possessors' ground of burial—*

She stopped. Wrote it on the left side of the page. *1697 petition — scan (Wren's copy). Check resolution. Need to confirm repository watermark is legible.*

Nora's affidavit. Tayak had it. Tayak had read it in the trench. Kimi had not read it herself — had not been offered it — but Tayak had told her enough: sixty-two years old, notarized in Charles County in 1962, the jurat signed under the pains and penalties of perjury, the chain of names running from Yanu all the way forward. The question was whether Tayak would authorize the affidavit for the archive the same way she'd authorized the report.

Kimi wrote on the right side of the page, under *NEED*: *Nora's affidavit — ask Tayak. Separately: ask Nora.*

She wrote that second line deliberately. Tayak was not the only person who got to make that decision.

The artifact photographs. She had Wren's documentation images — the copper pendant in situ, the cord-marked ceramic rim sherd, the shell gorget from stratum four, the antler figure from stratum six. Wren had sent her a set of the field documentation photos three weeks ago, before any of this had escalated, when Kimi had asked for something visual for the campaign page. They were not the conservation suite photographs — those would be Tayak's to release or not — but the field documentation images were part of the public excavation record, and Kimi had checked: under the Maryland Public Records Act, field documentation produced on a state-permitted excavation was presumptively public unless the lead archaeologist filed a restriction, and Tayak had not filed one.

She wrote it on the left side. *Artifact photos (field documentation set — Wren's). Check public records status. Already clear.*

The land grant chain. This was the piece she was most uncertain about. She had the 1687 Calvert patent printout — the one Marcus Redcloud's legal team had circulated — with the boundary description that used the Piscataway burial mound as a survey marker. She had the chain from Calvert through Sorrell through the Harrington partition. She had the 1683 county court order, the one that displaced the Piscataway for forty shillings, the one that had led Garrett Slade to sit in a records room six weeks ago and understand something about his own family that he had not known before.

She did not know what Slade intended to do with that understanding. She did not know if he would sign the designation packet or revise it or refuse it. What she knew was that the land grant chain was a matter of public record — every deed volume, every book and page reference — and that she could build it herself from the county registry scans if she had to.

She wrote it on the left side with an asterisk: *Land grant chain — 1683 to present. Public record. Build independently if needed.*

Then she came to the last item, and she set the pen down.

The video of Nora.

She had recorded it herself, three weeks ago, on her phone, in Nora's kitchen on Billingsley Road. Nora had agreed to it — had sat at her own kitchen table in the good light from the south window and spoken for thirty-one minutes without stopping. The clip-on lavalier mic had been borrowed from a friend who did documentary work, and the audio was clean, and Nora's voice in the recording was steady and unhurried and eighty-two years old, and it carried in it something that no document, however well-scanned, could carry.

Nora had spoken the names. All of them that she would say on a recording. From Yanu — the first name in the chain that Nora would give to a microphone — forward through every generation she knew, each one placed in sequence, the older names said with the care of someone handling something fragile, the more recent ones said faster because they were closer and the closeness made them easier and harder at the same time. She had ended with Tayak.

She had not said the name at the bottom. The name before Yanu — the first woman's name, the one that required a weighted pause before its second syllable. She had said: *The one before Yanu I will say in person. Not on a recording. Not yet.*

Kimi had not pressed her. She understood, or thought she did. Some things required the right place.

She wrote on the left side: *Video — Nora, names recitation, 31 min, lavalier audio, 1080p.* Then she paused and added: *Incomplete — bottom name withheld. Note this in the archive. Do not hide the gap.*

That felt important. She wrote it again on a separate line, underlined: *Do not hide the gap.*

She sat back and looked at the two columns.

The left side was longer than the right. That was good. That was more than she'd expected to have when this had started, when she'd been reading a press release about a colonial landmark designation and feeling the particular cold anger of someone who has seen this before and knows exactly what it means and cannot yet prove it to anyone who isn't already convinced.

She looked at the folder on her desktop. *Archive_Final.* Still empty.

She did not open it yet.

Instead she turned to a fresh page on the legal pad and wrote at the top: *Errors I cannot make.*

She stared at that heading for a moment. Then she started writing.

*One: Upload anything not confirmed as public record without checking twice. The state will find the one document with a chain-of-custody question and use it to discredit the rest.*

*Two: Misrepresent what the report says. It says pre-contact deposits to approximately 100 BC. It does not say 'proof of unbroken occupation.' The distinction matters. Anyone who wants to discredit this will use imprecision against us.*

*Three: Publish before the structure is right. The archive has to tell a story in order. Land grant chain first — the colonial record establishing that the state knew. Then the petition — the community's own voice in 1697. Then the affidavit — the living chain. Then the report — the ground itself. Then the names. The names last.*

She stopped. Read that back.

The names last. She had not planned it that way consciously, but it was right. The names were not evidence in the legal sense. They were something else. They were the thing the evidence was in service of. You put the evidence first so that by the time someone heard Nora's voice saying those names in sequence — Yanu, and all the ones that came after, and Tayak at the end — they already understood what they were hearing.

She wrote: *Four: Go too fast.* Then she crossed it out. That one was not an error she could make a rule about. That one was a feeling she had to manage every hour for the next forty-eight.

The coffee on the counter was cold enough now that she could smell the staleness from across the room. She got up, poured it down the drain, and filled the kettle. While the water heated she stood at the window over the sink and looked out at the street. It was past eleven. The neighborhood was quiet. Someone had left a porch light on three houses down, and it threw a yellow oval on the sidewalk that the wind moved leaves across in slow, uneven pulses.

She thought about Saya. She always thought about Saya at moments like this — the woman who had stood in a colonial assembly with ink drying on her palm, who had understood that two forms of keeping were better than one, that paper was for courts and story was for people, that you needed both because each one could survive what the other could not. Saya had known that the petition might fail and had buried it anyway. She had known that the story might thin and had told it anyway. She had done both things at once because she was not willing to bet everything on a single form.

Kimi was not willing to bet everything on a single form either.

The kettle clicked. She made tea — a bag of Irish breakfast, the one thing she and Tayak agreed on — and carried the mug back to the table.

She opened the folder. She opened Tayak's email. She right-clicked the attachment and saved it to *Archive_Final* with the filename Tayak had given it, unchanged. *18CH-447_FieldAssessment_Final_Moyaone.pdf.*

One file. The folder now contained one file.

She looked at it for a moment. Then she opened a browser tab and navigated to the county registry of deeds digital archive. The earliest volumes had been scanned as black-and-white images of handwritten folios, the pages uneven, the margins crowded with later scribbled references in different hands. She had done this before — had spent two evenings in October working through the volumes — and she knew where to start.

She typed into the search field: *Liber 4, page 312.*

The scan loaded slowly. When it resolved, she could see the handwriting clearly — the secretary hand of a colonial court clerk, the ink browned and the fold lines cutting across the text at regular intervals where the document had been stored in a bundle for three hundred years. At the top of the page, in a slightly different hand that suggested a later addition, someone had written a single word in the margin.

She leaned closer to the screen.

The word was *Indian.*

She sat very still for a moment. Then she opened a new subfolder inside *Archive_Final* and named it *land_records.* She took a screenshot of the folio, noted the book and page reference on her legal pad, and kept going.

The Liber 4 scan was the easy one. She had found it in October and knew its shape — the 1683 court order, the forty shillings, the high ground near Mattawoman Creek transferred to William Dent while the Piscataway still lived on it. She saved the screenshot, labeled it *1683_Dent_grant_Liber4_p312.jpg*, and moved on.

The 1687 Calvert patent took twenty minutes to locate. The county registry's digital index was organized by grantee surname, and Calvert appeared forty-seven times across three centuries. She worked through them in chronological order, cross-checking against the notes Marcus Redcloud's legal team had circulated — the ones with the boundary description that named the Piscataway settlement at the high ground as a survey marker. When she found it, she read the boundary language twice before saving it.

*Beginning at a marked white oak standing on the east side of the high ground adjacent to Mattawoman Creek, being the same ground held in use by the Piscataway people as a place of their dead.*

The colonial government had known. They had written it into the patent. They had used the mound as a landmark because it was the most permanent thing they could find, and then they had granted the land around it to an Englishman anyway.

She saved the file. *1687_Calvert_patent_Liber5_p88.jpg.* Then she kept going.

The chain from Calvert to Sorrell to Anne Calvert to James Harrington she had reconstructed in October. She had the book and page references on her legal pad from that session, and she worked through them now in sequence, pulling each scan from the registry archive, checking the boundary language in each deed to see when it changed and when it didn't. The 1712 Sorrell partition still copied the Calvert language verbatim — *the high ground held in use by the Piscataway people* — as if the clerk had simply moved the words forward without reading them. By 1741, when Anne Calvert transferred the residue to James Harrington, the language had been softened: *the high ground adjacent to Mattawoman Creek, formerly occupied.* Formerly. Someone had made a choice about that word.

She noted it on the legal pad. *1741 — 'formerly occupied.' First erasure in the deed language. Document it.*

By one in the morning she had eight files in the *land_records* subfolder, arranged chronologically from 1683 to 1922, when Harold Slade had purchased the parcel from George Wills for eight hundred dollars. The chain was complete. It ran from a colonial court order displacing the Piscataway to the family of the man who had signed the suspension notice, and it was entirely in the public record, documented in the county registry of deeds, available to anyone with a browser and enough patience to work through the volumes.

She made a second mug of tea. While the bag steeped she stood at the counter and looked at her legal pad.

The petition was next.

She had a high-resolution scan of the 1697 petition — Wren had sent it weeks ago, a TIFF file large enough that it took thirty seconds to open. The original was in the Maryland State Archives, filed under the colonial court records for that winter session, and it was technically a public record under state law. She had checked this twice. She had also checked that the scan itself, made from a public-record original, carried no reproduction restriction. It did not.

She opened the file.

The petition was written on hand-laid rag paper, the chain lines visible in the scan as faint parallel shadows running across the page. The ink had browned to a dark sepia — iron gall, she knew from Wren's notes — and there were three small holes near the left margin where the acid in the ink had eaten through the fiber over three centuries. The handwriting was a colonial secretary hand, neat and practiced, but at the bottom of the page, below the formal closing, there were two signatures in a different hand — rougher, the letters formed with the careful deliberation of someone who had learned to write in a language not their own. And beside one of them, in the clerk's hand, a small notation: *her mark.*

She had read this document many times. She read it again now.

*The humble petition of the Piscataway people, humbly beseeching that the high ground adjacent to Mattawoman Creek, being the place of their dead from time beyond the memory of any living person, be acknowledged as held by them as ancient possessors of this ground, that no further grant or patent issue for the same, and that the said ground remain inviolate for the keeping of their dead and the continuance of their people in this county.*

*Ancient possessors.* Judge Notley's phrase, carried forward into the petition itself. Saya had known what she was doing — had taken the colonial court's own language and turned it back on the colonial court, made it into an argument the court would have to answer in its own terms.

The petition had failed. The court had received it and done nothing. But Ware had entered it into the record, and the record had survived, and now Kimi was going to put it on the internet where two million people could read it before the state could decide what to do about it.

She saved the file to a new subfolder. *1697_petition_scan_hd.tiff.* Then she created a PDF version at a lower resolution for easier loading and saved that too. *1697_petition_scan_web.pdf.*

She looked at the time. One forty-seven.

The affidavit was already in her possession — Tayak had sent it as a separate attachment, scanned from the original that Nora kept in a cedar box in the back bedroom. The affidavit was sixty-two years old, notarized in Charles County in 1962, the jurat at the bottom in the statutory language of the period: *Sworn to and subscribed before me this fourteenth day of March, nineteen sixty-two.* The notary's seal had been embossed into the paper and showed clearly in the scan as a circular impression, slightly off-center, the ink of the stamp faded to near-invisibility but the physical impression still sharp. Eleven pages of plain declarative sentences. Kimi had read it so many times she could recite sections of it.

She saved it. *Nora_affidavit_1962_notarized.pdf.*

Then she opened the subfolder Wren had sent — the artifact photographs. There were forty-three images, shot in the conservation suite under controlled lighting, each artifact photographed against a gray card with a scale bar and a reference number. She went through them one by one.

The copper pendant. Shot from above and from the side, the surface color a deep blue-green where the patina had formed thickest, lighter at the edges where the metal was thinner. The hole at the top, worn smooth from use. She looked at it for a long moment. Someone had worn this. Someone had pressed it between their hands and said a name over it and then laid it in the ground.

She saved it. *artifact_18CH447_051_pendant_obverse.jpg.* And the reverse. And the side view.

The gorget — the older one from stratum four, the shell worked into a flat disc with an incised design she could not quite read at this resolution. The pipe bowl, dark-fired clay, the stem hole still clean. The glass bead, cobalt blue, catching the conservation suite's overhead light in a way that made it look lit from inside.

She saved them all, organized them into a subfolder labeled *artifacts*, and cross-referenced each filename against Tayak's field assessment, noting the stratum and the catalog number for each object. If anyone questioned the chain of custody, the catalog numbers would take them directly to the field log entries, which Tayak had signed and Wren had witnessed.

It was three fifteen. She got up, walked to the bathroom, splashed cold water on her face, and stood looking at herself in the mirror for a moment. Her eyes were tired but her hands were steady. She thought about Saya again — the ink drying on her palm, the petition entered into the record by a clerk who probably did not remember her name the following week. Saya had done what she could do with what she had. Kimi was doing the same thing. The difference was that Kimi had a broadband connection and a county registry that had digitized its colonial folios, and by morning she could put everything Saya had fought for into a form that three hundred years of institutional forgetting could not reach.

She went back to the table.

The field assessment was already in the folder. She opened it now and read the abstract again — the forty-three pages of careful, qualified professional language that said, in the plainest terms Tayak could use without overstating: the mound's earliest deposits dated to approximately 100 BC, the copper pendant had been recovered from stratum five, the cord-marked ceramic from the east transect was consistent with Woodland period manufacture, and the land grant chain documented that the colonial government had acknowledged the site as a Piscataway burial ground at the time of the 1687 patent.

She made a note on her legal pad: *Do not say 'proof of unbroken occupation.' The report does not say that. Say: 'consistent with continuous use.' Say: 'the earliest deposits date to approximately 100 BC.' Say what the report says.*

She added the assessment to the folder index she was building — a plain-text document that would sit at the root of the archive and tell anyone who opened it what each file was and where it had come from and what public record it was drawn from. She had seen other activists build archives without this and watched journalists get lost in the folders and give up. The index was not for her. It was for the person who would open this at six in the morning with a deadline in two hours and need to understand the whole thing in ninety seconds.

She typed the index entry for the assessment: *18CH-447_FieldAssessment_Final_Moyaone.pdf — Forty-three-page field assessment by Dr. Tayak Moyaone, University of Maryland. Documents pre-contact deposits at Site 18CH-447 (Charles County, Maryland) from approximately 100 BC. Includes stratigraphy, artifact catalog, and land grant analysis. Public record; provided by author with permission for public release.*

She read it back. It was accurate. It did not overstate. She kept going.

By four thirty she had the index complete for every file except one.

She looked at the gap in the list. Between the affidavit and the field assessment, she had left a blank line with a single placeholder: *[VIDEO — Nora speaking the names — PENDING].*

She had known this was coming. She had been building toward it for six hours, putting everything else in place first so that when she reached this item she would know exactly what she needed and why she needed it and what it would cost Nora to give it.

She picked up her phone. It was four forty-one in the morning. She should wait until seven. She should let Nora sleep.

She put the phone down.

She looked at the blank line in the index.

The names were the last item. She had planned it that way deliberately — the evidence first, the colonial record and the petition and the affidavit and the ground itself, so that by the time someone reached the video they already understood what they were hearing. Without the video the archive was complete in the legal sense. It had everything the state would need to take seriously, everything a journalist could cite, everything a court could examine. It was a strong archive without the video.

But it was not the same archive.

The evidence told you what had happened. The names told you who it had happened to. And there was no document that could do what Nora's voice could do — an eighty-two-year-old woman sitting in the house on Billingsley Road where she had lived her entire life, saying the names in order from the beginning of the chain she held to the end, from Yanu forward through every generation she knew, and making the span between them audible to anyone who listened.

She picked up the phone again. It was four forty-three.

She called.

It rang four times. She was composing an apology for the hour when the line opened.

"I've been up since three." Nora's voice was clear. Not groggy, not annoyed — just present, the way she always was, as if she had been waiting for something to do. "The hip."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Are you building it?"

"Almost done. I have everything except the video."

A pause. Not hesitation — Kimi had learned to read Nora's pauses. This one was the kind where she was deciding something she had already mostly decided.

"I'll do it once," Nora said. "I won't do it twice. If something goes wrong with the recording, that's the recording we use."

"I understand."

"And you come here. I'm not doing this on a phone screen with you in College Park."

"I can be there by seven."

Another pause. "Come at eight. I'll have had breakfast. And bring that good coffee — not the gas station kind."

"The place on Route 6."

"That's the one." A brief silence, and then Nora said, in a different register — quieter, more careful: "Kimi. The names go all the way back to Yanu. You understand that. Some of them — the older ones — they're hard to hear. They're not easy names."

"I know."

"And there's one I won't say on the recording. The one before Yanu. That one I'll say to Tayak in person. Not into a microphone. Not yet."

Kimi looked at the blank line in her index. *[VIDEO — Nora speaking the names — PENDING.]*

"I understand," she said.

"I want you to be ready for that. For the gap."

"I'll mark it in the archive. I won't hide it."

Another pause, shorter. Something shifted in Nora's breathing — not quite relief, but close to it.

"Eight o'clock," Nora said, and ended the call.

Kimi set the phone down on the table next to the legal pad. The room was quiet. Outside, the porch light three houses down had gone off at some point in the last hour, and the street was dark now except for the orange wash of the corner streetlamp.

She looked at the archive folder. Nine subfolders, forty-seven files, one blank line.

She picked up her pen and wrote at the bottom of the legal pad, below everything else: *The names are not evidence. They are what the evidence is for.*

Then she closed the laptop, set her phone alarm for six thirty, and lay down on the couch without taking off her shoes.

The coffee was still hot when she pulled onto Billingsley Road at seven fifty-eight. She had bought two cups at the place on Route 6 — the one with the hand-lettered chalkboard and the woman behind the counter who always remembered orders but never names — and driven the last four miles with both cups wedged in the passenger seat footwell because the cupholder was broken and she did not trust it with anything that mattered.

Nora's house sat back from the road behind a silver maple that had dropped most of its leaves in the last week. The leaves had been raked into a pile near the fence and left there, which meant Nora had raked them herself and then her hip had said no to the bagging. Kimi made a note to finish that before she left.

She knocked once and went in. That was the rule: knock so she knew you were coming, then come in.

Nora was in the kitchen, in the good chair — the one with the cushioned back that had been reupholstered twice, most recently in a dark green fabric that Nora had chosen herself from a sample book and then second-guessed for a month before deciding she had been right the first time. She was dressed. Hair done. Hands flat on the table in front of her, the way she sat when she was waiting for something she had already prepared for.

"You're early," she said.

"Two minutes."

"That's early." But she was not displeased. She looked at the cups. "Which one is mine."

"The one without the sleeve. They were out of sleeves."

Nora took it. She wrapped both hands around it and said nothing for a moment, just held it, and Kimi sat down across from her and did the same.

The kitchen smelled of toast and the particular warmth of a house where the heat had been on since before dawn — dry and close, with something underneath it that was just the house itself, wood and plaster and eighty-two years of the same woman's cooking. The window above the sink showed the back yard, gray-white sky, the bare stalks of what had been the summer's tomato plants still tied to their stakes with the same frayed baling twine Nora bought every spring from the hardware store in La Plata.

Nora drank. Kimi drank.

"How much did you get done," Nora said.

"Everything else is in. Forty-seven files. The index is done. I just need the video."

Nora nodded. She looked at the table. Then she said, "Show me what it looks like."

Kimi opened her laptop and turned it so Nora could see the archive landing page — the Carrd site she had built between midnight and two, dark background, clean white text, the title at the top: *Site 18CH-447: The Public Record.* Below it, the index. Each file listed with a one-sentence description. The petition. The affidavit. The field assessment. The land grant chain. The artifact photographs. The blank line at the bottom, still there, still marked *[VIDEO — PENDING].*

Nora read it. She read it slowly, the way she read everything — not because she was slow but because she did not like to read something twice when once done right was enough. Her eyes moved down the list. When she reached the blank line she stopped.

"That's where I go," she said.

"Yes."

She looked at it for another moment. Then she turned the laptop back toward Kimi. "All right," she said. "Let's do it."

---

Kimi set her phone on the table between them, propped against the sugar bowl so the microphone faced Nora directly. She opened the voice memo app. The interface was plain — a red button, a waveform display, a running timer. She had tested it in the car on the way over, recording thirty seconds of herself saying the alphabet, playing it back at full volume. The audio was clean.

She looked at Nora. "Ready when you are."

Nora straightened in the good chair. Not a performance of straightening — just the adjustment of a woman who understood that posture was part of how you held something correctly. She set her coffee to the side. She put her hands flat on the table again.

Kimi pressed record.

The red button turned solid. The timer began.

Nora closed her eyes for a moment. Then she opened them, fixed her gaze on a point just above and past the phone, and began.

The first name was Yanu. Kimi had known it would be — Nora had said as much on the phone — but hearing it said here, with the recorder running and the archive waiting, was not the same as knowing it. Nora said it with a particular care, the way you say the name of someone you have carried for a long time: not dramatically, but with the weight of long acquaintance.

Then the next. And the next.

Kimi had thought she was ready. She had told Nora she was ready, and she had meant it. But she had been thinking of readiness as a kind of bracing — something you did with your body, your attention, your professional composure. She had not understood that the names would do something else entirely.

They were not dramatic. That was the thing. Nora did not perform them. She said them the way you say the names of people you have known your whole life — with the particular flatness that comes not from not caring but from caring so long that the caring has become structural, built into the way the name sits in your mouth. Some of them were short. Some had consonant clusters that Kimi could not have reproduced if asked. A few were names she recognized from Tayak's field notes — Okwi, Matta, Tessa — and hearing them here, in Nora's voice, in sequence, was like watching a photograph develop: the outline was familiar but the whole thing was suddenly present in a way it hadn't been before.

Nora did not rush. She did not stumble.

The names moved through centuries the way the creek moved through the county — not because it was trying to get somewhere but because that was what water did, what names did, what people did when they handed something forward with enough care.

Kimi kept her eyes on the waveform. She watched the peaks and valleys of Nora's voice rendered in gray lines on the phone screen, each name a small mountain range, the silences between them the flat ground. She watched it because if she looked at Nora's face she was not sure she would be able to maintain the stillness the moment required.

The names came forward through time. The older ones required more of the mouth, more precision, more of the thing Nora had called *holding without the ground's help.* The later ones were closer, more familiar: Ida. And then the name Kimi had not expected to hear said aloud in this sequence — the name of the woman who had left, Nora's daughter, Tayak's mother — said once, quickly, with a steadiness that cost something Kimi could see in the line of Nora's jaw even though her voice did not waver.

And then: Tayak.

Nora said it the same way she had said all the others. No pause before it, no weight added, no signal that this was the last one. She said it and then she stopped.

The kitchen was quiet.

The timer on the phone read four minutes and eleven seconds.

Nora looked at the table. She did not look at Kimi. She picked up her coffee and held it with both hands and said nothing.

Kimi stopped the recording.

She sat with the phone in her hand for a moment. Then she pressed play and listened to the first ten seconds: the ambient sound of the kitchen, a small breath, and then Nora's voice beginning with Yanu — clear and close and exactly as it had sounded in the room.

The audio was clean.

She stopped it.

"Do you want to hear it," she said.

Nora shook her head. Not quickly — it was a considered movement, a small and final one. "No," she said. "I already know what it sounds like."

She set her coffee down. She looked out the window at the bare tomato stakes in the back yard.

"How long before you put it up," she said.

"A few hours. I want to add captions. A transcript. So it's accessible."

Nora nodded. "Good." She paused. "Will they understand it. The people who watch it."

"Some of them won't. But the ones who need to will."

Nora considered this. "That's probably enough," she said.

Outside, a car went past on Billingsley Road. The sound of it faded. The tomato stakes stood in the pale morning light, still tied with the frayed baling twine.

"There's one more thing," Kimi said.

Nora looked at her.

"The name at the beginning. The first one in the chain — the one before Yanu." Kimi kept her voice steady. "Tayak doesn't know it yet. She hasn't heard it. You said you'd say it to her in person."

Nora was quiet for a moment. "Yes."

"When."

"When she comes here." Nora's voice was plain, without apology. "She needs to be standing in the right place when she hears it. That's not a recording. That's a conversation." A pause. "She'll be angry I waited this long."

"Maybe."

"She has the right to be." Nora picked up her coffee again. "But she'll understand it. Eventually." A brief pause. "She always does. It just takes her a while to get there."

Kimi looked at the phone in her hand. Four minutes and eleven seconds. Forty-seven files plus one. The blank line in the index, about to be filled — but not all the way. The gap would stay marked. She had promised.

She thought about Tayak at the site right now, or in the car, or wherever she was in the early morning — not knowing that by the time she looked at her phone today the archive would be live and Nora's voice would be moving through it, saying her name at the end of a chain that went back through Ida and Saya and Matta and Tessa and Okwi and Yanu and further still, to a name Tayak had not heard yet, a name that would require the right place and the right moment and her grandmother's voice saying it aloud.

"She's going to call you," Kimi said.

"I know." Nora set down the cup. "I'll be here."

Kimi closed the voice memo app. She opened the archive folder on her laptop and created a new file: *Nora_Moyaone_Names_Recording_2024-11-07.m4a.* She plugged in the cable and began the transfer.

While the file moved she looked at Nora, who was looking out the window again, at the bare yard and the gray sky and the stakes standing in the cold ground where the tomatoes had been.

"Nora."

"Mm."

"Thank you."

Nora did not turn from the window. But something in the line of her shoulders changed — a small settling, like a weight set down carefully rather than dropped.

"Go finish it," she said. "While it's still morning."

The drive back to College Park took forty minutes on empty roads. Kimi kept the radio off. The archive folder sat open on the passenger seat — not the laptop, just the printed index she'd made three days ago, forty-seven items with handwritten notes in the margins, the last line still blank. She had filled it in before leaving Nora's kitchen, in ballpoint, in her own handwriting: *Nora_Moyaone_Names_Recording_2024-11-07.m4a — names from Yanu forward; bottom name withheld, to be given in person.* Forty-eight items. The blank line was gone, but the gap was marked.

She got home at half past nine. The apartment was cold — she'd left a window cracked when she went out that morning. She closed it, turned on the kitchen light, and sat down at the table with her laptop and the cold dregs of a coffee she didn't bother to reheat.

The archive page was already built. She'd spent two days on the structure: a clean header in plain sans-serif type, no decorative border, no stock image of a mound or a forest or anything that would make it look like a heritage tourism site. Just the title — *Piscataway Presence: The Documentary Record of Site 18CH-447, Charles County, Maryland* — and below it, in smaller text, the date and her name and the words *This archive contains public records, legal documents, oral testimony, and archaeological evidence relating to the Piscataway people's presence on this land. All documents are available for download. All materials may be shared for educational, journalistic, or legal purposes with attribution.*

She had written that paragraph on Sunday. She read it now for the fourth time. It was right.

Below the paragraph, the documents ran in chronological order, oldest to newest. She had organized them into four sections with plain headings — no boldface, no color, nothing that would look like advocacy design to someone who came in hostile. Just dates and descriptions.

**The Land Record** — the 1687 Calvert patent, scanned at 600 dpi from the county archives, the chain lines of the hand-laid rag paper visible in the high-resolution version, the iron-gall ink browned to the color of old tea. She had labeled it: *Patent granted to Thomas Calvert, 1687. Boundary description references Piscataway burial ground at high ground adjacent to Mattawoman Creek. Original held at Maryland State Archives.* A download link. A note that the document was a public record obtained through a Public Records Request filed on October 3, 2024.

**The Petition** — the 1697 document, the one she had read at the press conference three weeks ago, the one that had started all of this in its current form, though it had been moving underground for three hundred years before she touched it. The scan showed the deckled edge of the rag paper on the left side, a fold line running diagonally across the lower third, and at the bottom, where a signature would be, the mark — a small pressed shape, neither letter nor symbol, with Thomas Ware's clerk notation beside it in a different hand: *her mark.* She had captioned it: *Piscataway petition to the colonial court, 1697. Filed by Saya of the Piscataway. Entered into the court record by clerk Thomas Ware during the winter session. Original held at Maryland State Archives.* She had not written anything about what the petition said. The document said it itself.

**The Affidavit** — Nora's. Notarized in Charles County in 1962, sixty-two years old, the jurat at the bottom in the statutory language of the period: *Sworn to and subscribed before me this fourteenth day of March, nineteen sixty-two.* The notary's seal showed as a faint circular impression in the scan, slightly off-center but sharp. Eleven pages of plain declarative sentences. *I have known the location of this mound since I was a child. My grandmother told me its name and the names of those buried in it. I have held this knowledge in trust for the Piscataway people.* She had labeled it: *Affidavit of Nora Moyaone, notarized Charles County, Maryland, 1962. Original held by Nora Moyaone; copy filed with the Office of Federal Acknowledgment.*

**The Field Report** — Tayak's. Forty-three pages on university letterhead, the language careful and qualified throughout, every inference separated from every observation by the ruled-line convention Tayak had learned from Cecile Marant and never stopped using. The copper pendant in stratum five. The cord-marked ceramic in the east transect. The radiocarbon dates. The stratigraphic sequence running from the colonial period down to approximately 100 BC without interruption. Kimi had labeled it: *Archaeological Field Assessment, Site 18CH-447, Dr. Tayak Moyaone, University of Maryland. Published with the express permission of the author.*

**The Artifact Photographs** — twelve images. The pendant, face-up on the conservation suite's white paper, the verdigris along one edge catching the light. The pipe bowl. The shell gorget, both of them, photographed side by side so the difference in age was visible in the patina. The ceramic sherd, the cord-marking clear in raking light. Each image captioned with the stratum, the date of recovery, and the field log reference number.

**The Land Grant Chain** — a single-page PDF she had built herself from the title abstract, the Registry of Deeds references running from the 1687 Calvert patent through every transfer to Harold Slade's purchase in 1922. She had labeled it: *Chain of title, Site 18CH-447 and adjacent parcels, 1687–2024. Compiled from Maryland Registry of Deeds records. Sources cited in footnotes.*

And then, last, the video.

She had uploaded it to YouTube an hour ago, unlisted, while she was still at Nora's kitchen table waiting for the file transfer to finish. She had added captions — hand-corrected, every name spelled correctly, the pause before the second syllable of the oldest names rendered in the transcript as an em dash because there was no other way to show it in text. She had written a content note at the start: *This video contains the names of deceased individuals, spoken as part of an oral kinship chain. The names are shared with the permission of the keeper of the chain. Note: the chain begins with Yanu. The name before Yanu will not be spoken on a recording; it will be given in person.* She had added a downloadable transcript as a PDF. The video was four minutes and eleven seconds long. The thumbnail was a still frame of Nora's hands on the table, both of them, holding the coffee cup, the kitchen light warm behind her.

She embedded it at the bottom of the page. Below the embed she wrote: *Nora Moyaone, age 82, speaks the names of the Piscataway ancestors buried at Site 18CH-447, in the order they were given to her. The chain begins with Yanu and extends to the present. The name before Yanu is held for in-person transmission. Recorded November 7, 2024.*

She read the whole page from the top. It took four minutes. She found one broken link — the affidavit PDF, wrong file path — and fixed it. She checked the mobile view. She checked that every download link opened. She checked that the video played.

Then she sat with her hands in her lap and looked at the publish button.

It was a small thing, visually. A blue rectangle in the upper right corner of the screen. She had published things before — the press conference announcement, the initial post about the designation letter, the thread about NAGPRA that had gotten three thousand shares and a call from Dana Osei. She knew what the button did. She knew what happened after.

She sat with it for ninety seconds.

She thought about Saya. Not the historical abstraction of her, not the name in the petition caption, but the image she had carried since she first read Nora's affidavit eight months ago: a woman standing in a colonial assembly room with ink drying on her palm, having pressed her hand to a document that the men in that room would file and forget, knowing they would file and forget it, doing it anyway. Pressing her hand to the paper as if the paper were the ground itself. As if the act of pressing were the thing that mattered, not the outcome.

*Her mark.*

Kimi clicked publish.

The page went live at 10:23 AM. She could see it now in a new tab — the header, the explanatory paragraph, the documents in their chronological order, Nora's hands at the bottom of the page.

She closed the laptop.

She did not check the share count. She did not open her phone. She turned off the kitchen light and went to her room and lay down on top of the covers in her clothes and looked at the ceiling, which was the same ceiling it had always been, water stain in the northeast corner shaped vaguely like a mitten, the sound of a car passing on the street below, then nothing.

She did not sleep.

She lay there and thought about the forty-eight files. She thought about the blank line on the printed index, now filled in ballpoint — but with the gap marked, the gap visible, the gap honest. She thought about Tayak somewhere in the early afternoon — at the site, maybe, or in the car — not yet knowing that her name was the last one in the chain, said in her grandmother's voice, in a kitchen on Billingsley Road, in a four-minute-and-eleven-second recording that was now moving through servers she would never see, toward people she would never meet, carrying the names forward the way the names had always moved: from one person to the next, without knowing where they would land.

She thought about Nora's jaw when she said the name of the woman who had left. The steadiness that cost something.

She thought about the name before Yanu. The one not on the recording. The one Tayak had not heard yet. The one that required the right place and the right moment and her grandmother's voice saying it aloud, not into a microphone but into the air of a room where Tayak was standing.

Tayak would have to go to Billingsley Road to hear it. Nora would be there.

Outside, the sky was pale and still. A bird started somewhere, stopped, started again.

Kimi lay still and listened to it.

---

By morning, the archive had been shared two hundred thousand times.

The alarm on her phone said 6:04 AM. She had not set it. She had not slept.

She sat up and pulled the laptop from the nightstand and opened it on the covers. The screen was too bright. She squinted at it and waited for her eyes to adjust.

The archive page loaded. The share counter at the bottom — she had embedded a simple tracker, the kind that pulled from social aggregators every fifteen minutes — read 204,318. She stared at the number. It did not look real. Numbers that size belonged to other things: stadium capacities, election margins, the population of a mid-sized city. She refreshed the page. 204,391.

She opened her phone.

Forty-seven notifications. She did not read them in order. She scrolled the preview pane the way you scan a crowd for a face you know, looking for the shape of something familiar. Dana Osei at the Post: *Kimi — I need fifteen minutes. Today if possible. This is significant.* Three journalists she did not recognize, two from outlets she had never heard of, one from a wire service with a name that made her breath go shallow. A professor at the University of Maryland whose name she recognized from the footnotes of Tayak's field assessment. A tribal council member she had met once at a hearing in La Plata. Seventeen messages from people she did not know at all — some careful and formal, some not, some in languages she could not read, some with photographs attached that she did not open yet.

Then a number she did not recognize. Area code 410.

The preview showed the beginning of a voicemail transcription, auto-generated and imperfect: *This message is from the Office of the Governor. We are calling regarding Site 18CH-447 and the colonial landmark desig—*

She stopped reading.

She sat on the edge of the bed with the phone in both hands and looked at the transcription preview the way you look at a word in a foreign language that you almost know — almost, but not quite, not yet, not without hearing it spoken aloud.

The voicemail was forty-three seconds long. She could see that in the timestamp. Forty-three seconds was enough for a statement. Forty-three seconds was enough to say something had changed.

She did not play it.

She called Tayak.

The phone rang once. Twice. Three times. On the fourth ring Kimi pulled the phone slightly away from her ear, already composing the voicemail she would leave, already thinking about what she could say in twenty seconds that would carry the weight of —

"Yeah." Tayak's voice. Awake. Not just-woken-up awake — already-awake, had-been-awake awake, the voice of someone who had been waiting for something to happen and had been waiting for several hours.

Kimi said: "It's out."

A pause. Not long. The length of a breath.

"How far?"

"Two hundred thousand shares. As of six this morning." She looked at the laptop. "More now, probably."

Another pause. She heard something in the background — wind, or a car, or the particular quality of outdoor silence that is not silence at all. Tayak was at the site. Of course she was at the site.

"The Governor's office called," Kimi said. "Voicemail. Four-ten area code. I haven't played it yet."

This pause was different. Longer.

"Play it," Tayak said.

"I know. I will." Kimi looked at the phone's screen, at the notification sitting there with its forty-three-second timestamp. "I wanted to tell you first."

"Kimi."

"I know." She switched to speaker, set the phone on the nightstand, and pressed play.

The voice was a woman's, measured and professional, the kind of voice trained to deliver information without inflection in either direction. *This message is for Kimi Moyaone from the Office of the Governor of Maryland. The Governor's office has become aware of materials published regarding Site 18CH-447 in Charles County. It is our understanding that the colonial landmark designation for this site is currently under review, and the Governor's office will be seeking additional stakeholder input before any further action is taken. We have no further comment at this time. If you have questions, you may contact the Communications Director's office at —*

Kimi stopped the playback.

Through the phone she could hear Tayak breathing.

"Under review," Kimi said.

"Yes."

"That's not a reversal."

"No," Tayak said. "It's not."

Kimi stood up from the bed and walked to the window. The street below was empty. The sky above the building across the street had gone from orange-gray to a pale, watery blue, the kind of blue that meant the sun was coming but had not arrived. A delivery truck was parked at the corner with its hazards on, the yellow light pulsing in a slow, indifferent rhythm.

"The hearing is still scheduled," she said.

"Yes."

"So this is — what. Pressure. They're feeling the pressure."

"It means someone in that office watched the share count this morning," Tayak said, "and made a calculation. It doesn't mean they've decided anything."

Kimi pressed her forehead against the cold glass. The delivery truck's hazards kept pulsing. Yellow, dark, yellow, dark.

"Dana Osei messaged me," she said. "The Post. He wants fifteen minutes."

"I know. He called me too." A pause. "Talk to him."

"Tayak —"

"Talk to him. You built the archive. You know what's in it. Talk to him the same way you'd explain it to anyone who needed to understand it fast." Another pause, shorter. "You already did that once. At the press conference."

Kimi thought about the podium. The petition in her hands. Her hands, which had been steady.

"The hearing is in —" she started.

"Eight days," Tayak said. "I know."

The sky was getting lighter. The delivery truck pulled away from the corner, hazards still blinking, and turned south and was gone. The street was empty again. In the silence she could hear the bird from earlier — or a different bird, she couldn't tell — starting up again somewhere beyond the building's roofline, a single repeated note, patient and unhurried, as if it had all the time in the world and knew it.

"The Governor's office called you 'stakeholder,'" Kimi said.

A beat. Then Tayak made a sound that was almost a laugh. Almost.

"Yes," she said. "They did."

Kimi looked at the laptop on the nightstand. The archive page was still open, the share counter updating in its fifteen-minute cycle, the number climbing in increments she could not see in real time but knew were happening — somewhere, in servers she would never see, the names were moving. Nora's voice was moving. The petition with its sepia ink and its deckled edges and its *her mark* notation was moving through the hands of two hundred thousand people she would never meet, toward people those people knew, toward journalists and lawyers and council members and, apparently, toward someone in the Governor's office who had listened to enough of it by six in the morning to pick up a phone.

*Under review* was not a reversal. She knew that. Tayak knew that. Eight days was not a long time.

But Saya had pressed her hand to a document in a room full of men who would file it and forget it, and the document had survived three hundred and twenty-seven years, and it was on the archive page right now, and two hundred thousand people had seen it before sunrise.

"I'll call Dana Osei," Kimi said.

"Good," Tayak said.

"And then I'm going to the site."

A pause. Not disagreement — something else. Something that sounded, through the phone, like Tayak turning to look at something. The mound, maybe. The trench. The place where the ground kept what it kept.

"All right," Tayak said. "Come to the site."
