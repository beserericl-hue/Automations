The canoe frame sat on two sawhorses in the middle of the shed, ribs sprung outward like the chest of something breathing. Okwi moved along its length with a drawknife, peeling thin curls of cedar that fell to the dirt floor and caught the light from the single open shutter. The shed smelled of green wood and the tallow he rubbed into his tools at the end of each day. It was a smell Saya had known since she was a girl watching her father-in-law work in this same building, on these same sawhorses, with the same economy of motion that made the labor look like rest.

She spread the document on the workbench along the wall.

Two sheets of laid paper, dense with a sloping clerk's hand, the ink faded from black to the color of old tea. A wax seal at the bottom had cracked clean through, and the two halves were held together by nothing but the paper they had been pressed into. She had paid the clerk at Saint Mary's City three shillings for this copy, three shillings borrowed from her cousin without explanation, and she had carried it home wrapped in oilskin inside her basket, under the dried corn and the bundle of sedge grass, as if it were something that could be taken from her.

"Come and look," she said.

Okwi did not come and look. He ran the drawknife along a rib, checked the curve with his eye, ran it again.

"Okwi."

"I hear you."

"Then come."

He set the drawknife on the sawhorse and came to the bench. He was taller than the doorframe and had learned years ago to move through the world at a slight forward tilt, as if always about to step into a wind. He looked at the document without touching it.

"Read it," Saya said.

"You read it."

She put her finger on the line she had found, the line she had read so many times in the three days since the clerk handed her the copy that she could have spoken it in her sleep. "Here. *The said Piscataway people being the ancient possessors of the tract in question, their prior occupation of the same being a matter of record and of common knowledge in this county, the Court finds that no English patent may be held to extinguish a claim founded upon such occupation without the said people's consent or an act of lawful purchase.*"

She looked up. Okwi was watching her, not the page.

"Ancient possessors," she said. "Prior occupation. Their own court wrote those words. Their own judge. If they wrote it once, we can make them write it again."

Okwi was quiet for a moment. A curl of cedar lay on the bench beside the document, and he picked it up and turned it between his fingers.

"Who signed it?" he said.

"Judge Thomas Notley. He sat the Provincial Court."

Okwi dropped the cedar curl. "Notley."

"You know him?"

"He has been dead twenty years." He went back to the canoe. "Maybe longer."

Saya kept her eyes on the document. "The ruling still stands."

"His rulings do not stand." Okwi picked up the drawknife. "Every man he ruled for, the next court ruled against. I heard my father say it. Notley had ideas about the Indians that his bench did not share, and when he died they spent two years undoing what he had done." He drew the knife along the rib, slow and even. "Three shillings?"

"That is not the point."

"Three shillings is always the point."

"The language is still in their record," Saya said. "Even if they reversed his judgments, the words are still there. A clerk still copied them. They are still in the book." She pressed her palm flat on the paper, feeling the slight roughness of the grit the Saint Mary's clerk had shaken over the ink to dry it, fine sand still caught in the grain of the page. "I can cite this in the petition. I can say their own court acknowledged us. That we were here first and their law knew it."

Okwi said nothing. The drawknife moved.

"Will you at least read it?" Saya said.

"I read it."

"You looked at it."

"I read enough." He stopped and turned to face her. His hands were pale with cedar dust to the wrist. "Saya. A dead judge's reversed ruling, copied out by a clerk who charged you three shillings and forgot your name before you reached the door. You are going to build a petition on that."

"I am going to build a petition on what their own law says we are. Ancient possessors. It is their word, not mine. If they want to argue against us, they have to argue against themselves."

He looked at her for a long moment. Outside, a crow called twice and went quiet.

"Who will hear the petition?" he said.

"The Provincial Court. Or the Governor's Council, if the court will not take it."

"And who sits the Provincial Court now?"

She knew he knew the answer. "Blakiston's men."

"Blakiston's men," he said, and turned back to the canoe.

The cedar curls at her feet were the color of the inside of a shell, pale and almost pink, still holding the faint warmth of the living wood. Saya rolled the document carefully and wrapped it in the oilskin. She worked slowly, smoothing each fold.

"Then we will have said it," she said. "Even if they refuse us. It will be in their record. They will have to write down that we came, and what we claimed, and that they turned us away."

Okwi said nothing for so long that she thought he had finished with the conversation. Then he spoke without looking up from his work.

"Their record," he said. "Not ours."

Saya tucked the oilskin parcel under her arm. The shed was cold. The Maunder cold had been pressing down on the winters here for as long as she could remember, arriving earlier each year and staying later, and the oldest people said it was not the cold they had known as children. She could see her breath.

"Then we keep both," she said.

Okwi did not answer. But his hands slowed on the cedar, just for a moment, before he found his rhythm again.

The cold had not broken by morning. Saya sat at the table with the petition draft in front of her, the ink she had mixed the night before gone thick in the cold. She held the small clay pot between her palms to warm it and watched the door.

Okwi came in from the shed with cedar dust on his shirt and set his adze against the wall. He looked at the draft. He did not sit.

"The reversal," he said. "You thought about it."

"I have thought about nothing else."

"And?"

"It does not matter." She set the ink pot down and picked up the quill. "The reversal proves the ruling existed. You cannot reverse a thing that was never written. If they reversed Notley, they had to name what they were reversing, his language, his finding, his words about ancient possessors. Which means those words appear twice in their record now. Once when he wrote them and once when they tried to undo them."

Okwi pulled out the bench across from her and sat. That alone told her something. He was not dismissing this yet.

"They appear twice as something the court rejected," he said.

"They appear twice as something the court had to reckon with. That is different."

He was quiet. Outside, the river was audible, ice cracking somewhere upstream, a sound like a distant axe.

"Say I grant you that," he said. "Say the language survives the reversal. Say a judge reads your petition and finds the Notley ruling and agrees the words mean what you say they mean." He put his hands flat on the table. His knuckles were cracked from the cold, the skin rough as bark. "Who carries the petition to that judge?"

"I will."

"You will walk into the Provincial Court."

"I have been in the Provincial Court."

"You have stood in the yard of the Provincial Court while your cousin argued a trading dispute and you watched through the door." He was not unkind about it. He said it the way he said most things, level, like a plank he was checking for true. "Who carries it inside? Who puts it in front of a clerk and says, this petition is to be heard?"

Saya set down the quill. "Thomas Ware."

Okwi said nothing.

"He clerks for Blakiston's secretary. He has been at the court three years. He knows the procedure, how a petition is entered, which docket it goes on, who has to sign the order for it to be heard."

"I know who Thomas Ware is."

"Then you know he owes my cousin two months on a tobacco debt. Twelve pounds of leaf, not yet settled. My cousin will forgive the debt."

"For what."

"For Ware entering the petition and marking it received."

Okwi stood. He went to the fire and stood with his back to her, looking into it. The wood was green. She could hear the sap hissing in the grain.

"A debt paid in trade goods is not the same as a man who will risk his position," he said.

"I know the difference."

He turned. "Do you."

"He is not being asked to argue for us. He is being asked to write a date in a book and put a document in a stack. That is his ordinary work. We are asking him to do his ordinary work."

"His ordinary work does not include entering petitions from Indian women."

The word landed the way it always landed. Not a blow, just a fact stated plainly, which was in some ways worse. Saya kept her voice even.

"His ordinary work includes entering petitions from anyone who presents them in proper form and pays the filing fee. I have the fee. The petition will be in proper form. He will not have to do anything he has not done a hundred times."

"Except do it for you."

"Except do it for me." She picked up the quill again. "Which is why the debt matters. Not because twelve pounds of leaf buys his courage. Because it buys his attention long enough to see that this is not dangerous for him. That we are not asking him to take a side. We are asking him to do his job."

Okwi came back to the table. He did not sit this time. He stood over the draft and read it, read it properly, she could see his eyes moving, and then he looked up.

"You have thought about this man," he said.

"I have thought about this man for four months."

"What is he afraid of?"

She had not expected him to ask that. She set the quill down.

"His position," she said. "He came from Kent three years ago with a letter of introduction and nothing else. He has made himself useful to Blakiston's secretary and he does not want to be un-useful. He is afraid of being sent back."

"So he will not enter a petition that makes Blakiston's secretary uncomfortable."

"He will enter a petition that arrives in proper form, with a fee, from a party with legal standing, before Blakiston's secretary knows it exists." She held his gaze. "The court opens its winter session in six weeks. The secretary will be in Annapolis for two of those weeks. Ware will be managing the office alone."

Okwi looked at her for a long moment. The fire spat. A knot in the green wood gave way with a sound like a small bone breaking.

"You are not sure of him," he said.

"I am not sure of anyone."

"That is not what I said."

She looked down at the draft. The ink had warmed enough. She could see the sheen of it where she had tested the quill on the corner of the page. The letters she had written the night before were cramped, too close together, the way her hand always went when she was tired.

"I believe he will do it," she said. "I have watched him for four months. I know what he wants. I know what he fears. I believe the debt and the timing and the form of the petition together are enough."

"Believe," Okwi said.

"Yes."

"Not know."

She looked up at him. "No. Not know." She kept her voice steady. "I do not know. I believe. Those are different things and I know the difference."

He was quiet for a moment. Then he pulled out the bench and sat down across from her again, and she understood that this was not agreement. It was something she did not have a clean word for. He was staying in the problem with her. He did not leave the problem.

"If he refuses," Okwi said. "Or if he takes the fee and does nothing. Or if Blakiston's secretary comes back early."

"Then I find another way in."

"What other way."

"I do not know yet." She picked up the quill. "But I will know it when I need to." She looked at the draft. "Help me with this line. I want to say we have maintained this ground without interruption since before any English patent was issued, but I do not know the right word. The word they use in the court books for that kind of continuous holding."

Okwi was silent long enough that she thought he would refuse. Then he said, "Immemorial."

She looked up.

"Time immemorial," he said. "I heard it in the trading dispute. The English use it when they want to say a thing has always been so and no one can remember it being otherwise." He said it without expression. "It is their word for what we already know."

She wrote it. *From time immemorial.* The ink went down dark and clean. She blew on it gently, and a little of the warmth from her breath hung in the cold air between them before it was gone.

The fire had burned low enough that Saya could see the individual coals, orange at the center, grey at the rim, the whole bed breathing in small pulses when the wind found the smoke hole. She had written *from time immemorial* and now she was reading back what she had, and the draft was nearly there. Nearly.

Okwi had not moved. He sat with his forearms on the table, watching her read. She could feel that watching the way she always could, not as pressure but as presence. He was in the problem with her. He was also in something else, something he had not yet said.

She set the page down.

"There is one more thing," she said. "The 1666 judgment."

He waited.

"Blakiston's predecessor, the governor before Copley, there was a land dispute on the Patuxent, north of here. A Mattapooni family had been farming a tract for two generations. An English planter claimed the patent superseded their right. The court found that the patent could not extinguish what it called the ancient possessors' prior claim without formal purchase or treaty." She kept her voice flat, the way she did when she was most certain of something. "I have a copy. The clerk at St. Mary's made it for me. It cost me eight shillings and three days waiting on a bench outside his door."

Okwi looked at her. "You went to St. Mary's."

"In September. Before the weather turned."

"You did not tell me."

"I did not know yet if it was useful."

He absorbed this without expression. She had learned, in nine years of marriage, that his silences were not all the same. This one was not anger. It was something more like recalibration, the way he looked at a timber he had measured twice and found a third dimension he had not accounted for.

"Show me," he said.

She reached under the oilcloth at the edge of the table and brought out the folded sheet. It was laid paper, heavy, the clerk's hand a sloping italic with flourished capitals that made the letters look more confident than the content warranted. The ink had gone brown at the edges where it had sat against the fold. She spread it flat and turned it so he could read.

He read slowly. She watched his eyes move down the page, the preamble with its *whereas* and *be it known*, the recitation of the Mattapooni family's occupation, the planter's claim, the court's finding. The wax seal at the bottom had cracked in transit and one edge had lifted, but the clerk's attestation was clear: *Extracted from the Records of the Provincial Court, this 14th day of November, 1693. Thomas Ware, Clerk.*

"Thomas Ware," Okwi said.

"The same man."

He looked up from the page. "He copied this for you."

"He did not know what he was copying. I asked for the Patuxent land record from 1666. I told him I was acting on behalf of a tobacco merchant in a boundary dispute." She paused. "Which was not entirely false. Nikkis Askew has a boundary question on his eastern line."

Okwi looked at her for a moment. Then he said, "Nikkis Askew knows you used his name."

"Nikkis Askew owed me a favor."

He almost smiled. She could see it at the corner of his mouth, the thing that was not quite a smile but was the shape one made before it. He looked back at the document.

"*Ancient possessors*," he read. "That is what they called them."

"Yes."

"And the court honored it."

"The court found it was a fact that had to be addressed before the patent could be enforced. The planter had to negotiate. He paid for the release of the claim." She tapped the page. "It is not a victory. It is a mechanism. A door in the wall. It says the prior claim exists and must be answered."

Okwi was quiet. He read the passage again. She could see him doing it, his lips just barely moving on the Latin phrases the clerk had left untranslated. Then he folded the document along its original creases and set it on top of the draft.

"So you cite this," he said. "In the petition."

"In the petition. We recite our occupation. We name the mound, the planting grounds, the fishing rights on the creek. We invoke the 1666 finding as precedent, that prior occupation is a claim the court must address. We ask for formal recognition of our right to remain."

"And the court will read it."

"The court will have it in its record. Whether they read it is another matter."

He looked at her. Something shifted in his face, not the almost-smile this time. Something quieter and more careful.

"Then we will have said it," she said. "It will be in their record."

Okwi was still for a moment. Outside the wind moved through the bare oaks and the house timbers creaked once, a long low note, and then were quiet.

"Their record," he said, "is not our record."

She had expected argument. She had not expected the flatness of it. Not dismissal, not contempt. Just a fact stated the way you stated something about weather or water depth, something you had lived inside so long you had stopped needing to be angry about it.

"I know that," she said.

"Your name in that book," he said. "Siosaia, wife of Okwi, Piscataway woman. It is not your name. It is a mark in their ledger. It serves their memory of what happened here, not ours."

"I know."

"Their record says what they need it to say. It said the Mattapooni family were *ancient possessors* when it was useful to have a name for what they were paying for. It will say something else when it is useful to say something else."

"Yes," she said. "I know that too."

She did not reach for the quill. She let him finish.

"We have kept this ground in our memory for longer than their colony has existed," he said. "We know where the mound is. We know what it holds. We know who is in it. We know the names of the people who planted this soil before any Englishman's grandfather was born. That is the record. It does not live in a book in St. Mary's City."

"No," she said. "It does not."

She looked at the draft. The fire pulsed. A coal shifted and a small bright fragment fell through the grate and went dark.

"Their record is the only one their court will accept," she said. "So it is the one I must use."

He did not answer immediately. She could hear him breathing, slow and even, the way he breathed when he was working through a problem in his hands, fitting a joint, testing the flex of a rib before he committed to the shape.

"I am not saying you are wrong," he said.

"I know."

"I am saying that when this fails, "

"If."

"When," he said, not cruelly. "When this fails, the record they keep will say what they choose. It will say the petition was heard and denied, or it will say the petition was irregular and returned, or it will say nothing at all. And our record, what we know, what we have kept, that will still be here. In us. In her." He did not look toward the sleeping room where their daughter lay, but Saya heard the word *her* and felt it in her chest.

"Yes," she said.

"So we are not doing the same thing," he said. "You are putting our claim into their vessel because it is the only vessel they will look at. I understand that. But the vessel is not the claim."

She looked at him across the table, across the draft and the 1666 precedent and the quill and the small clay inkpot that had been her mother's, a smooth brown thing with a chip on the lip that she had never repaired.

"No," she said. "The vessel is not the claim."

She picked up the quill.

"Then help me make the vessel as strong as it can be," she said. "Because if it holds even one season, if it gives us one winter where they do not move against us, then I will have bought time. And time is what I need."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he reached across the table and turned the draft so it faced him, and he read it from the beginning, slowly, and she watched him read, and she waited.

"This word," he said. He put his finger on a line near the middle of the page. "Here. You have written *humbly*."

She looked. *Your petitioner humbly sets forth.*

"Take it out," he said.

She looked at him.

"You are not humble," he said. "You are correct. Those are different things."

She looked at the word. She thought about Thomas Ware and his letter of introduction from Kent and his fear of being un-useful to Blakiston's secretary. She thought about what the word *humbly* would do for him, how it would make the petition smaller, easier to file, easier to ignore.

She crossed it out.

Okwi watched her do it. He did not smile. He went back to reading.

The fire breathed. The ink dried. Outside, the creek was moving under a skim of ice. She could hear it, a faint irregular sound, water finding its way through the places where the cold had not yet closed. The mound was out there in the dark, beyond the tree line, holding what it held. It had been holding it since before her grandmother's grandmother's name was spoken. It would hold it after the petition was filed and answered and whatever happened next had happened.

She did not let herself think about that yet.

She dipped the quill and kept writing.

The fire had burned lower by the time Okwi set the draft down.

He did not say anything at first. He turned the pages back to the beginning and read through them again, more slowly. Saya watched his finger move along the lines, not tracing the words, just resting near them, the way he rested his hand against a plank to feel for warp before he committed the adze.

"Here," he said. He tapped a line near the top of the second page.

She leaned across. *Your petitioner, being of the Piscataway people, ancient possessors of the said land by right of prior occupation and long continuance therein.*

"Ancient possessors," he said.

"It is their language," she said. "From the 1666 entry. The clerk wrote it of the Mattapooni family. I am using it because they used it."

"I know why you used it." He did not move his finger. "I am asking whether you want to."

She looked at the phrase. She had copied it deliberately, had felt something almost like satisfaction when she found it in the transcript, proof that their own court had once acknowledged what everyone already knew, that the land had held people long before any patent was granted. She had thought: their word, their court, their record. She had thought it was a kind of leverage.

"It is accurate," she said.

"It is accurate and it is theirs," he said. "They named the Mattapooni that when it served them to have a name for what they were paying for. Now you are borrowing the name back." He sat back. "I am not saying it is wrong. I am asking if you have thought about what it does."

"It tells the court we have standing."

"It tells the court you are asking them to recognize what they have already written down, which is different from standing. Standing is something you have. Recognition is something they give." He picked up the quill she had set down and turned it in his fingers, not to write, just to hold. The feather was worn, the barbs stripped back near the tip from use. "You are asking them to be consistent."

"Yes."

"Courts are not consistent. They are convenient."

"I know that."

"Then you know the phrase will not save the petition."

"No single phrase will save it," she said. "That is not why I am writing it phrase by phrase."

He set the quill down. He looked at her. The fire gave a low sound, wood settling, and the light shifted across the table so that the draft lay half in shadow, the iron gall ink brown against the pale laid paper, the letters dense and close. She had written a fair hand. Her mother had made her practice on birch bark before she was allowed near paper, and the discipline showed.

"Tell me why you are writing it phrase by phrase," he said. Not a challenge. He wanted to hear her say it.

"Because every phrase is a door," she said. "Some of them will be shut. Some of them may not be. I do not know which until I knock." She looked at the draft. "The 1666 entry is a door. *Ancient possessors* is a door. The list of our plantings on the south field, going back three generations, that is a door. The names of the men who witnessed our boundary with the Calvert patent, that is a door. I am not writing one argument. I am writing ten. If nine of them fail, one may hold."

He was quiet. Outside, a branch cracked, cold working the wood, the way it did in January when the temperature dropped fast after dark. The sound was sharp and then gone.

"And if all ten fail," he said.

"Then we will have said it," she said. "It will be in their record."

He looked at her. Something moved in his face that was not quite disagreement and not quite grief. "Their record is not our record," he said.

"I know."

"You say you know." He leaned forward, both forearms on the table, his hands flat beside the draft. His hands were rough from the adze and the drawknife, the skin thickened at the base of each finger, a white scar running from his left thumb to his wrist where a plank had slipped three winters ago. "But you are acting as though putting our claim into their book is the same as keeping it. It is not. What they write down, they can also cross out. What they record, they can also lose. They can say the pages were damaged. They can say the entry was irregular. They can say the petition was returned without action and file it somewhere it will not be found." He paused. "You have seen them do this."

She had. She thought of the Choptico petition, seven years ago, the one her father had helped draft, the one that had gone to the secretary's office in St. Mary's City and come back with a notation she had never fully understood, *referred to the governor's consideration*, and had not been heard of since. She thought of the Mattapooni deed, which existed in the record but had not stopped the enclosures. She thought of all the doors that had been built to look like doors and were in fact walls.

"Yes," she said. "I have seen them do this."

"Then you know their record is not a vessel that holds. It is a vessel they carry. They take it where they go. They tip it when they choose."

She did not answer immediately. She looked at the draft, at the careful phrases, the borrowed language, the names of witnesses she had already spoken to, the list of plantings, the reference to the 1666 entry with its clerk's notation and the fee she had paid to have it copied. She had spent two weeks on this. She had walked to St. Mary's City in November cold and stood in the courthouse yard while a clerk with ink-stained fingers searched through three leather-bound order books before he found the entry, and she had paid him four pence for the transcript and walked home with it folded inside her shirt against her skin because she did not trust the saddlebag in the rain.

"I know what their record is," she said. "I am using it because it is the only one their court will look at. That does not mean it is the only one that matters."

He was quiet for a moment. Then he said: "I will help you write it."

"You already are."

"I mean I will help you write it so that it is also ours," he said. "So that when their record fails, ours remains."

She looked at him. "What do you mean?"

He did not answer straight away. He looked at the draft, then at the fire, then at his hands. He picked up the quill again and this time he held it the way he held a tool he was about to use, not turning it, not considering it, just holding it ready.

"I mean that the petition is one thing," he said. "But the petition is also a story. It says where we are. It says how long we have been here. It says the names of the people who were here before us and the names of the people who will come after. It says what we planted and where we planted it and why the south field has been ours since before any Englishman's grandfather set foot on this river." He set the quill down on the table between them. "That story should not only live in their book."

She did not speak.

"If we write it into their record," he said, "we should also speak it. We should tell it to her." Again he did not look toward the sleeping room, but the word *her* was the same word it had been before, their daughter, four years old, asleep on the other side of the wall. "We should tell it the way our mothers told it to us. So that when the book is lost or crossed out or filed where no one will find it, the story is still here. In a person. In a voice."

Saya looked at him for a long time. The fire had burned to coals, giving off a steady red heat, and the room was dim enough that she could see the draft only by leaning close. The ink had dried to brown. The words were small and dense and careful, and they were, she thought, as strong as she could make them. They might hold. They might not.

"You are saying the petition is not enough," she said.

"I am saying the petition is one kind of keeping," he said. "And we should also do the other kind."

She thought about that. She thought about her mother's voice, the particular quality it had when she was telling something that mattered, lower than her speaking voice, slower, the way water moves when it is deep. She thought about the names her mother had given her, the ones that were not in any record, the ones that lived only in the telling. She thought about the mound beyond the tree line, out in the cold dark, holding what it held without any clerk's notation, without any seal or signature, without any fee paid for copying.

"Their record is not our record," she said.

"No," he said. "But ours is older. And it does not depend on them to keep it."

She reached for the quill. She did not answer him, not because she disagreed, but because she did not yet know what she was agreeing to. Something was opening in her, slow and large, the way a river opens in spring when the ice goes out, not all at once but in pieces, the current finding the places where the cold has not held.

She dipped the quill. She looked at the draft.

"Tell me the line again," she said. "The one about the south field."

He told her. She wrote it. He watched her write it, and when she had finished he read it back to her, and she changed one word, and he said nothing, and she wrote the word again, and it was right.

They worked until the coals went grey. The creek moved under its ice outside, finding its way through. The mound held what it held. And in the sleeping room, their daughter breathed in the dark, unknowing, carrying already in the small fact of her existence the other kind of record, the one that did not live in any book in St. Mary's City, the one that Saya was only beginning to understand she had been keeping all along.

The fire was coals now, no more than a low red breathing in the hearth. Saya felt the cold coming through the wall at her back the way it always did after midnight, not through any gap she had ever found, just through the logs themselves, through the wood and the clay chinking, as if the cold had patience and the wall did not.

She set down the quill.

The draft lay before her, six pages of it, the ink dried to the color of old bark. She had changed the line about the south field twice and now it was right. She knew it was right the way she knew when a basket was finished, not because she had checked it against anything, but because the thing had closed. It held.

"I want to look at her," she said.

Okwi nodded without looking up. He had been reading the last page again, his finger tracing the lines without touching them, the way he checked a hull plank for true, not pressing, just feeling for the deviation.

She rose and went to the sleeping room.

Her daughter lay on her side, one fist curled under her chin, her breath so even and small that Saya stood still for a moment and listened to it the way she listened to the creek. The child had kicked her blanket half off. Saya drew it back up over her shoulder and stood another moment in the dark, her hand resting on the blanket's edge.

*It says the names of the people who were here before us and the names of the people who will come after.*

She had written that line. It was on the fourth page, in the third paragraph of the recitation of prior occupation. She had written it and it was true and it was in the draft and it would go into the petition and the petition would go to the court and the court would do with it what the court would do.

And her daughter would not know any of that unless someone told her.

She stood in the dark a moment longer. Then she went back.

Okwi was not at the table.

The draft was still there, weighted at its corners by the ink jar and the candle stub and two small river stones he had set there at some point without her noticing. The quill lay across the last page. The fire was lower still.

He was at his workbench.

She had not heard him move. He had the tallow lamp from the corner, the small one he used for close work, and it was burning on the bench beside him, throwing a yellow circle over his hands and the surface of the wood. His back was to her. He was bent forward in the way he bent when he was fitting something, shoulders dropped, head low, his whole attention gone into his hands.

She thought: he has gone back to the canoe.

There was always something with the canoe. A seam to check, a lashing to test, some small correction that had been waiting in the back of his mind while they argued and worked and argued again. He built canoes the way other men breathed, without choosing to, without stopping.

She crossed to the fire and crouched before it, feeding the last two pieces of wood from the stack beside the hearth. The wood caught slowly, the bark darkening before the flame took hold. She held her hands out to it.

Behind her she heard the soft sound of something being unfolded. Then the sound of it being measured, the particular silence of a man holding something flat against something else and reading the difference with his fingers. She had watched him do it a hundred times with planks, with bark, with the long strips of inner wood he bent for ribs.

She did not turn around.

She thought about the petition. She thought about the clerk at St. Mary's City with his brown-stained fingers and his expression of careful neutrality, the way he had not looked at her when she paid him the four pence, the way the transcript had felt against her skin on the ride home, stiff and slightly damp, the laid paper already softening at the fold. She thought about the 1666 entry, the words *ancient possessors* in the clerk's sloping hand, the small sand-grit still caught in the binding where some earlier clerk had blotted the ink and not shaken the page clean.

*Ancient possessors.* Their word for it. She had used it in the draft, had borrowed it back from them, turned it into an argument, pressed it into the shape of a claim.

It might hold.

She had written it as strongly as she knew how to write anything. She had used their language and their precedent and their form. She had signed it with her name and she would put her mark beside it and she would carry it to the court and she would stand in whatever room they made her stand in and she would say: we were here first, and here is your own record saying so, and now what do you say.

And they would say whatever they said.

She fed the last piece of wood into the fire and watched the flame find it.

Behind her, Okwi was still at the bench. The sound now was different. Not measuring. Something slower. The sound of something being folded with care, the particular deliberateness of a man folding something he means to keep flat.

She turned.

He had a piece of oilskin on the bench. Not large, perhaps the size of two pages laid side by side. He had it spread flat under the lamp, and he was folding one edge over with the same attention he gave to the first fold of a hull seam, pressing the crease with his thumbnail, checking it, pressing again. The oilskin was from his stores, rendered fat worked into the skin until it was nearly translucent, supple and close-grained, the same material he used to seal the seams of the canoes against the river. She had seen him cut pieces of it a hundred times. She had never seen him fold one like this.

He did not look up.

She watched him set the folded piece beside the lamp and take the draft from the table, her six pages, her careful lines, her borrowed precedent and her true names, and hold it against the oilskin. He was measuring. Not reading. Measuring.

The draft was a little longer than the oilskin. He set it down, took his knife from the bench, and cut a strip from the edge of the remaining hide. Added it to the piece. Held the draft against it again.

Now it fit.

He set the draft back on the table. He folded the oilskin again, smaller this time, and set it on the bench beside the lamp, beneath the edge of the workbench where the shadows were deepest. He set a block of wood over it, not hiding it exactly, just setting it aside. Out of the way. Waiting.

Then he straightened and picked up the draft again and began to read.

Saya did not move.

She stood by the fire with the warmth at her back and looked at her husband bent over her words in the lamplight, and she thought: he is reading the petition. Then she thought: he is not reading the petition. He was reading it the way he read a plank he was about to shape, not for what it said but for what it would need to be.

She thought about the oilskin folded under the bench.

She thought about the way he had measured it against the draft.

She opened her mouth to ask him.

He looked up then, and his face in the lamplight was the face she knew, the particular stillness he had when he had decided something and was not yet ready to say it, the way the decision sat in him like a stone in still water, settled, not moving.

"You should sleep," he said. "We finish the last page tomorrow."

She looked at him. She looked at the bench, at the block of wood sitting at the edge of the shadow.

"What are you building?" she said.

He looked back at the draft.

"Nothing yet," he said.

She held his gaze for a moment. He did not look away, but he did not offer her anything more, and she knew that look. She knew it the way she knew the particular quiet before he told her something she was not going to like, the way she knew the sound of a seam that was not yet finished but would be.

She let it go. She did not know why she let it go. Perhaps because the fire was warm and the draft was done and she was tired in the way she was only tired when something important had been finished, a deep tiredness that was not quite grief and not quite relief but something between them. Perhaps because she trusted him with the things he was not yet ready to say.

She went back to the sleeping room. She lay down beside her daughter and pulled the blanket over them both and listened to the small even sound of the child's breath.

In the other room, the lamp stayed lit.

She could hear him at the bench. Not the sound of canoe work, not the rasp of the drawknife or the tap of the mallet. Just the quiet sound of something being handled carefully, being considered, being set in order. The sound of a man preparing for something he had not named yet, working in the patient dark while his wife lay on the other side of the wall and believed she knew what he was doing.

She did not know.

She thought she did. She thought he had gone back to the canoe.

She closed her eyes. The creek moved under its ice outside. The fire in the other room settled with a soft sound, ash falling through ash. Her daughter breathed.

Saya lay in the dark and thought about the petition and thought about the court and thought about the word *ancient* in the clerk's brown ink, and she did not think about the oilskin folded under the bench, waiting in the dark with the patience of a thing that already knows what it will be asked to do.
