The smell woke her before the light did.

She lay still on her sleeping mat and breathed it in. Something heavy and close, like wet stone, like the inside of a clay jar that had held water and then sat empty through a long season. Not rain yet. The promise of it. She had not smelled anything like it in three years, and her body recognized it before her mind did. A slow tightening in her chest. Not quite hope. Not quite dread.

Outside, a dog moved across the plaza. She heard its claws on the packed earth, then nothing.

She did not rise.

The thatch above her head was dark still, the last stars barely visible through the smoke hole. She lay on her back and let the fears come the way she always did in the hour before dawn. One at a time, so she could look at each one clearly before the day made that impossible.

The ceremony had changed nothing. That was the first fear, and the truest. She had led it well enough. She had done what her grandmother's grandmother had done, and the words had been the right words, and the people had stood in the right order around the plaza fire, and old Waka had sung the rain-calling song so slowly that the pauses between phrases felt like held breath. Even the children had been still. It had been a good ceremony. The kind that holds people together.

But the sky the next morning had been the same white-blue emptiness it had been for three years. The maize in the lower fields had stood with its leaves curled tight as fingers. Nothing had changed except that the people had briefly stopped watching each other with the particular hollowed look that came from hunger held too long.

She had told herself that was enough. She was not sure she believed it.

The second fear was Sokan.

She did not let herself think his name often. It was a habit she had built carefully over two seasons, the way you build a dam of woven branches and packed clay. Not to stop the water entirely, but to make it go where you could manage it. He had been her closest friend for twenty years. He had been wrong about the grain stores, and she had said so in front of the council, and he had not spoken to her since. Four months. She had counted them without meaning to.

She did not think she had been wrong to say it. She still did not think that. But she had said it in a way that left him no path back, and she had known she was doing it while she was doing it. That knowledge sat in her like a stone in a water jar. Not poisoning anything. Just present, making everything slightly heavier.

The third fear was harder to name. It was the fear that the drought had done something to the people that rain could not undo. She had watched it happen slowly. The way generosity contracted when the stores ran low. The way old arguments surfaced that had nothing to do with food. The way the young men's laughter had changed its quality, become something with an edge in it. Hunger did that. She knew it from the stories her mother had told, and her mother's mother before her. Hunger was patient. It worked on people the way water worked on clay. Not breaking them. Reshaping them, until one day you looked at someone you had known all your life and saw a shape you did not entirely recognize.

She rose.

Her deerskin dress was stiff with the cold. She pulled it over her head and felt it settle against her shoulders, smelling of woodsmoke and the dried sweat of the ceremony. She did not braid her hair. She pulled it back with a strip of hide and stepped over her sleeping daughter, a small child with her mouth open and one fist pressed against her cheek, and ducked through the low door of the house.

The air outside hit her like stepping into a river.

Not cold, exactly. Dense. The kind of air that had weight to it, that pressed against the skin rather than moving past it. The plaza was empty except for the dog she had heard, a lean brown animal that watched her from across the open ground with the calm, assessing gaze of something that had survived the same three years she had and reached similar conclusions about the world.

The hearth in the center of the plaza held a few orange coals under a grey crust of ash. Someone had covered the wood pile with bark sheets the night before. She noticed that. Someone had looked at the sky last evening and thought: it might rain.

She walked past the hearth and past the long house where the council met and past the two granary platforms, nearly empty now, the raised floors showing through the gaps in the woven walls, and up the slope toward the mound.

It was not a long walk. The mound rose at the northern edge of the village, low and broad, its grass still short and pale from the drought. She had walked to it every morning for as long as she could remember. First as a child at her grandmother's side, then alone, then with her daughter strapped to her back, then alone again. It was the oldest thing she knew. It had been there before the village in its current form, before the houses were arranged as they were now, before the names she knew for things. The people who had built it were her people's people, and their bones were in it. She thought of them sometimes as she walked. Not with grief exactly. More with the particular attention you gave to a fire that had been burning for a very long time.

She reached the crest and stopped.

The sky to the east was the color of a bruise. Not the clear dark blue of a dry night but something murkier, layered, with a greenish-grey underbelly to the clouds that had built while she slept. She could not see the horizon. The tree line that normally stood sharp against the dawn sky was blurred, softened, as though something was already falling between her and it.

She stood very still and breathed.

The smell was stronger here. Wet stone and turned earth and something else underneath. The faint sharp smell of lightning, still far off, still only a rumor in the air. She had not smelled lightning in so long that it took her a moment to name it.

Below her, the village was quiet. The thatched roofs of the houses were dark with moisture already, the dry grass having drunk whatever dew the night had managed to produce. The fields beyond the village were invisible in the grey light, but she knew where they were. She knew exactly how the maize stood in them, leaf-rolled and pale, the stalks thinner than they should have been at this point in the season. She knew the beans were not going to recover fully even if the rain came today. She knew the squash on the low ground had already given up.

She knew all of this. She stood on the mound's crest and watched the clouds build in the east and felt the weight of the air press against her face, and she thought: it is coming.

Not a prayer. Not a certainty. Just the plain observation of someone who had waited long enough to recognize what waiting almost over felt like.

The first frog called from somewhere in the direction of the wetlands. A single, tentative note, as if it too was not quite ready to believe.

She sat down on the damp grass at the mound's highest point and pulled her knees to her chest and waited for the light to come, or the rain, whichever arrived first.

The rain came before the light.

She heard it first. A sound like something being poured from a great height onto the canopy of trees to the east, a rushing, continuous sound that grew as it moved toward her across the ridge. Not the tentative pattering of the false starts she had learned not to trust. Not the three or four minutes of drops that had twice before raised her heart and then stopped as if reconsidering. This was the sound of something that had already committed.

She stood up.

The first drops hit her face before she could turn toward them. Large, cold, separate drops that struck her forehead and cheekbones with enough weight that she felt each one distinctly. Then the sound reached the village below her and the smell came with it: wet earth, ash, the sharp green smell of dry grass suddenly soaked, and underneath everything the deep mineral smell of the ground itself opening up after three years of being sealed. She had no single word for it in the language she thought in, but her body knew it the way her body knew her daughter's face. Without effort, without translation. Pure recognition.

The rain thickened.

Within the space of a few breaths it went from separate drops to a steady pour, and within a few more it was a real rain, a rain that filled the air and made a sound on the thatched roofs below her like someone running their hands across a drum. She could hear it on every surface. On the bark sheets covering the woodpile. On the clay jars someone had set under the granary eaves. On the leaves of the trees at the village edge. Each surface producing its own note in the same continuous chord.

She stood on the mound's crest and let it hit her.

Her hair was soaked through in moments, the strip of hide that had held it back now useless, the whole weight of it pressing cold against her neck and shoulders. Her deerskin dress darkened from pale brown to the color of wet bark and clung to her arms and her back. The rain ran down her face in streams. She did not move. She stood with her feet in the grass of the mound's crest and her face tilted slightly upward and she let it come.

Below her, the village woke.

She heard a child's voice first. A high, wordless shout of pure surprise, then another, then the sound of a door being pushed open and feet on wet ground. She watched two children run out of the nearest longhouse into the plaza, arms spread, turning in circles with their faces up. A third followed, smaller, stumbling in the mud that was already forming where the packed earth of the plaza had gone soft. A woman appeared in the doorway of the longhouse and stood there watching the children, one hand on the doorframe. Even from the mound's height she could see the particular stillness of her. The stillness of someone who does not yet trust what they are seeing and does not want to move in case it stops.

An old man came around the side of the council house. He had come to the village two winters ago from a settlement upriver, after that settlement's own stores ran out, one of the quiet additions that drought years produced. He walked out into the center of the plaza in the rain and then, without any apparent decision, dropped to his knees in the mud. He did not raise his hands or cry out. He simply knelt there with the rain coming down on his grey head and his hands open on his thighs, and she watched him and felt something move through her chest that was not quite grief and not quite gratitude and did not have a clean name.

She looked at him. She looked at the children spinning in the rain. She looked at the woman in the doorway who had not moved, and she thought: the ceremony did not do this.

She let herself think it plainly. The ceremony had been three days ago. The dancing, the smoking, the offerings at the mound's base, the songs she had learned from her grandmother and taught to the younger women last winter. She had led it with everything she had. She had meant every word of it. And then two days of nothing, the sky as pale and dry as bleached bone, and she had lain in her house on the second night and felt the familiar hollow feeling of a person who has asked for something and been told no.

The rain had come on its own schedule. The clouds had built because the season had finally turned, because the long dry pattern that had gripped them for three summers had exhausted itself, because weather moved in cycles that did not consult ceremonies or the people who held them.

She knew this. She had always known it, the same way she knew that a poultice did not heal a wound. The body healed the wound, and the poultice kept it clean while the body worked. You did not stop using poultices because of this.

The ceremony had not made the rain. The ceremony had done something else.

She looked at the old man still kneeling in the mud. She looked at the children, who had pulled a fourth child out of a doorway and were now all four of them standing in the deepest puddle they could find, which was growing by the minute at the low end of the plaza. She looked at the woman in the doorway, who had finally stepped out into the rain and was standing with her face up and her eyes closed, both hands at her sides.

Three years. Three years of watching people reshape themselves under hunger, of watching the particular erosions that fear and scarcity produced in people she had known all her life. Three years of ceremonies that she had led with full conviction while knowing, in the private part of herself that she could not silence, that conviction was not causation. She had led them anyway. She had led them because the people needed somewhere to put their fear that was not each other's throats. She had led them because the ceremony was the shape of what they were. The specific, irreplaceable shape of this people in this place. To abandon it in the hard years would have been to let the drought take the thing that mattered most.

The rain came down harder. Thunder rolled somewhere to the south, low and continuous, more felt than heard.

She was soaked to the skin. Her feet had sunk into the soft grass of the mound's crest and she could feel the cold mud between her toes, and the smell of the wet earth was so strong now that it was almost a taste. She reached down and pressed her palm flat against the ground, against the mound's wet flank, and held it there for a moment. The earth was cold and yielding and smelled of everything that had been put into it over all the years it had been accumulating the dead.

*You waited too*, she thought. Not to anyone in particular. Just the thought, offered to the ground the way you offered smoke. Not expecting an answer. Just completing the gesture.

She straightened and looked down at the village again.

More people were outside now. A group of women had gathered near the granary platforms with their arms around each other, talking, their voices lost in the rain. Two men were walking toward the fields, moving fast, wanting to see what the water was doing to the soil. The old man was still on his knees in the plaza mud, and one of the children, the smallest one, the one who had stumbled, had gone to him and was standing close. Not touching him. Just present, the way small children sometimes positioned themselves near grief or wonder without fully understanding either.

She watched all of it and felt the rain run down her face and did not move.

She was not ready to go back down yet. The mound's crest was where she thought clearly, and she needed one more moment of it before the day began its demands. Because the rain had come, and the rain was good, and the ceremony had not caused the rain. All three of these things were true at once, and she needed to hold all three of them steadily before she went back into the village and became, again, the person other people looked at when they needed to know what to think.

Below her, the smallest child had taken the old man's hand.

The rain came down., -

He came at first light.

She heard him before she saw him. The soft pull of wet grass under careful feet. The particular hesitation of someone who has been walking toward a thing for a long time and is not certain, even now, that he will go through with it. She was crouched at her fire pit, coaxing the coals back from the night's rain, and she did not look up.

The coals were stubborn. The wood she had brought inside before the storm was dry enough, but the morning air still held moisture, and the smoke curled low and reluctant, stinging her eyes. She had been awake since before dawn. She had slept badly. Not from worry, but from the sound of the rain, which had continued through the night in long, steady sheets, drumming on the thatch above her head, running off the eaves in a steady pour into the clay jar she had set out. The jar was full. She had checked it twice in the night, the way you check a thing you do not quite trust to be real.

Sokan stopped at the edge of her fire's circle and stood.

She fed a small piece of dry bark to the coals and watched the flame catch. The smoke thinned and rose straight for a moment, then bent in the morning air. Outside, the village was beginning to stir. She could hear voices near the granary platforms, and somewhere a child was already awake, already running, already shouting something she could not make out. The maize fields would be drawing people out all morning. The men who had gone to check the soil last night would be back with reports, and then there would be talk, and then decisions about the planting stores, and then more talk. The day had its shape already. She knew its shape.

She did not look at Sokan.

He did not speak. He lowered himself to the ground, and she heard the soft compression of wet grass, and he sat. Not close. Not far. The distance a man sits when he is not sure of his welcome but has come anyway.

She waited.

The fire settled into something steadier. She straightened and finally looked at him.

He was wet through, his hair loose and dark with rain, plastered flat against his neck and shoulders. His face was the face she had known for thirty years. Broad across the cheekbones, a jaw that had softened with age, eyes that had always been better at watching than at speaking. He looked as though he had been outside for some time. He looked as though he had not slept.

Between them, the fire put out its thin column of smoke.

He did not look away from her. She gave him that.

She waited for him to speak. He did not speak. A full breath passed, then another. The fire popped once and sent a small spray of sparks sideways into the wet grass where they died immediately. Somewhere near the granary, a woman laughed. A short, surprised sound, as if she had not expected to. The sound carried clearly in the washed air.

Then Sokan reached into the fold of his shirt and brought out the figure.

He set it on the ground between them.

She looked at it.

It was antler. A short length of shed tine, the base cut clean, the tip worked down to a blunt point. The carving covered most of its length: a figure, human or near-human, its arms close at its sides, its face suggested rather than detailed, two shallow cuts for eyes and a single horizontal line for a mouth. The legs tapered into the antler's natural narrowing. She could see the tool marks where the stone blade had worked the surface. Small, controlled cuts, each one deliberate, the kind of work you did slowly and in good light, the kind that could not be hurried without showing the hurry in the finished thing.

The figure was not large. It would have fit in a closed fist. But it had a quality she recognized from the best of his work. A stillness that was not emptiness. A sense of something attended to and resolved. She had watched Sokan carve before, in the years before the drought when there had been time for such things, sitting outside his house in the long evening light with a piece of antler across his knee, his hands moving in the small, patient rhythms of a man who was not in a hurry. He was good at it. He had always been good at it.

She had not known he was making this.

She looked at the figure for a long time. The fire's light moved across its surface and the antler caught it. Not bright, not flashy, but with the warm, faintly translucent glow of old bone that has been handled and smoothed by many hours of work. The eyes, if you could call them that, caught the light differently than the rest. Deeper, darker. Two small shadows in the pale surface.

She looked up at him.

His hands were in his lap. The knuckles were roughened and there was a thin line of dried blood along the base of his right thumb, the stone blade's work. He did not offer an explanation. He did not say this is for the ceremony or this is for the rain or this is because I was wrong. He said nothing. He had set it on the ground between them and now he was waiting, the way a man waits when he has done the only thing he knew how to do and has no words to put around it.

She thought about the night of the argument. She thought about what he had said. Not all of it, but the part that had landed in her like a stone dropped into still water. The part about the ceremony being a story she told herself to feel useful while the people starved. He had said it quietly, the way Sokan said most things, which had made it worse. She had not answered him. She had walked away and not spoken to him for three days, and then the ceremony had come and she had led it without looking at him, and then two days of nothing, and then the rain.

She thought about what the ceremony had and had not done.

She thought about what it cost a man to make something out of his own wrong.

She reached out and picked up the figure.

The antler was warm in her palm. Warmer than she expected, as if it had been held recently, as if Sokan had been carrying it inside his shirt for some time before he came. She turned it once. The carved figure caught the firelight from a new angle and the small shadow-eyes deepened. She ran her thumb along the body's length. Even. Deliberate. The work of a man who had sat with his mistake for a long time and made something out of it instead of burying it.

She did not say she forgave him.

She said nothing.

She closed her hand around the figure and held it.

His face did not change. But something in it, some held tension along the jaw, some careful stillness around the eyes, released. Not relief, exactly. Something quieter than relief. The look of a man who has been carrying a weight and has set it down and does not yet know what his hands will do without it.

She reached for the pot at the fire's edge. Maize porridge, thick, made with the rainwater she had collected from the eaves jar before dawn. The first cooking water in three years that had not tasted of mud and low river. She filled a bowl and held it out to him.

He took it.

They sat together at her fire while the village woke around them. The figure warmed in her closed hand. Outside, the wet maize in the fields stood a shade greener than it had at first light, as if even in a single morning it had remembered what it was supposed to be.

She would not forget what he had said. She did not think she was supposed to. The figure was not an erasure. It was a mark. Small and still and attended to, shaped from the same patience that had kept him in the wrong for four months before he found his way to her threshold. She understood this. She thought he understood it too.

The porridge steamed between his hands. He ate without speaking. She watched the fire.

Somewhere out in the fields, a man's voice rose. Not alarm. Something closer to wonder. A second voice joined it, then a third, and she could tell from the sound that they were standing in the maize rows and looking at the soil and saying to each other what the rain had done to it overnight. The dark mud. The standing water in the low rows. The way the plants had already shifted in the hours since the storm, leaves uncurling from their drought-rolled edges, silk less withered at the tips.

She would go and see for herself soon. She would need to know how far the rain had reached, whether the river was rising, what the storage pits looked like after the night's soaking. There was much to do. There was always much to do, and now the doing of it would be different. Not easier, not yet. But possible in a way it had not been yesterday.

She looked at the figure in her palm. Small and still. The face that was not quite a face. Two cuts and a line, enough to suggest a person without demanding to be one person in particular. She thought about that choice. She thought about how easy it would have been to carve more, to add detail, to make it specific. He had not. He had stopped at the point where the thing was itself without being anything else, and she thought that was its own kind of statement, the kind Sokan made when he was being most honest.

She had not said she forgave him. She was holding it. He would understand the difference, and the distance between those two things, and what it meant that she had chosen one and not the other.

That was enough for now.

The voices in the field grew louder. A fourth man now, then a woman's laugh, sharp and surprised, the kind of sound that comes when something you had stopped believing in turns out to be true. She heard children's feet on the wet path outside, quick and slapping in the mud, then a child's voice asking something she could not make out, and an older child answering with the authority of someone who has just learned a thing and wants to spend it.

Sokan set down the empty bowl.

She did not look at him. She was watching the fire, which had burned low while they sat, the coals gone orange and quiet under a thin skin of ash.

He said nothing. He did not thank her for the porridge. She did not expect him to. There were things between them that a bowl of porridge could not address, and both of them knew it, and the knowing was its own kind of honesty.

He stood. The movement was unhurried. A man rising from a fire the way men do when they have sat long enough and the day is waiting. He picked up his outer wrap from where it had dried across the hearthstone, the tanned hide still dark at the edges from the rain, and settled it over his shoulders. Then he looked at her. One look, brief and direct, the kind that does not ask for anything. He nodded once.

She nodded back.

He left.

She listened to his footsteps cross the threshold, heard the wet sound of his feet on the mud path, heard it fade into the larger noise of the village waking. Jars scraping stone. A dog shaking water from its coat. Someone calling a name across the plaza.

She sat with the fire.

The figure lay in her open palm now, and she looked at it. The small still body, the suggested face, the tool marks running in their careful parallel lines down the length of the tine. She thought about how long it took to make a thing like this. Not the hours of cutting and smoothing, though those too, the bone dust, the slow work of the stone blade. The other kind of time. The time of deciding to make it at all. The time of sitting with the decision before you could begin.

He had not come to her the morning after the argument. He had not come the day after that. He had come now, three years of drought and two days of rain later, with his hands empty except for what he had made.

She thought: some debts cannot be paid. She thought: this is not payment. She thought: but it is something.

Outside, the voices in the maize rows had multiplied. She could hear them moving through the field in the way people move when they are looking at something they want to measure. Slow, stopping, bending down to touch the soil or the base of a stalk, then moving on. She knew that walk. She had walked it herself at first light, before Sokan came, stepping through the rows in the dark with her feet reading the ground, feeling where the water had pooled in the low places and where the roots had drunk and where the soil was still only wet at the surface. She had not let herself feel anything yet. She had been gathering information, the way you gather it before you let yourself believe it.

She believed it now.

She pressed her palms flat on her thighs and stood.

The figure she tucked into the fold of her dress, against her ribs, where it rested small and solid with each breath. She thought: I will feel this all day. She thought: that is the point.

She ducked through the doorway into the morning.

The air hit her. Cool and wet, carrying the smell of the earth drinking. That deep green-dark smell had been absent for so long she had forgotten it was a smell and not just a word for something. The plaza was tracked with footprints, human and dog, pressed deep into mud that yesterday had been packed and cracked and pale as old bone. The cooking fire of the house two doors down sent a thin line of smoke straight up into the still air. No wind yet. The sky above the eastern tree line was the color of a mussel shell, pale and faintly iridescent, and the light coming through it was the soft, flat light of a morning that has not yet decided what kind of day it will be.

She stood at her threshold and looked at it.

She was not a woman who wept easily. She had not wept during the drought. Not when the second planting failed, not when the river dropped so low you could walk its sandbars in midsummer, not when old Peku died in the hungry month and they had buried him with the ceremony shortened because no one had the strength to do it properly. She had not wept during the ceremony two nights ago, though she had felt something move through her that was close to it. Not grief. Something more like grief's older cousin. The feeling of having held a thing so long your hands have shaped themselves around it.

She did not weep now.

But she pressed her hand to the fold of her dress where the figure rested, felt its small hard shape through the deerskin, and she thought about the face that was not quite a face. Two cuts and a line. She thought about the shape of it, small and still and attended to, carrying its maker's long silence in every careful stroke.

She had not said she forgave him. She was carrying it.

She did not know yet what the distance between those two things was. She thought it might be the most honest distance she had ever kept.

She let her hand fall and walked out into the mud, toward the sound of voices in the field, toward the maize that had remembered overnight what it was supposed to be., -

She did not go to the field.

She meant to. She had taken three steps toward the sound of voices when something pulled her left instead of forward. Not a thought, not a decision. More the way water finds a low place without deciding to. Her feet took her along the edge of the plaza, past the house where the cooking smoke rose, past the storage pit where someone had already set a clay jar to catch the eave-drip, past the place where the path narrowed between two houses and the mud was ankle-deep and cold and she felt it press up between her toes and over the top of her foot with each step.

She was walking toward the mound.

She had known it, probably, before her feet did.

The path climbed slightly as she left the village edge, the ground firming under her as the soil changed from the dark plaza mud to the mound's own earth. Older, denser, packed by seasons and ceremony and the slow weight of what it held. The grass on the mound's lower slope was bent flat by the rain, each blade pressed down and glistening, and where the runoff had cut small channels down the side she could see the exposed soil in thin ribbons. Dark at the surface, pale and sandy below, and in one place a fragment of shell catching the flat morning light, white as a thumbnail.

She stopped and looked at it. She did not pick it up.

The rain was still falling. Not the heavy rain of two nights ago, the kind that had come in behind a wall of wind with lightning walking across the ridge, but something quieter. A steady, soft, purposeful rain that fell straight down in the still air and made a sound on the leaves like someone turning pages. She could hear the frogs again. They had been silent for so long she had stopped expecting them, and now they were everywhere in the low places, filling the wet air with a noise that was almost too much, almost reckless, as if they too were not sure they believed it yet and were saying so as loudly as they could.

She began to walk the perimeter.

She did not decide to do this either. It was simply what her feet knew to do on the mound's slope. The old path, worn into the grass in better years, nearly invisible now after three years of thin attendance and dry ground. She found it anyway. Her feet found it. The body remembers what the mind sometimes sets aside out of grief or necessity, and walks the path again when the conditions allow.

The figure shifted against her ribs as she walked, small and hard through the deerskin, warm now from her body's heat. She did not hold it. She let it move.

She was thinking about the ceremony.

She had been thinking about it since the rain began. Not the ceremony as it had looked from outside, the singing and the fire and the tobacco and the people standing together in the plaza with their faces turned up, but the ceremony as it had felt from inside, from the place where she stood at the center of it and held the thread of it in her hands and felt, the whole time, the gap between what she was doing and what she was claiming to do.

She had not claimed, exactly. She was not a fool. She had said: we will do what we know to do. She had said: we will hold ourselves together in the shape of people who have not given up. She had said these things in the language of ceremony, which is not always the same as the language of speech, but she had said them. And then the rain had come.

She knew the rain had not come because of the ceremony.

She knew this the way she knew the river's moods, the way she knew where the deer trails ran, the way she knew which children were sick before their mothers said so. Not as an argument or a conclusion but as a simple fact about the world, the kind that does not require defending because it does not require believing. The rain had come because the rain had come. Because the drought had run its course, or the winds had shifted, or the sky had finally done what skies do when they are ready. Not because she had sung the right words or burned the right tobacco or held the people in the right formation on the right night.

She had known this while she was doing it.

She had done it anyway.

She rounded the mound's northern face, where the slope was steepest and the runoff had left the longest channels, and stopped for a moment and looked out over the fields. From here she could see the maize rows in the gray morning light, and she could see the people moving through them. Small figures, bending and rising, bending and rising, their voices carrying up to her in fragments she could not quite make into words. She watched them. She thought about what they had looked like two nights ago, standing in the plaza with the fire between them and the dark pressing in from all sides, singing the songs she had taught them, their voices uncertain at first and then finding each other and steadying, the way voices do when they are held together by something larger than any of them.

That was what she had been doing. Not making rain. Making that.

She turned and continued walking.

The mound was not large. She could walk its perimeter in the time it took the cooking fire to go from flame to ember. She had walked it hundreds of times. In ceremony, in grief, in the ordinary daily habit of a woman who lived near a thing and could not help being drawn to it. She knew every slope and hollow of it, knew where the ground was softer and where a root had lifted the surface slightly and where, in the wet seasons, a small pool formed at the southwestern base and held standing water for days.

The pool was there now. She saw it as she came around the southern side. A brown oval of sky reflected in still water, the maize field beyond it, the tree line beyond that, and above the tree line the clouds beginning to thin at their edges, showing a pale brightness that was not yet sun but was the promise of sun, the sky's way of saying: not yet, but soon.

She stopped at the pool's edge.

She was thinking about the ones inside the mound.

She thought about them often. Not as presences that watched her, but as the fact of them, the sheer accumulated fact of lives that had been lived and ended and placed here with care, wrapped and arranged and given back to the earth with the things they had made or worn or loved. She did not know all their names. She knew some. She had been taught some, and she had taught others, and she understood that the chain of knowing was imperfect, that names fell out of it the way water falls through cupped hands. Not from carelessness, but from the simple fact that hands are not made to hold water forever.

But she knew they were there. And she knew that every ceremony she had ever conducted on this mound had been, among other things, a way of saying: we know you are there. We have not stopped knowing.

She crouched at the pool's edge and pressed her palm flat to the mound's wet slope. The earth was cold and soft, and it gave slightly under her hand, and she felt the moisture seep into the lines of her palm and the small calluses at the base of her fingers. She held her hand there.

She thought: the ceremony did not bring the rain.

She thought: but the ceremony held us together long enough for the rain to matter.

She thought about what would have happened if the rain had come to a people who had already scattered. Who had left for other villages, other river bends, other ground. The rain would have fallen on empty fields. The maize would have had no one to harvest it. The mound would have had no one to walk its perimeter, no one to say the names, no one to teach the names to the ones who came after. The rain would have been rain, which is only water, which means nothing without the people it falls on.

She had not brought the rain. She had kept the people here to receive it.

That was not nothing. That was, in fact, everything. Or at least the part of everything that was hers to do.

She lifted her hand from the earth. The mud had left a clear print of her palm on the slope, every line distinct, the rain already beginning to soften its edges. She looked at it for a moment. Then she stood.

She pressed her hand to her ribs where the figure rested, felt it through the deerskin. Small, still, the blunt tip of the tine pressing faintly against her palm. She thought about Sokan's face when she had taken it. The way he had looked at her hands and not her eyes. She thought about the argument they had not finished and the years that had passed inside it and the fact that she had not said she forgave him and was carrying it anyway, and she thought: maybe that is what forgiveness actually is. Not the saying. Not the feeling, which comes and goes and cannot be trusted. The carrying. The decision to keep the thing on your body, close, where you feel it with each breath. Not repaired, exactly. Acknowledged. Marked. Made possible to return to.

She did not know if that was enough. She thought it might have to be.

The rain fell on the mound's slope, on the pool, on the bent grass, on the maize rows where the voices moved. Somewhere below her, a child laughed. A short, surprised sound, the laugh of someone who has stepped into a puddle deeper than expected and found it funny rather than terrible. She heard it and felt something shift in her chest, small and warm, like an ember catching.

She looked out at the fields one more time. The clouds were thinning faster now at the eastern edge, and the light coming through was beginning to have color. The faint gold of a sun not yet risen but close, the kind of light that makes wet leaves look as if they have been painted, each one distinct, each one holding its own small pool of sky.

She thought of the ones already inside the mound. She thought of the ones who would follow. Herself, one day, and after her the ones she had not yet met, the ones who would walk this same perimeter in seasons she would never see, in rains she would never feel, saying names that included hers.

She thought: hold together long enough. That is all. Hold together long enough for the rain to matter, and then hold together after, because the rain is not the end of anything, only a pause in the needing.

She touched the figure once through the deerskin. The small hard shape of it, still and attended to.

Then she turned and walked down the mound's slope toward the voices in the field, her feet pressing clear prints into the wet earth behind her, the frogs loud in every low place, the smell of the ground still drinking rising around her with each step.
