The room smelled of carpet cleaner and something older underneath it, a faint mineral damp from the concrete slab the carpet had been laid over, the particular civic smell of a building maintained without ever being loved.

Tayak stopped in the doorway for three seconds. She counted them. Cecile Marant had taught her that: three seconds to read a room before you walk into it, because once you're in, you're already performing.

Seven panelists at the dais. The table was draped in dark blue cloth with the state seal centered on the front panel, and the nameplates were white tent cards with black block lettering, the kind you'd see at any county planning meeting in Maryland, the kind that suggested the proceedings were orderly and that orderliness was itself a form of fairness. Small desktop microphones in front of each seat. A camera on a tripod at the back of the room, its red light already on.

She found Slade's nameplate before she found his face. Far right of the panel, which put him at the edge of her sight line when she faced the witness table. G. SLADE, SECTION 106 COMPLIANCE REVIEW. He was writing something on a yellow legal pad when she came in, and he didn't look up.

She had spent forty minutes at 3 a.m. deciding whether to file a conflict-of-interest objection. She'd drafted it twice, deleted it twice. An objection would be noted, then denied, and she would spend the rest of the hearing being the witness who had tried to remove a panelist. The science had to be enough. The science was enough. She told herself that again now, standing in the doorway, and then she walked in.

The witness table was a folding table pushed to the center of the room, facing the dais, with a single gooseneck microphone and a plastic pitcher of water that had been there long enough to sweat through its paper coaster. Two padded metal chairs behind it, one for her and one empty. She set her folder on the table: the forty-three-page field assessment on top, then Voss's support letter, then the stratigraphic profile photographs in a labeled sleeve, then the chain-of-custody log for artifact 18CH-447-051. She had arranged them in this order at six this morning, rearranged them twice, then put them back the way they started.

The gallery behind her was filling. She could hear it: the creak of metal chair legs, the low murmur of people settling, a phone going to silent with a soft double-tap. She did not turn around. She had told herself she would not turn around, because if she saw Nora's face she would lose something she needed to keep for the next two hours.

But she knew they were there. Kimi had texted at seven: *We're coming. Both of us. Don't argue.* Tayak had not argued.

The chair of the commission was a woman named Adaira Hollins, whose nameplate read CHAIR A. HOLLINS and who had the particular stillness of someone who had run difficult meetings for a long time and had stopped being surprised by what people said in them. She was reviewing a document, her reading glasses pushed to the end of her nose, and she did not look up when Tayak sat down either.

The fluorescent panels above hummed at a frequency just below conscious attention. Tayak was aware of it the way she was aware of her own heartbeat: not constantly, but whenever she stopped moving. She had not slept. She had lain in the dark in the house on Billingsley Road, Nora having insisted she stay, and she had stared at the ceiling and run through the testimony in sequence. The stratigraphy. The radiocarbon dates. The artifact provenance. The chain-of-custody documentation. The oral history evidence, which she had decided to present last, not because it was weakest, but because it needed to land into silence rather than into the noise of procedural questions about the science.

She had rehearsed the oral history section eleven times. She knew exactly how she would say it, and she also knew that when she stood at the microphone and said it in this room, with Nora sitting behind her, it would not sound the way it had sounded in her car at 2 a.m.

A staff member, a young woman in a dark blazer with a laptop open on a side table, spoke quietly into a headset and nodded at something on her screen. The livestream was running. Somewhere out there, people were watching a static shot of a witness table with a gooseneck microphone and a sweating pitcher of water and a woman in a gray blazer who had not slept.

Chair Hollins set her document down and looked up. She had a way of making the room come to attention without raising her voice or her hand. She simply stopped doing what she had been doing, and the room noticed.

"We'll begin," she said. "This is the Maryland Heritage Commission's formal hearing on Site 18CH-447, Charles County. The record will reflect that all seven panelists are present." She paused. "Dr. Moyaone, you've been designated as the primary invited witness for this session. You'll be sworn in before you begin."

The staff member appeared at Tayak's elbow with a printed card. Tayak stood. She raised her right hand and read the affirmation from the card, that her testimony was true and complete to the best of her knowledge, and her voice was steady, which surprised her.

She sat back down.

Under the table, her left hand pressed flat against her thigh. It was something she had started doing in the field when a find was significant enough to make her hands want to shake: press one hand flat, feel the pressure, let the ground hold it. There was no ground here, only thin carpet over concrete, but the pressure helped anyway.

Slade had stopped writing. She could see him in her peripheral vision. He had set his pen down and folded his hands on the table in front of him, and he was looking at her with the careful, closed expression of a man who has decided in advance to listen.

She opened her folder to the field assessment. The first page: *Site 18CH-447, Charles County, Maryland. Field Assessment and Stratigraphic Report. Principal Investigator: Dr. Tayak Moyaone.* Her name on the first line, which was not something she had taken for granted when she filed it.

Chair Hollins said, "Dr. Moyaone, you may begin when you're ready."

Tayak leaned toward the microphone. The room behind her was very quiet.

She was ready. She had been ready for two thousand years, give or take. She thought that, almost smiled, and then she began.

She had written the opening sentence of the field assessment fourteen times before she got it right. She read it now the way she had written it: plainly, without apology.

"Site 18CH-447 is a burial mound located on a river terrace above Mattawoman Creek in Charles County, Maryland. Excavation began in January of this year under a permit issued by the Maryland Historical Trust. Over eleven months, my team identified seven distinct depositional strata. The earliest deposit dates to approximately 100 BC. The most recent dates to the late seventeenth century. The site documents continuous Indigenous presence on this land across two thousand years."

She paused. Not for effect. She had told herself not to do anything for effect. The next section required her to shift from summary to evidence, and she wanted the panel to know the shift was coming.

"I'll walk you through the strata in sequence, beginning at the surface."

She had the photographs in the labeled sleeve. She did not reach for them yet. She had learned, in eleven months of talking to people about this site, that photographs produced before the words were ready became a distraction. People looked at the image and stopped listening to the explanation.

"Stratum one. Depth zero to thirty centimeters. Late colonial period, consistent with the late seventeenth century. We recovered a blue glass trade bead, cobalt, drawn-glass construction, consistent with Dutch manufacture between 1660 and 1700, and a fragment of a folded document sealed in oilskin. The document is a copy of a 1697 petition filed by Piscataway community members in Charles County court. It was placed deliberately, not lost. The oilskin is a preservation method. Someone put it there to keep."

Slade had unfolded his hands. He was writing again. She kept her eyes on the chair.

"Stratum two. Depth thirty to sixty-five centimeters. Pre-contact, late woodland period. Cord-marked ceramic sherds recovered from the eastern face, consistent with regional Piscataway manufacture. No European material. The transition from stratum one to stratum two represents a clear cultural boundary: colonial-contact material above, pre-contact below, with no mixing, no disturbance, no evidence of later intrusion into the lower layers."

She reached for the sleeve. She pulled out the stratigraphic profile photograph, a clean shot of the east wall of the main trench, the strata visible as distinct bands of soil color and texture, labeled in her handwriting along the right margin, and she held it up toward the panel.

"This is the east wall of the primary trench. The dark band at approximately forty centimeters is the cord-marked ceramic layer. The lighter band above it is the colonial deposit. The boundary between them is sharp. You can see it clearly. This is not gradual accumulation. These are intentional deposits, separated by time and by purpose."

Chair Hollins leaned forward slightly. "Dr. Moyaone, when you say intentional, can you explain how you distinguish intentional deposit from natural accumulation?"

It was a fair question. It was the right question. Tayak had been hoping someone would ask it, because the answer made the case better than any single artifact.

"Yes. Natural accumulation is characterized by gradual, undifferentiated soil buildup: organic matter, leaf litter, wind-blown sediment. What we see at 18CH-447 is different in three ways. First, the stratum boundaries are abrupt rather than gradual. Second, the artifacts within each stratum are arranged, not scattered. The trade bead was positioned at the center of the first deposit, not at the edge. The oilskin document was folded and placed face-down. Third, and most significantly, the mound itself is a constructed feature. It does not occur naturally on this terrace. The underlying geology is flat river clay. The elevation, approximately 1.4 meters above the surrounding grade, was built by human hands, in stages, over centuries."

She set the photograph down and reached for the next one: the ground-penetrating radar survey overlay, the mound's internal structure rendered in false color, the successive layers visible as concentric bands.

"This is the GPR survey. Each band represents a distinct construction episode. Seven episodes over two thousand years. The mound was not built at once. It was added to, deliberately, by people who knew what was already there and chose to continue it."

She let the photograph speak for a moment, then moved on.

"Strata three through six. Depth sixty-five centimeters to one meter forty. Pre-contact, spanning roughly the third century CE through the thirteenth. We recovered a shell gorget in stratum three: marine shell, engraved, consistent with Woodland-period manufacture. A second gorget, older, in stratum four. A copper pendant in stratum five, cold-hammered, Great Lakes copper, consistent with regional trade networks active between 200 and 400 CE. An antler figure in stratum six, carved, consistent with late Woodland ceremonial practice."

She had the chain-of-custody log open now, and she read the catalog numbers for each artifact without looking up: 18CH-447-019, 18CH-447-033, 18CH-447-051, 18CH-447-064. The numbers were not interesting. The numbers were the point. They meant the artifacts were real, documented, photographed, logged, conserved, and held in a controlled environment at the university lab. They meant no one could say she had made this up.

"Stratum seven. The deepest layer. Depth one meter forty to one meter sixty-eight. This is where I want to spend a moment on methodology, because this is where the dating evidence is strongest and where I expect the most questions."

A panelist toward the center of the dais shifted in his chair and leaned forward. He had a yellow legal pad and had been writing steadily since she began. He looked up.

"Stratum seven yielded two radiocarbon samples. Both were submitted to the University of Maryland's radiocarbon lab under blind protocol: the lab did not know the site number or the excavation context when it processed the samples. Sample one was charred wood from a prepared hearth feature at the base of the mound. Sample two was organic material associated with the primary burial, specifically degraded plant fiber consistent with a burial wrapping."

She paused. She looked directly at the panelist with the legal pad, because she could see from his body language that he was the one who would push on this.

"Sample one returned a date of 2,078 years before present, plus or minus 45 years. Sample two returned 2,091 years before present, plus or minus 52 years. Calibrated against the IntCal20 curve, both samples fall within the range of approximately 120 to 75 BC. The midpoint of that range is approximately 100 BC."

The panelist set his pen down. "Madam Chair, may I?"

Chair Hollins nodded.

"Dr. Moyaone." He had a measured, unhurried voice, the voice of a man who asked questions for a living and was not in a hurry to be answered. "The margin of error you've cited: forty-five years on one sample, fifty-two on the other. That's a range of nearly a century. How confident are you in the 100 BC figure, given that uncertainty?"

She had rehearsed this. She had rehearsed it because it was the right challenge, and because the answer mattered.

"The margin of error is not a weakness in the dating. It's a property of the method, and it's reported honestly. What matters is that both samples, run independently, overlap at the same calibrated range. If they had returned divergent results, that would be a problem. They didn't. They corroborate each other. The range they share, roughly 120 to 75 BC, is the actual finding. When I say approximately 100 BC, I'm using the midpoint of that shared range as a shorthand. The finding is not that the mound was built in exactly 100 BC. The finding is that the earliest deposit at this site is no more recent than 75 BC and no older than 120 BC, and that it is therefore pre-contact by more than fifteen hundred years."

The panelist looked at her for a moment, then wrote something on his legal pad.

"And the two samples," he said. "Were they from the same feature, or different features?"

"Different features. The charred wood was from a prepared hearth, a circular arrangement of fire-cracked rock at the mound's base, which is a construction-phase feature. The organic fiber was from the burial itself, approximately thirty centimeters northeast of the hearth. Different features, different material types, same calibrated range. That's the corroboration."

He nodded once and wrote again. She took that as permission to continue.

"The primary burial in stratum seven is a single individual, interred in a flexed position, oriented toward high ground. No European material anywhere in the burial context. The copper pendant, catalog number 18CH-447-051, was placed beneath the individual, face up. The pendant is cold-hammered Great Lakes copper. It did not arrive at this site through European trade. The nearest source for this copper is the Lake Superior basin, and the trade routes that carried it south are documented in the archaeological literature going back to the Early Woodland period."

She pulled the last photograph from the sleeve: the pendant in situ, before it had been lifted, lying in the pale clay of stratum seven with the burial's skeletal outline visible at the upper edge of the frame. She had taken this photograph herself, lying on her stomach at the edge of the trench in the cold, her breath fogging the lens, and she had taken it six times before she had one that was sharp enough.

She held it up.

"This is the pendant in context. Before removal. The position, centered beneath the individual, face up, is consistent with intentional placement as a grave offering. It was not lost. It was not dropped. It was put there by someone who knew what they were doing and why."

The room was quiet. Not the polite quiet of people waiting for the next slide, but the different quiet of people looking at something and understanding, for the first time, what they are looking at.

Chair Hollins said, "Dr. Moyaone, one more clarifying question before we continue. The seven strata: you've described them as representing continuous presence. But is it possible that the site was abandoned for periods between deposits and then reoccupied by different groups?"

Tayak set the photograph down.

"It's a fair question, and I want to answer it carefully. Abandonment between deposit episodes is possible. We can't rule it out from stratigraphy alone. What we can say is this: the construction method is consistent across all seven strata. The mound was built by the same technique, successive layers of compacted earth, each layer capped before the next was begun. That technique is not generic. It requires knowledge of what was already there and deliberate continuation of the same practice. Additionally, the artifact sequence is internally coherent. The materials shift over time in ways consistent with a single cultural tradition responding to changing circumstances, not with a succession of unrelated groups using the same location independently. The shell gorgets, the copper pendant, the antler figure, the ceramic: they speak the same material language across fifteen hundred years. That's not reoccupation. That's continuity."

Chair Hollins wrote something. She did not look up when she wrote it, which Tayak had learned to read as a sign that what had just been said was going into the record as stated, without revision.

"Thank you," Chair Hollins said. "Please continue."

Tayak looked down at her folder. The scientific evidence was complete. She had given them the strata, the dates, the artifacts, the methodology, the corroboration. She had answered the hard questions without conceding anything that was actually true. The room was attentive: she could feel it behind her, the particular quality of attention that is not yet moved but is no longer neutral.

The next section was the oral history evidence.

She turned the page.

She had rehearsed this part differently from the rest. The scientific section she had rehearsed like a deposition: question, answer, citation, source. This part she had rehearsed the way her grandmother told stories, from the beginning, in order, without skipping.

She set her folder flat on the table.

"I want to describe a second category of evidence," she said. "Not as a replacement for the stratigraphic record, and not as an alternative to it. As a parallel record that the stratigraphic evidence has now corroborated, independently, layer by layer."

She paused to let that framing land.

"The Piscataway people maintained an oral record of this mound. Not a general tradition. Not a legend about a sacred hill. A specific, structured account of what the mound contains, who placed each object, when, and why, transmitted from named person to named person across generations, with each link in the chain identified by name, by relationship to the teller, and by the occasion on which the transmission occurred."

The panelist with the legal pad had his pen in his hand. He was not writing.

"I want to be precise about what I mean by structured," Tayak said. "This is not a story that was told generally, to anyone who happened to be present. It was transmitted as a formal act of keeping: a deliberate, occasion-specific transfer of a specific account from one named keeper to the next. The keeper knew who had told them. They knew who they were to tell. They knew the account was not theirs to alter. This is not folklore. It is not myth. It is a form of record-keeping that predates writing in this region by more than a millennium and that Western scholarship has systematically misclassified as the former in order to avoid treating it as the latter."

A chair shifted in the audience. She did not look back.

"I can name the links I can verify."

She opened her folder, not because she needed the notes, but because she wanted the panel to see that the names were written down, that they existed in the record.

"The current keeper is my grandmother, Nora Moyaone, eighty-two years old, Billingsley Road, Charles County. She has held this account for sixty-two years. She received it from a woman named Ida, who held it before her. Ida received it from her own grandmother, who transmitted it to Ida when Ida was nineteen years old, in a kitchen in Charles County, and who told her at that time that some of the earliest names had thinned over generations, that the chain had been damaged but not broken. That woman knew the chain back to a woman named Saya."

She looked up from the folder.

"Saya is documented. She is not only a name in an oral chain. She appears in the colonial court record. She filed a petition in Charles County in 1697 on behalf of the Piscataway people, arguing continuous occupation of the high ground adjacent to Mattawoman Creek, the ground on which Site 18CH-447 stands. That petition is in the Maryland State Archives. It was entered into the court record by a clerk named Thomas Ware during the winter session of that year. Saya's name is in the journal. The petition uses the phrase 'ancient possessors,' language drawn from a ruling by Judge Thomas Notley acknowledging prior Piscataway land claims. Saya knew the mound. She knew what it held. The oral chain and the colonial record agree on this."

A panelist at the near end of the dais, a woman Tayak had not heard speak yet, who had been still for the better part of an hour, had stopped writing. Her pen rested flat on her legal pad. She was looking at Tayak with the particular stillness of someone who has understood something and is waiting to see if the speaker knows it too.

Tayak continued.

"The oilskin document we recovered from stratum one, the copy of Saya's 1697 petition, was wrapped and placed in the mound by Saya's husband, Okwi, after the petition failed. The oral chain preserves this. Okwi understood that two forms of keeping were better than one: paper was for courts and story was for people, and the mound needed both. He was not a keeper of the oral chain himself. That keeping passed through Saya and forward through her line. But the act of burial was his, and the chain names him for it."

She let that settle before continuing.

"After Saya, the chain names a man called Matta, her grandson in the oral account. A pipe-maker. The account associates him with a ceramic pipe bowl, dark-fired, placed in what I can now identify as stratum three. We recovered a ceramic pipe bowl from stratum three. The oral account describes its placement. The stratigraphy confirms it."

She said it plainly, the way her grandmother said things that were simply true.

"After Matta, a keeper named Tessa, his granddaughter. The oral account associates Tessa with a shell gorget placed in the mound in an act of deliberate keeping at a time when the ceremonies were being suppressed and the community was under active colonial pressure. We recovered a shell gorget from stratum four. The oral account says Tessa stood alone at the mound and spoke the names of those who had stood there before her. The gorget was there when we opened that layer. It was exactly where the account said it would be."

The room was quiet in a way that Tayak had not heard it be quiet before. Not the attentive quiet of the scientific testimony, which was the quiet of people following an argument, but a different quiet: the quiet of people who have stopped following and started listening.

She did not rush it.

"The chain goes further back than Tessa. It goes back through names I cannot all document in colonial records, names that exist only in the oral account, transmitted keeper to keeper, occasion to occasion, for two thousand years. But I want to be precise about what 'only in the oral account' means in this context, because I think the panel deserves precision on this point."

She turned the page in her folder, though she was not reading from it.

"The chain does not merely claim that the Piscataway were present at this location. It describes specific acts. It describes a woman, the earliest named keeper, who placed a copper pendant beneath a burial in the mound's deepest layer when her daughter died, because she had no other place to put her grief. It describes the pendant as cold-hammered copper, placed face up, centered beneath the individual. It describes this as the founding act of the mound: the first deposit, from which everything else followed."

She reached into the folder and took out the photograph again, the pendant in situ, lying in the pale clay of stratum seven.

"This is what we found in stratum seven. Cold-hammered Great Lakes copper. Face up. Centered beneath the primary burial." She held it up, not toward the panel this time, but toward the room. "The oral account described this object and this placement. We did not know that when we opened stratum seven. We found the pendant, documented it, and then, afterward, my grandmother told me what the chain says about it. The description matched. Not approximately. Precisely."

She set the photograph down.

"I am not asking this panel to treat oral history as equivalent to a radiocarbon date. I am asking something more specific than that. I am asking the panel to recognize that when a structured, named chain of transmission describes a specific object in a specific position in a specific layer of a specific mound, and the archaeological record confirms that description independently, the oral account has been verified. Not by faith. By evidence. The same standard we apply to any corroborating source."

She looked at the dais. The panelist with the legal pad had his pen flat on the table. Chair Hollins had her hands folded. The still panelist at the near end had not moved.

Slade, at the far right of the dais, had been still for some time. He had not asked a question since the scientific testimony. He was looking at the table in front of him with an expression Tayak could not read from the podium. Not hostility, not agreement. Something more interior than either, the face of a man carrying a decision he had already made, waiting for the moment when the decision would become an action.

She thought of Nora on the phone, three weeks ago: *The record is not the thing. The thing is the thing. But the record is how the thing survives you.*

"The chain of names that my grandmother holds," Tayak said, "goes back to a woman who buried a copper pendant in a mound above the Mattawoman Creek approximately two thousand years ago. That woman's name is in the chain. My grandmother knows it. She has held it for sixty-two years. She will testify to it before this panel tomorrow."

She closed her folder.

"Every link in that chain is a person who made a choice. They chose to receive this account, to hold it accurately, and to pass it on. Some of them made that choice under colonial suppression, when holding Piscataway knowledge was dangerous. Some of them made it in kitchens and at fires and on deathbeds, with no audience and no institutional recognition. They made it because they understood that the ground does not speak for itself, that someone has to speak for it, and that the speaking has to be careful and continuous and named."

The HVAC system hummed in the ceiling. Behind her, a chair creaked once and was still.

"The mound has corroborated their keeping," Tayak said. "Stratum by stratum. Object by object. The science and the oral record are not two different arguments for the same conclusion. They are two independent witnesses who have never spoken to each other and who have given the same testimony."

She stepped back from the microphone.

Chair Hollins looked down at her notes, then up, and her expression was not the careful neutral of a chair managing a difficult hearing. It was something else: the face of a woman who had heard something she was going to have to think about for a long time.

"Thank you, Dr. Moyaone," she said. "We'll take a fifteen-minute recess."

The recess was over before Tayak had finished her water.

She had stood in the corridor outside the hearing room, a narrow municipal hallway with a water fountain bolted to the wall at the wrong height and a framed photograph of the Chesapeake Bay that had been hanging slightly crooked for long enough that no one saw it anymore, and she had drunk from a paper cup and watched the fluorescent light buzz in its fixture overhead and tried not to think about anything. The room had changed in fifteen minutes. The HVAC system hummed. Someone behind Tayak shifted in a chair, a faint complaint of metal on tile, quickly stilled.

"I have no questions for this witness," Slade said.

His voice was even. Not flat. There was something in it, some quality of deliberateness, as if each word had been chosen from among several and these were the ones that survived. He did not look at Hollins when he said it. He was looking at the table in front of him, at the space between his hands and his nameplate.

The silence that followed was different from the silence before it.

Before, the room had been quiet the way rooms are quiet when they are listening, a collective held breath, attention directed outward toward the podium. This silence was interior. It turned inward, toward the panel, toward the far right of the dais where Slade sat with his hands flat and his notepad closed and his face giving away nothing that Tayak could name from where she stood.

She looked at him directly for the first time since the hearing began.

He was not what she had expected. She had built a version of him in the eleven months since the suspension notice, a version assembled from the notice's language, from Marcus's briefings, from the single meeting where he had sat across a conference table and asked careful questions and given nothing away. That version was adversarial, or at least instrumental: a man who had signed the notice because it served something, who had sent the designation packet because it was legally defensible, who had managed the situation with the methodical competence of someone who had learned, over eleven years, to keep the machinery running without letting it touch him.

The man at the far right of the dais was not that version, or not only that version. His face was methodical, yes, the same careful, unhurried quality she had seen in the conference room. But there was something else in it now. Not hostility. Not agreement. Something more interior than either: the face of a man carrying a decision he had already made, waiting with the patience of someone who had thought it through completely for the moment when the decision would become an action.

She did not know what he had decided.

That was the thing she could not get past, standing at the witness table with her hands on its surface and the room still holding that different silence. She had spent eleven months learning to read the situation, the permit, the designation packet, the suspension, the thirty-day window, the hearing itself, as a series of moves in a system she understood. She knew where Voss stood. She knew where Marcus stood. She knew where the panel's procedural interests lay and where the state's legal exposure was and what the OFA would require and what the radiocarbon dates could carry and what the oral history could carry if presented correctly.

She did not know where Slade stood.

He had signed the notice. He had authored the designation packet. He had sat on this panel for three hours and asked not one hostile question. He had just declined to ask any question at all, at the moment when the testimony was most vulnerable, when a single precise question about verification standards or evidentiary thresholds could have opened a gap that would take days to close.

He had not asked it.

Chair Hollins moved on. "Thank you, Mr. Slade." She looked down at her notes, then up at Tayak. "Dr. Moyaone, on behalf of the panel, I want to thank you for the thoroughness and care of your testimony today. The panel will enter the full text of your prepared remarks into the record, along with the supporting documentation submitted in advance." She paused. "Before we adjourn for the day, I want to note for the record that tomorrow's session will include testimony from Nora Moyaone, who will speak to the oral history chain as its current keeper. Panelists should have received the supplemental notice this morning."

Tayak gathered her folder. The photographs. The site number printout. The field assessment summary with its forty-three pages of careful, qualified language. She stacked them in the order she had brought them in, put them back in the folder, and closed it.

Behind her, the room was beginning to move: the soft percussion of chairs, voices returning to their normal registers, the camera operator at the back wall reaching for something on his equipment bag. Kimi would be in the third row. Marcus would be near the door.

She did not look at the panel again. But as she stepped back from the table, she was aware of Slade at the far right of the dais, still sitting, still with his hands flat on the table, still with his notepad closed, watching the room with the patient, interior expression of a man who was waiting for tomorrow.

The chair's voice reached her from the dais as she was closing the folder's clasp.

"The panel will recess at this time. Written deliberation will follow. The commission's decision will be issued within ten business days of the close of testimony."

Ten business days. Tayak did the arithmetic before Hollins had finished the sentence. The thirty-day clock had eleven days left. Ten business days was inside it, but only just, the way a trowel blade fits inside a feature boundary when the feature is smaller than you expected and you are working with the handle nearly vertical, the angle that gives you control but no margin.

She picked up the folder.

Around her the room came apart in the way rooms do at the end of formal things, not all at once but in layers, the outermost first. The camera operator at the back wall was already capping a lens. The staff member with the laptop was unplugging something. In the rows of padded metal chairs, people were standing, retrieving coats from chair backs, speaking in the cautious, half-lowered voices people use when they are not yet sure the formal part is entirely finished. The fluorescent panels above cast the same flat, humming light they had cast for three hours, indifferent to the difference between testimony and its absence.

She had the folder. She had the photographs inside it, and the site number printout, and the forty-three-page assessment. She had given them everything the panel's standards required and several things they had not thought to require. She did not know if it was enough.

She moved toward the aisle.

Kimi reached her before she cleared the gallery rail, not running, but moving with the particular economy of someone who has been watching for the exact moment. She didn't say anything. She put her hand on Tayak's arm just above the elbow and squeezed once, hard, then released it. Her eyes were dry and her face was composed and that was, Tayak understood, the most honest thing anyone had offered her all day.

"Ten days," Tayak said.

"I know."

"Inside the window."

"Barely."

"Barely is enough."

Kimi looked at her the way she sometimes looked at things she was not certain she believed but was choosing not to argue with. Then she stepped back to let the people behind Tayak through.

Marcus was near the door, as she had known he would be. He had his coat already on and his phone in his hand and the expression he wore when he was managing three things at once and not letting any of them show. He gave her a single nod, not congratulatory, not cautious, something between the two, the nod of a man who understood that the work was not finished and was not going to pretend otherwise.

She nodded back.

She was looking for Nora.

The gallery had been full when the session opened. She had not let herself turn around to look during the testimony, but she had been aware of it, the weight of the room at her back, the particular quality of attention that a full room has when it is listening to something it has waited a long time to hear. Now it was thinning. She could see the rows of chairs, most of them empty or emptying, and the door at the far end propped open to the corridor, and the November light coming through the corridor windows, pale and flat.

Nora was coming through the gallery door slowly, which was how she moved now, not frail, exactly, but deliberate, with the deliberateness of someone who has decided that each step should be worth taking. She was wearing her good coat, the charcoal wool one she kept for occasions that required it, and she had her hands in front of her, not in the pockets but held loosely together, the way she held them when she was thinking about something and did not want to be interrupted.

Tayak waited.

The room continued to empty around her. Someone bumped her shoulder and apologized. She did not look away from Nora.

When Nora reached her, she stopped and looked at Tayak for a moment without speaking. Her eyes moved over Tayak's face in the way they had moved over things for as long as Tayak could remember, not searching, exactly, but taking inventory. Then she said, quietly, as if she were noting a fact she had wanted to confirm:

"You said the names correctly."

Tayak looked at her.

"All of them," Nora said. "In the right order."

The fluorescent light hummed. Someone's chair leg scraped the floor across the room.

Tayak said, "I don't know when I learned them."

Nora's expression did not change, but something in it settled, the way ground settles when it has been waiting for weight and the weight finally comes. "You've known them a long time," she said. "You just didn't know you knew them."

Tayak thought about the drive to the site at four-thirty in the mornings, the radio off, the dark fields on either side of Route 6, the way her mind would sometimes move through the names the way it moved through a stratigraphic sequence, not reciting, exactly, just holding them in order, checking the logic of the chain. She had not thought of it as memorization. She had thought of it as thinking.

"Come on," Kimi said, from somewhere behind her left shoulder. "It's cold in the corridor and it's going to be colder outside.", -

The building's front steps were broad and shallow, the kind of steps that civic buildings build to signal importance without quite achieving grandeur. The November air hit her when the door opened, not brutal, but definite, the kind of cold that has made up its mind. The sky above Annapolis was the color of old pewter, the clouds moving in from the bay with the slow, committed pace of weather that intends to stay.

She stopped at the top of the steps.

Behind her she could hear Kimi and Marcus and, further back, the slower sound of Nora's footsteps. Below her, on the sidewalk, a few people from the gallery were already moving toward their cars, collars up, breath visible. A woman in a red coat was on her phone. A man she didn't recognize was talking to someone from the panel staff, both of them looking at a document.

The hearing was over.

She had known, going in, that the hearing was not the decision, that the panel would deliberate, that the written ruling would come in its own time, that the process had its own pace and she could not hurry it. She had known this the way you know things you have told yourself carefully and repeatedly. Knowing it had not made the three hours any easier. And knowing it now, standing at the top of the civic steps with the cold coming off the bay and the folder under her arm, did not make the outcome feel any closer.

Ten business days.

She thought about the site. The trench at C-4, the east transect, the way the soil changed color below the third stratum, that darkening she had documented and dated and described in forty-three pages of careful, qualified language that she had stood at a witness table and delivered to seven panelists who had listened with the attentiveness of people who understood, at least partially, what they were hearing. She thought about the copper pendant in the conservation suite, properly catalogued and housed. She thought about the radiocarbon dates and the XRF results and the 1697 petition in the Maryland State Archives with Saya's name in the clerk's hand.

She thought about Slade.

He had not asked a question. In three hours of testimony, across two sections of scientific evidence and one of oral history, he had not asked a single hostile question, and at the end, when Hollins had turned to him for the final round, he had passed. She had sat at the witness table and watched him decline, and she had not known what to do with it. She still didn't.

She had spent eleven months building a picture of the opposition, its legal instruments, its procedural leverage, its institutional interests. She had built the picture carefully, the way you build a stratigraphic profile: layer by layer, each observation separated from each inference by a ruled line. The picture had Slade in it as a clear figure. The man who had signed the notice. The man who had authored the designation packet. The man who had managed the situation with the methodical competence of someone who had learned to keep the machinery running.

That picture was not wrong. But it was incomplete in a way she could not yet map.

His silence had not been neutrality. She was certain of that, as certain as she was of anything she had not yet confirmed with data. Neutrality was the absence of position. What she had seen in his face during the testimony, and in the particular stillness of his hands on the table when she described the chain of names, was not the absence of position. It was a position held interior, out of the room's sight, waiting for something she could not identify.

A door she could not see the other side of.

Kimi came to stand beside her at the top of the steps. She had her coat buttoned to the collar and her hair pulled back and she was looking at the street below with the focused, slightly distant expression she wore when she was deciding something.

"Dana Osei texted again," she said. "Washington Post."

"I know."

"She's been patient."

"She has."

"Tayak."

"Not yet." She said it without sharpness. "Ten days. Let the panel issue the ruling. Then we talk to the Post."

Kimi was quiet for a moment. Then: "Okay."

Behind them, the door opened and Nora came through it, and Marcus behind her, and the cold air moved between all of them and the pewter sky pressed down on the bay and the city and the broad, shallow steps. Tayak stood at the top of them with the folder under her arm and the names in the right order in the part of her mind that had been holding them, apparently, for longer than she knew.

She started down the steps.

The decision would come in writing, in ten business days, inside the thirty-day window but only just. The panel would deliberate in a room she would not be in, with documents she had submitted and testimony she had given and the chain of names entered into the official record of the state of Maryland for the first time in its two-thousand-year life. She had done what she could do. The ground had given her what it had to give. The rest was not hers to control.

She reached the sidewalk and turned toward the parking garage and did not look back at the building.

But she thought about Slade, his hands flat on the table, his notepad closed, his face holding its interior decision with the patience of a man who had already thought it through. She thought about the door she could not see the other side of, and she thought: tomorrow Nora testifies. Whatever Slade was carrying, he would be sitting at that dais when Nora stood at the witness table and spoke the chain of names from the beginning, not in summary, not in a field assessment's careful qualified language, but in full, the way they had been carried: person to person, mouth to ear, across two thousand years of ground that had never stopped being theirs.

Whatever the door was, it would still be there tomorrow.

She walked toward the parking garage with Kimi at her side and the November cold settling into her coat and the folder pressed against her ribs. Ten days is enough, she thought. Barely is enough. She thought it the way you think things you are choosing to believe because the alternative is to stop moving, and stopping was not something she had learned how to do.
