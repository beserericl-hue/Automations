The parking structure smelled of exhaust and wet concrete, and the fluorescent tube above bay 4C had been flickering since September. Tayak sat in the truck and let the engine tick down.

She had driven forty minutes from La Plata without the radio on.

The NSF listing was folded in her left breast pocket, the paper still warm from the printer at the copy shop on Route 301. She had printed it at 6:48 a.m. because she did not trust herself to walk into Voss's office holding only a phone screen. Paper was harder to dismiss. Paper was harder to deny you had seen.

She did not take it out. She knew what it said.

*Site 18CH-447 — Piscataway burial context, Charles County, Maryland. Principal Investigator: Elliot Voss, Ph.D., Department of Anthropology, University of Maryland. Co-Investigator: T. Moyaone.*

Co-investigator. Her name, her site, her permit, her ground — and she was listed second.

The filing timestamp was 4:17 p.m. on the day the document had come up from the mound. She had still been in the trench at 4:17. Her hands had still been in the earth.

She picked up her phone. One message from Voss — not eleven days of silence, but one: the voicemail he had left at 8:43 in the morning, three days ago, which she had listened to twice and not returned. *Tayak, it's Elliot. I want you to know I've withdrawn the NSF submission — the listing has been pulled. I'd like to discuss a formal institutional support letter for the petition. Call me when you can.* She had listened to it twice and said nothing back, because she needed to see his face when he said it, and because a withdrawal you announce by voicemail before the person has said a word to you is not a withdrawal born of conscience.

It is a withdrawal born of calculation.

Wren had texted at 7:02 a.m.: *Stratigraphy log updated through Layer 3b. Cross-section measurements confirmed. Let me know if you want me to run the density calculations before Friday.* Wren, who was careful and patient and had no idea what was happening above the trench.

The third notification was from Nora. Sent at 5:49 a.m., which meant Nora had been awake before the light. Tayak had not opened it. She did not open it now. Whatever Nora had to say at 5:49 in the morning was something Tayak was not ready to carry into this building.

She put the phone in her jacket pocket, on top of the folded paper.

Outside, the campus was doing what it always did in November: students moving in clusters against the cold, hoods up, coffee cups in hand, the brick paths darkened with overnight rain. The anthropology building was across the quad. She had been to Voss's office four times in three years — twice for grant meetings, once for a departmental review, once to return a monograph he had lent her on Chesapeake trade networks. She had liked him then. She had thought he was one of the careful ones.

She was hoping, still, that she was wrong. That was the honest thing to admit, sitting in a parking structure at seven in the morning with her name filed under someone else's. She was hoping Voss would have an explanation that did not mean what the listing said it meant. That he had filed some kind of protective hold — a bureaucratic misunderstanding, a form that required a named institutional PI and he had used his own without thinking through the implications, intending to call her, meaning to call her, the voicemail being the beginning of that conversation.

He had withdrawn the listing. He had said so. But she had been at the trench at 4:17 and he had filed at 4:17, and a man who files at 4:17 and withdraws three days later when the press attention keeps building is not a man who made a bureaucratic error. He is a man who tested the water and found it hotter than he expected.

She got out of the truck.

The concrete was cold under her boots. She locked the door without thinking about it, the sound sharp in the low ceiling of the structure. The fluorescent tube above 4C threw its light in a slow, seasick pulse — on, almost off, on again — and she walked past it toward the stairwell, the folded paper in her pocket, the unread message from Nora pressing against it like a second heartbeat.

She did not know yet what she was going to say. She knew she was going in.

The anthropology building smelled of old coffee and radiator heat, the way it always did in November. Tayak took the stairs. She did not want to stand in an elevator.

Voss's office was on the third floor, second door past the departmental administrator's desk. The administrator was not in yet. The corridor was empty. Tayak's boots were quiet on the linoleum, which was the kind of institutional grey-green that had probably been there since 1974 and would be there in 2074. She did not knock.

Voss was at his desk.

He was reading something on his laptop, a coffee mug at his elbow — university logo, dark blue, the handle repaired with a thin line of dried epoxy. He looked up when she came in and his face did exactly what she had been afraid it would do: it settled. Not surprise. Readiness. He had been expecting her.

"Tayak." He said it the way you say a name you've been rehearsing.

She crossed the room and set the printed NSF listing on his desk. Face up. She did not speak.

He looked at it. He did not touch it. The paper was still warm from her jacket pocket and it lay between them on the birch-veneer surface like something that had been a long time coming. His desk was the kind of organized that meant he had tidied it recently — ring binders lined along the shelf behind him, labeled by project acronym in his own hand, a stack of pages squared at the corner, a single framed photograph of a field site she didn't recognize. No clutter. A man who had prepared his space.

He read the listing. She watched his eyes move across it, though there was nothing on it he did not already know.

Then he sat back.

"I withdrew it," he said.

"Three days ago."

"Yes."

"After Kimi's video had two million views and the Washington Post had run the story twice."

He held her gaze. "The timing was what it was."

"What was it?"

He picked up the mug, set it down without drinking. "Sit down, Tayak."

"No."

He looked at her for a moment, then nodded, as if that were also something he had prepared for. He was sixty-two years old, with the kind of face that had been outdoors enough to look honest, and she had trusted it. That was the thing she kept coming back to. She had trusted it.

"I'm not going to pretend I didn't file it," he said.

"Good."

"But I need you to understand the context—"

"Don't use that word."

He stopped. Outside his window, rain had started again — a thin, cold November rain that darkened the brick paths and drove the students under their hoods. A gull cried somewhere above the building, which was wrong for this far inland, and then it was gone.

"The site is exposed," Voss said. "Kimi's video — the media coverage, the Washington Post piece — it's put Site 18CH-447 in a position where any procedural gap becomes a liability. Slade's inquiry is already in motion. The Trust is watching. If the permit comes up for review in that environment and the institutional backing isn't—" He paused. "If the institutional home isn't bulletproof, they will use that. You know they will use that."

Tayak said nothing.

"I've been through this before," he said. "The Chesapeake trade network project, 2017 — the moment it became politically visible, the first thing the state looked at was whether the permit structure could hold. It couldn't, and we lost eight months." He opened his hands on the desk, a gesture that was meant to look like candor. "I filed the listing to give the project an institutional anchor that no one can challenge. That's all this is."

"You filed yourself as principal investigator."

"As a protective measure—"

"Of my site. My permit. My excavation."

"Our excavation." He said it quietly, the *our* placed with care. "Tayak, I've been involved with this project since the grant application. My name is on the methodology review. I testified at the recognition hearings. I wrote the Chesapeake trade network monograph that half your stratigraphy citations are built on. I'm not an outsider here."

She had thought about this moment in the parking structure. She had run through what he might say and what she might say back, and she had been afraid she would lose her temper, which would give him something to manage. She had not lost her temper. She was not going to.

"You filed at 4:17 p.m.," she said. "On the day the document came up from the mound. I was still in the trench."

He did not answer that.

"You didn't call me first. You didn't email me. You left a voicemail three days later, after the press attention had made the filing a liability, offering to withdraw and write a support letter." She kept her voice level. "That's not protection. That's positioning. And the withdrawal is the same thing."

"It's both." He said it without flinching, which was the first honest thing he had said. "I won't pretend those things are entirely separate. But the project needed cover, and I gave it cover, and the alternative was watching Slade find a procedural gap and use it to shut the site down before you reached the third stratum. You're at what — Layer 3b? You haven't found what you're looking for yet."

The rain tapped against the window. Somewhere down the corridor, the coffee machine in the break room began its cycle — a low, mechanical hiss, the smell of it reaching even here.

"I'm going to file a formal complaint with the university research office," Tayak said. "About the original filing. The withdrawal doesn't change what the filing was."

Something shifted in his face. Not guilt. Calculation.

"I'd ask you to think carefully about that," he said.

"I have."

"A formal complaint triggers a review." He picked up the mug again, turned it in his hands. "A review of the project means a review of the permit. Your permit for 18CH-447 was issued on the basis of your university affiliation — that's in the filing language. If the research office opens a case, the permit question comes with it. Slade will hear about it. The Trust will hear about it." He set the mug down. "You have what — thirty-five days before Wills's construction schedule begins? A permit review takes sixty, minimum."

She had known this. She had known it in the parking structure, had turned it over in the truck on Route 301, had held it against the unread message from Nora all the way across the quad. Knowing it did not make it easier to stand in this room and hear him say it.

"I'm also in a position to offer you something," Voss said. "Formally. The support letter I mentioned — that's real. A letter from my department, on university letterhead, explicitly endorsing the Piscataway federal recognition petition and affirming the excavation's methodological integrity. That goes to the Bureau of Indian Affairs and to the Trust. It gives the petition a peer-reviewed institutional anchor." He leaned forward slightly. "And co-investigator status on any future NSF submission arising from this site — full access to the project record, co-authorship on publications. That's not nothing, Tayak. In terms of your career, in terms of the recognition petition, having this project anchored at Maryland with my name attached gives it a credibility that—"

"My name."

"What?"

"My name gives it credibility." She picked up the printed listing from his desk. "My name. My permit. My grandmother's ground."

He looked at her.

"You want me to be co-investigator on my own excavation," she said. "You want me to thank you for the withdrawal and the support letter and sign on below your name and keep digging while you manage the institutional relationship with the Trust and decide what gets published and when and how it gets framed." She folded the paper once, along the original crease. "And if I don't, you'll let the permit review happen and the site will be closed before I reach the fourth stratum."

Voss said nothing. He had the look of a man who had told himself a story about his own motives for long enough that he almost believed it, and who was now watching someone refuse to believe it with him.

"I need you to understand," he said finally, "that I have spent twenty years building relationships with the Trust, with the state archaeology office, with the federal compliance structure. I am not your enemy. I am trying to keep this project alive."

"I know," Tayak said.

He seemed to take that as something. He leaned forward slightly.

"Then let's talk about how we move forward—"

"I said I know what you're doing." She put the folded paper back in her pocket. "I didn't say I was going to let you."

She turned and walked to the door.

"Tayak." His voice had something in it now that wasn't calculation. Something older. "If you file the complaint, I can't protect the permit. I want you to understand that. I'm not threatening you — I'm telling you how the process works. Once it's in the research office's hands, it's out of mine."

She stopped with her hand on the door frame. The corridor was still empty. The coffee machine had finished its cycle. Somewhere outside, the gull cried again — closer now, or maybe a different one.

She did not turn around.

"I know how the process works," she said.

She walked out. She left without answering his offer. She left without telling him whether she would file, and not telling him was its own kind of answer, and she carried it down the corridor and past the administrator's empty desk and down the stairs and out into the rain, which was cold and thin and fell without mercy on the darkened brick, and she put her hand in her pocket and felt the folded paper and, beneath it, her phone, with Nora's message still unopened, sent at 5:49 in the morning, before the light.

She looked at him. He had the face of a man who believed what he was saying. That was the hardest part. He was not performing sincerity. He had arrived at a position where protecting his own interests and protecting the project had become, in his mind, the same thing, and he had lived there long enough that the seam had disappeared.

She had a mentor at Howard — Cecile Marant, who had taught her to listen with her hands, to let the ground speak before deciding what it was saying — who had told her once that the most dangerous people in any institution were not the ones who wanted to harm you. They were the ones who wanted to help you in ways that required you to become smaller.

She stood up.

Something moved in his face. Not surprise. He had been ready for this. But underneath the readiness, something older — a man who had told himself for twenty years that he was one of the good ones, watching that story become complicated.

"Tayak." He said her name the way people say a name when they want to stop a door from closing. "If you file, I cannot protect the permit. I want to be clear about that. I'm not withholding protection as leverage. I am telling you that the process, once started, is not in my hands."

She pulled into the field office lot and sat for a moment with the engine off, rain ticking on the roof. The lot was gravel, badly drained, and her boots would be wet before she reached the door. She got out anyway.

The field kit was padlocked in the truck bed under the tonneau cover. She worked the combination in the cold — her fingers were stiff, and she had to start twice — and lifted the lid and found the permit folder beneath the site log and the spare trowel roll, exactly where she had put it in September when the permit came through and she had read it carefully and then filed it and gone back to the trench.

She had not read section four, paragraph three.

Inside, she sat at the folding table with the permit flat in front of her and a mug of coffee she had made from the camp kettle, the coffee already going cold. The field office was a converted equipment shed, twelve feet by eighteen, with a propane heater that cycled on and off and left the room smelling faintly of gas. The stratigraphy profile Wren had drawn was pinned to the board on the east wall — the careful hatching of oxidized lenses, the single confident line distinguishing the mound's constructed core from the surrounding matrix. She did not look at it. She read.

*Section 4. Institutional Affiliation and Permit Validity.*

*Paragraph 3. This permit is issued in part on the basis of the Principal Investigator's current institutional affiliation with the Department of Anthropology, University of Maryland. Any material change in that affiliation, or any formal review initiated by the University's research compliance office that places the PI's institutional standing under administrative hold, shall constitute grounds for a mandatory compliance review of this permit by the issuing authority. During such review, all fieldwork at the permitted site shall be suspended pending resolution.*

She read it twice. Then she set the permit down and picked up the mug and drank the cold coffee.

He had not bluffed.

She sat with that for a moment. The propane heater clicked on and the room filled with its low hiss. Outside, the rain had eased to a drizzle she could hear on the metal roof only when she listened for it.

She pulled the NSF listing from her jacket pocket — she had taken it from his desk without asking, had simply picked it up and folded it and put it in her pocket, and he had watched her do it and said nothing — and unfolded it on the table beside the permit. The listing was two pages, printed on university paper, the header reading *National Science Foundation — Award Notice — Collaborative Research: Archaeological Contexts of Chesapeake Trade Networks, Phase II.* Voss's name was first. Her name was not on it.

She had built the site access. She had written the field methodology. She had spent eleven weeks in a tent in Charles County in the rain.

Her name was not on it.

She folded the NSF listing and put it back in her pocket. She did not need to look at it again. She knew what it said. She knew what it had said even after the withdrawal — the withdrawal changed the listing's status, not its meaning. A man who files at 4:17 and withdraws three days later has still told you what he reached for when the moment came.

She thought about what she had, against what he had. She had the field record — every log entry, every photograph, every stratigraphy note, the 1697 petition in the conservation suite at Hurst Hall, the cobalt bead in its archival tray, Wren's profile on the wall. She had forty-three pages of field assessment on university letterhead, authored by her, signed by her, submitted to Slade's office under her name. She had the oral tradition that Nora had been carrying since 1962, which no court had ever seen and no permit listed, and which was not yet in the record.

Against that, Voss had section four, paragraph three, and sixty days minimum, and thirty-four days was less than sixty.

She looked at Wren's stratigraphy profile on the wall. The fourth stratum was there, below the line — unexcavated, undocumented, holding whatever it held. The pendant at stratum five, the gorget at three, the pipe bowl at two, the bead at one. Each layer a century deeper. Each layer a name in Nora's chain.

She did not know what was in the fourth stratum. She did not know what Nora had been holding that she was now ready to show.

She picked up her phone.

Nora's message was still open on the screen. *Come when you can. I have something to show you.*

Tayak looked at the permit. She looked at the NSF listing in her pocket. She looked at the stratigraphy profile on the wall.

She thought about what Cecile Marant had taught her in the field — not in a hallway, not in a seminar, but standing at a trench wall in the Patuxent lowlands in August heat, the two of them looking at a soil change that might be a feature or might be nothing. *Stand still,* Cecile had said. *Don't decide what you're looking at until you've looked long enough to be wrong twice.* She had stood there for what felt like a long time, and then she had seen it — not a feature, but a root channel, and below the root channel, a real feature, a pit that the root had grown into and obscured. If she had moved too fast she would have taken the root channel for the thing itself and missed what was underneath.

She was looking at a root channel.

Voss was the root channel — real, obstructive, growing into the thing she was trying to see. But he was not the thing itself. The thing itself was below him, below the compliance language and the institutional maneuvering and the sixty-day hold, and it had been there for two thousand years and it would still be there if she did not make a mistake in the next thirty-four days.

She did not know yet whether she could file and still reach the fourth stratum in time. She did not know yet whether Nora's *something to show* changed the calculation. She needed to know both things before she decided anything.

She put on her jacket. She locked the field kit. She locked the field office door.

She got in the truck and drove north.

The road back through Charles County was the same road she had driven south an hour ago — Route 301, the corridor Garrett Wills's construction schedule was already eating from the north end, the same stripped trees and low grey sky and wet asphalt — but she drove it differently now, faster, with the heater running and her phone on the seat and Nora's message on the screen.

*Come when you can. I have something to show you.*

Nora was eighty-two years old. She had been carrying the chain since 1962, when Ida had sat her down at the kitchen table on Billingsley Road and traced the mound's concentric chambers in the flour-sack cloth with one finger, choosing her words for the occasion, for the distance they would have to travel. Sixty-two years. And she had never once said *I have something to show you.*

Whatever it was, it had waited long enough.

Tayak pressed the accelerator and the truck moved north through the wet grey morning, and behind her the mound sat in Dawes's field under the low cloud, undisturbed, patient, holding the fourth stratum the way it had held everything else — without urgency, without deadline, in the dark.
