The soil in the trench was the color of old rust where Tayak had been working since first light, and she was on her knees when she heard Dawes's truck slow on the field road.

She didn't look up. The trowel found a density change at twelve centimeters — packed clay against looser fill — and she wanted to mark it before the light shifted. She pressed her thumbnail into the soil at the boundary, reached for her notebook, wrote the depth and the texture and the time: *08:41. Clay lens, possible feature edge. Confirm with probe.*

The truck stopped. A door opened and closed. Then another.

She looked up.

Dawes was walking the fence line toward the equipment shed, which meant he'd given someone a ride from the road. The someone was a man Tayak didn't recognize at first — dark jacket, collared shirt, manila envelope pressed flat against his side — and then the mud on his leather shoes caught the light and she placed him. Not Garrett Slade. Someone from the Governor's Office, or sent by it; she would know when she saw the envelope.

She set the trowel down.

She had known this was coming. She had known it since Kimi's press conference, since the phone rang twice the previous afternoon with a blocked number she had not answered. She had known it the way you know a storm is coming when the light goes the wrong color — not surprise, just the body registering what the mind has not yet finished thinking.

She climbed out of the trench.

The grass was wet with dew still, this early, and her knee pads left dark prints where she knelt on the bank to push herself up. She pulled off her right glove and waited.

The man stopped two feet from the trench edge. He looked down at the clay lens she'd been marking, then back at her, and his face had the particular blankness of a man who has decided what he is going to say and is not going to let anything change it.

"Dr. Moyaone."

"Yes."

He held out the envelope. "I was asked to deliver this in person. From the Maryland Governor's Office of Historic Preservation Coordination."

She took it. The envelope was heavier than it looked — stiff cardboard inside, the kind used for documents someone wants to arrive uncreased. She had not known that office existed until this moment, which told her something.

"Is there anything in here that requires my signature?"

"No."

"Then I'll read it."

She opened the envelope. Inside was a two-page letter on state letterhead, the seal embossed at the top in the way that means the document has already been through at least three offices. She read the first paragraph standing up, then the second, and then she stepped back and sat on the trench edge with her feet dangling into the cut and read the whole thing again from the beginning.

The language was careful. The language was always careful. The state had determined, following a review of existing archaeological reports, historical survey records, and documentary evidence pertaining to Site 18CH-447 in Charles County, that the site met the criteria for designation as a *colonial-era historical landmark* under the Maryland Historical Trust Act. The review had identified significant association with seventeenth-century colonial settlement patterns in the lower Potomac region. The designation process was being expedited under the Governor's Cultural Heritage Initiative. The state proposed to assume protective management of the site pending formal designation. Any party wishing to submit counter-evidence or request a modification of the proposed designation had thirty days from the date of the letter to do so in writing to the Maryland Historical Trust.

The date on the letter was yesterday.

Twenty-nine days, then.

She read the phrase *colonial-era historical landmark* a third time. It was precise in the way that weapons are precise — not because it described the truth of the site but because it described a different truth, one that had been chosen for what it would do. A colonial-era landmark. Not a burial mound. Not a site of continuous indigenous occupation. Not a place where her ancestors had been putting their dead into the ground for two thousand years. A colonial-era landmark, which meant the mound's significance began with the English, which meant the land-based argument for the recognition petition — the argument that the Piscataway people had never left this ground, had never abandoned it, had maintained their connection to it through every generation of erasure — that argument would be severed at the root.

The morning smelled of wet clay and cut grass and the faint diesel trace from Dawes's truck. A crow called once from the tree line and went quiet.

"The designation process," Tayak said. She kept her voice level. "The review that identified the colonial association — what documents did the state rely on?"

The man had been watching her read. "The letter references the archaeological survey reports on file with the Trust."

"Those are my reports."

"They're filed with the Trust under the site number."

"I wrote them. They document multi-component occupation going back at minimum eighteen hundred years. That's not a colonial-era site. That's not even close to a colonial-era site."

"The Trust's determination of significance is based on the totality of the record."

She looked at him. He was doing his job — she could see that plainly. He had driven out here in his office shoes because someone had decided the letter needed a hand on it, and he had put his hand on it, and now he was standing in her field site with mud on his toes waiting to be released.

"The totality of the record," she said, "includes a 1697 petition filed in the Provincial Court by a named Piscataway individual asserting prior land rights to this ground. It includes a colonial land patent whose boundary description references the Piscataway settlement at this high ground. It includes stratified burials that predate English contact by more than a millennium."

"You have thirty days to submit that documentation to the Trust."

"I'm aware of what thirty days means."

He nodded, once. He looked like a man who had more to say and had decided not to say it.

She folded the letter back into the envelope. The cardboard was already soft at the crease where she'd bent it reading. She pressed it flat against her thigh, smoothing it, and looked out at the mound.

From here she could see the full profile — the slight rise above the river terrace, the way the ground lifted just enough to catch the morning light differently than the field around it. Two thousand years of intention, of people choosing this place, returning to it, adding to it, placing their dead here because the dead deserved to be somewhere that would hold them. The state wanted to call that a colonial-era landmark. The state wanted to manage it.

Wren was working the east transect twenty meters away, head down, not looking over. Elspeth had the Total Station set up at the north marker. They had both gone very still in the way that field people go still when something is happening that they are not supposed to have heard.

"I'll need a copy of the review documentation the Trust relied on," Tayak said. "The full record, not just the summary in the letter. I'm entitled to that under the comment process."

"You can request it from the Trust's public records office."

"I'll do that today."

He picked up his envelope from where she'd set it on the trench bank — she hadn't realized she'd put it down, had already stopped thinking of it as his — and then he registered that it was her copy and set it back.

"Dr. Moyaone." He stopped. Started again. "The thirty days is real. I'd use it."

She looked at him. There was something in his face she couldn't read — not sympathy exactly, not apology. Something more like a man standing at the edge of what he was willing to say.

"I intend to," she said.

He nodded and walked back toward the road. She watched him go, watched the mud on his shoes, watched him pull out his phone before he'd cleared the fence line.

Then she turned and looked at the mound and felt the thirty days begin.

The messenger's car was still audible on the field road — the slow crunch of gravel under tires, unhurried — when Tayak unfolded the letter again.

She had already read it twice. She read it a third time now, standing at the trench bank with the morning light coming flat across the field, because she needed to be certain she had understood correctly.

*The period of significance has been determined to be 1687–1750, corresponding to the documented occupation of the Friendship patent and the subsequent Sorrell partition.*

1687. The Calvert grant.

1712. The Sorrell partition.

She stood very still.

Those were Wren's documents. Those were the documents Wren had pulled from the county deed books, the ones with the boundary language referencing the Piscataway settlement at the high ground, the ones Wren had certified and sent to Elena Slade — Elena Slade, who was an attorney in Albuquerque, who had told Wren she was building a case for the petition, who was also Garrett Slade's daughter.

Tayak had known that. She had known about Elena and had not yet decided what it meant. She had filed it under *unresolved* and kept working, because the work was what she could control.

She understood now what it meant.

The documents that named the Piscataway settlement — named it, in a colonial land patent, as the boundary marker for English property — those documents had been handed to the state. Not to help the petition. To establish the colonial-era association. To give the Trust its period of significance. To give the designation its anchor.

The evidence that proved indigenous presence had been used to prove colonial significance.

She folded the letter. The paper had gone soft at the crease from handling. She pressed it flat against her palm and held it there.

Twenty meters away, Wren was working the east transect, head down, trowel moving in the slow, careful arc that meant she was in good soil, reading the ground. She had not looked up since the messenger left. Her shoulders were set in a way that was not quite normal — a degree of stillness that wasn't concentration, that was something closer to waiting.

Tayak looked at the mound.

The morning light was doing what it always did at this hour, coming in low from the southeast and catching the slight rise of the earthwork against the flatter field around it. From the trench bank you could see the full profile: the way the ground lifted, gradual and deliberate, above the river terrace. Not dramatic. Not a monument in any sense a tourist would recognize. Just the earth, raised by intention, by repeated returning, by the weight of people choosing this place over and over across two thousand years.

The state wanted to call it a colonial-era landmark.

The state wanted to say its significance began in 1687, when Thomas Calvert received a patent for land his family had not previously occupied, and described the high ground in his boundary language because the Piscataway settlement was already there, already visible, already old enough to be a landmark that an English surveyor would use as a reference point.

That was the document. That was the proof of presence. And the state had taken it and turned it ninety degrees and said: *here is where the significance begins*.

She became aware that her jaw was clenched. She released it.

She walked to the trench edge — not her main trench, the exploratory cut she'd opened on the south face last week, the one that had given her the charcoal lens at sixty centimeters — and crouched down and looked at the soil profile. The wall of the cut was clean. She had done that herself, shaving it back with a flat trowel until the stratigraphy showed clearly: the dark organic band at the top, then the mottled clay of the colonial-period disturbance, then below that the undisturbed pre-contact fill, the packed earth that had been accumulating since before anyone named Calvert had set foot in Maryland. The color change was visible even in the flat morning light. Brown to orange-tan to the darker, denser material below. Each layer a different century. Each layer a different world.

*The period of significance has been determined to be 1687–1750.*

They had started the clock at the wrong layer.

She pulled out her field notebook — spiral-bound, the cover warped from three weeks of morning dew — and wrote the date and time at the top of a clean page. Then she wrote: *Designation letter received. Period of significance stated as 1687–1750. Basis: Calvert patent (1687) and Sorrell partition (1712). Review documentation requested from Trust public records office. Thirty-day comment window begins today.*

She wrote it in the same hand she used for everything in the field. Sequential, factual, no skipped lines. Carver had taught her that: the notebook was not for feelings, it was for the record, and the record would outlast the feelings.

She closed the notebook and looked at the ground.

Below her, at sixty centimeters, was the charcoal lens. Below that, the intact pre-contact stratigraphy. Below that, the copper pendant layer, now documented and transferred to conservation. Below that, whatever came next — the layer she had not yet opened, the layer that Wren was working toward from the east transect, the layer that might be the oldest deposit in the mound.

All of it, every centimeter of it, predated 1687 by centuries.

She had thirty days to prove that to a body that had already decided what it was looking at.

She stood up. Her knees popped — she had been crouching longer than she'd realized. The wet-clay smell of the cut earth rose around her, familiar and specific: not just dirt but this dirt, this particular mix of organic matter and compressed clay that smelled different from any other site she'd worked, that she had learned to read the way you learn to read a face.

She pulled out her phone.

Marcus first. Then Nora. Then she would call the Trust's public records office and request the full review documentation, and she would do it today, before close of business, so that the timestamp was on record.

She dialed Marcus.

He picked up on the second ring.

"I know," he said.

She stopped. "You know."

"Elena called me an hour ago. She didn't know what her father was going to do with it." A pause. "She says she didn't know."

Tayak looked at the mound. A crow had landed on the crest — she hadn't seen it arrive — and was walking along the top of the earthwork with the proprietary air of a bird that had been doing this longer than anyone had been watching.

"The documents she used to build the colonial association," Tayak said. "The Calvert patent and the Sorrell partition. Those are the documents Wren sent her."

"Yes."

"She sent them to help the petition."

"I know."

"And they used them to bury it."

Marcus was quiet for a moment. When he spoke, his voice was careful in the way it got when he was choosing words that would hold up later. "The Trust's determination isn't final. You have thirty days to submit counter-documentation. The pre-contact stratigraphy, the 1697 petition, the artifact sequence — all of it can go into the comment record."

"I know what goes into a comment record."

"Tayak."

"I'm not angry at you."

"I know that too."

She watched the crow walk the mound's crest. It stopped, tilted its head, looked at something in the grass she couldn't see from where she stood.

"The 1697 petition," she said. "Nora has something. She told me to come at seven tomorrow morning and bring my notebook."

"I know about Nora."

Of course he did. "Is it enough?"

"I don't know yet. Come to Thursday's meeting first. Bring everything you have." Another pause. "Tayak. Thirty days is real time. We can work with thirty days."

She thought about what the messenger had said. *I'd use it.* A man standing at the edge of what he was willing to say.

"I'll be there Thursday," she said.

She hung up and stood for a moment with the phone in her hand. The crow had gone. She hadn't seen it leave either.

She called Nora.

Four rings, five. Then the line clicked open and Nora's voice came through, unhurried, the way it always was — as if time moved differently for her, as if she had learned long ago not to spend it rushing.

"I heard," Nora said.

"From Marcus?"

"From Kimi. Twenty minutes ago." A breath. "Come at seven. Come hungry, I'll have something on the stove."

"Nora." Tayak stopped. She was looking at the mound, at the place where the crow had been. "The name you said you'd tell me in person. The woman at the bottom."

"Yes."

"Is she — " She didn't know how to finish the question.

"Come at seven," Nora said. "Bring your notebook and come hungry and I'll tell you everything I know. All of it. I should have told you years ago." A pause that felt deliberate, weighted. "The ground waited this long. It can wait until morning."

The line went quiet. Tayak lowered the phone.

She stood at the mound's edge for a long time. The field was loud with ordinary things — the wind moving through the tree line, a truck on the road a quarter mile south, Wren's trowel scraping against packed clay in the east transect, the sound of careful work continuing.

She crouched down and pressed her palm flat against the mound's southern face, against the soil that was cool and slightly damp from last night's dew, that smelled of clay and old organic matter and something she had no word for, something that was only this place.

The state had taken the evidence of presence and called it evidence of colonial significance.

She had thirty days and a notebook and a seven o'clock appointment with an eighty-two-year-old woman who had been keeping a name for sixty-two years.

She took her hand off the ground. She stood up. She walked back to the trench, picked up her trowel, and went back to work.

She had been in the trench for twenty minutes when her phone buzzed again — Marcus, calling back. She almost let it go to voicemail. Then she climbed out, stripped off her right glove, and answered.

"I need to move Thursday's meeting," Marcus said. "Tomorrow night. Seven o'clock."

She was walking toward the field road without thinking about it, the way you move when a conversation needs space around it. "Because of the thirty-day clock."

"Because Delia Swann called me forty minutes ago asking who authorized the document transfer to Elena Slade."

Tayak stopped at the edge of the road. The gravel was pale and dry, the only dry thing in sight — the fields on both sides were still dark with last night's rain, and the air smelled of wet soil and something faintly mineral, the way it did after a heavy dew burned off slowly. "What did you tell her?"

"I told her the council would discuss it tomorrow."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Marcus said. "It isn't."

She heard something in his voice — not evasion, exactly, but the sound of a man holding several things at once and not dropping any of them. She had heard it before, at the hearing in March, when he'd told her the council was behind her and she'd understood that *behind* could mean a lot of different distances.

"Harold's going to want the field log," she said.

"Harold's already asked for it."

"And Dorothy."

"Dorothy wants a meeting with you before the meeting. I told her tomorrow at five."

Tayak looked down the field road. Wren's truck was parked at the south end of the transect, a hundred yards out, and she could see Wren moving in the east trench — the top of her head, the slow, deliberate arc of her arm as she worked. Careful work. Good work. Work that had nothing to do with the decision Wren had made about the documents.

"Delia's question is a fair one," Tayak said.

Marcus was quiet for a beat. "Yes."

"The transfer wasn't authorized by the council. That's a fact. Whatever the intention was."

"I know."

She was not going to defend Wren on this call. Not because Wren didn't deserve defending — she did, or at least the intention did — but because the question Marcus was navigating wasn't about intention. It was about process, and process was the only thing standing between the tribe and the argument that they couldn't govern their own evidence. If she started explaining Wren's reasons, she was already conceding that the reasons were the point. They weren't. The process was the point.

"What counter-evidence does the petition actually have?" she said.

The silence that followed was three seconds long. She counted it.

"We have the artifact sequence," Marcus said finally. "The stratigraphy. The 1697 petition, if Nora's copy is what she says it is. The colonial deed records with the boundary language — which are now part of the Trust's own file, so we can cite them without the transfer question coming up."

"That's the colonial layer. What about pre-contact?"

Another pause, shorter. "The pendant. The gorget. The antler figure. The ceramic sequence."

"Which the Trust has just reframed as evidence of a colonial-era earthwork."

"Yes."

"So we have artifacts that prove presence going back two thousand years, and a state body that has decided to read them as colonial-period debris."

"That's the argument we have to break."

"Marcus." She turned away from the road and looked at the mound. From here she could see its full profile — the gentle rise of the southern face, the way it sat on the terrace above the creek bottom, the way it had always sat there, the way it had been sitting there before anyone in that Trust office had a surname. "The pendant alone dates to the third century at the latest. The antler figure is older. The ceramic sequence in stratum three goes back to the Woodland period. None of that is colonial."

"I know that."

"Does the Trust know that?"

"They know what's in your field report. The question is whether they're choosing to read it."

She pressed her thumbnail into the edge of her field notebook — an old habit, something to do with her hands when she couldn't use them for anything useful. The notebook's cover was stiff with dried mud along the bottom edge, and the spiral binding had a piece of flagging tape wrapped around it where one of the wires had bent out and kept catching on her glove.

"The designation language," she said. "'Colonial-era historical landmark.' That's specific. Someone chose that period of significance deliberately."

"Yes."

"Which means someone in that office read the field report and decided the pre-contact material wasn't sufficient to anchor the designation in the tribal period."

"Or they decided it was sufficient and designated it colonial anyway," Marcus said. His voice was flat when he said it. Not angry — something more tired than anger, the sound of a man who had been watching institutions make decisions like that for long enough that it no longer surprised him, only wore him down.

Tayak looked at the mound's crest. The crow was gone — she'd watched it leave from the trench, lifting off without urgency, banking south over the tree line. The grass along the top of the earthwork was still pressed flat where it had stood.

"The thirty days," she said. "What's the actual submission process? I need the Trust's comment record address, the format requirements, whether they'll accept supplemental archaeological documentation or only legal exhibits."

"I have the notice language. I'll send it tonight."

"I need it before five. I'm going to request the full review file from the public records office before close of business — I want my request timestamped today."

"Good." A pause. "Tayak. The pre-contact material is real. The stratigraphy is real. The problem isn't the evidence."

"I know what the problem is."

"The problem is that we need someone to say, on the record, what the evidence means. Someone the Trust can't dismiss as a party with an interest."

She understood what he was not quite saying. An outside voice. An institutional voice. A voice that didn't belong to the tribe, or to her, or to anyone who could be characterized as having a stake in the outcome. It was the oldest problem in the recognition process: the people with the most knowledge were always the people whose knowledge was most easily dismissed.

"Voss," she said.

"I wasn't going to say his name."

"But that's what you mean."

Marcus didn't answer, which was its own kind of answer.

"I'll think about it," she said. She wasn't going to think about it. She was going to find another way, and she had thirty days to find it, and she was going to start tonight. "The meeting tomorrow. Seven o'clock. I'll be there."

"Bring the field report. Bring the artifact log. Bring anything you have on the stratigraphy below stratum five."

"I know what to bring."

"I know you do." A brief pause — not awkward, just the pause of a man who had one more thing to say and was deciding whether to say it plainly or carefully. He chose plainly. "Delia's going to ask about Wren in the meeting. She's going to ask who made the decision and whether that person had authority to make it. You should be ready for that."

"I'll be ready."

"And Harold's going to ask whether the document transfer compromised the petition's evidentiary chain."

Tayak pressed the sole of her boot against the gravel, feeling the give of the wet soil underneath. Somewhere in the tree line a red-tailed hawk was working a thermal, circling without effort, its shadow crossing the field in slow arcs. She watched it for a moment.

"Marcus. The counter-evidence. If I strip out the colonial documents — if I set aside everything Wren sent to Elena and build the submission only from what we have in the ground — what do we actually have?"

He was quiet for three seconds. She counted those too.

"That's why you need to call Nora tonight," he said.

She looked at the mound.

"I already called her," she said. "Seven a.m."

"Good." His voice shifted slightly — something loosened in it, some held thing released. "Good. Then go back to work. I'll send the notice language before five."

She hung up. The hawk had moved south, out of sight. The field was quiet except for the sound of Wren's trowel in the east trench, that steady scraping rhythm that she had learned to read as a kind of reassurance — the sound that meant the work was still going, that the ground was still giving up what it held, one careful layer at a time.

She looked at her phone. Four-seventeen. The Trust's public records office closed at five. She pulled up the number, walked to the equipment shed for a signal, and made the call standing in the doorway with the smell of damp canvas and machine oil around her, the shed's corrugated roof ticking as it cooled in the afternoon air.

The clerk who answered had the voice of someone who had been answering the same questions all day and had decided to be pleasant about it anyway. Tayak gave her name, her institutional affiliation, the designation file number from the notice — she had written it in her notebook before she'd even read the full text, because Carver had taught her to write the file number first, always, before anything else, so there was never a question about what you were looking at.

"I'm requesting the complete review file for the 18CH-447 heritage designation determination," she said. "All documentation, correspondence, and supporting materials used in the designation decision. Under the Maryland Public Information Act."

A pause. Keys clicking. "That request will take up to thirty business days to fulfill."

"I understand. I need the request timestamped today."

More keys. "Can I get a mailing address?"

She gave the university address and her email. The clerk read it back, made a small correction, read it back again. Tayak confirmed it. The call took four minutes. When she hung up, the timestamp on her phone read 4:21 p.m.

She walked back to the trench. Wren looked up when she heard footsteps on the path — a quick look, reading Tayak's face the way she always did, then back to the work without asking anything. Tayak was grateful for that. She pulled on her glove, picked up her trowel, and crouched at the edge of the feature.

The soil here was darker than the surrounding matrix — a lens of compressed organic material, black-brown and faintly greasy between her fingers when she pressed it, the kind of deposit that meant repeated use over a long time, not a single event. She had been working this layer for three days. She had not yet decided what she was looking at.

*Do not decide what you are looking at until you have looked long enough to be wrong twice.*

She set the trowel against the soil and began.

She worked until the light failed.

Not because she wanted to stop then, but because Wren had quietly packed the documentation kit and said, "I can't read the context anymore," and Tayak had looked up and found the sky above the tree line the color of a bruise fading to yellow at its edges, and realized she had been working by feel for the last twenty minutes without knowing it.

She drove home with clay on her hands and the notice folded in her jacket pocket. She had read it four times. She did not need to read it again.

The apartment was cold. She had left a window cracked that morning and the October air had settled into the rooms like something that belonged there. She closed the window, ran the tap until it steamed, and washed her hands at the kitchen sink, watching the clay loosen and spiral down the drain — that particular red-brown of the subsoil at the site, which she had come to know the way you know a color that appears in your dreams.

She ate standing at the counter. She did not taste it.

At eight-seventeen she called Nora.

The phone rang twice. Then Nora's voice, unhurried, the way it always was — the voice of a woman who had decided long ago that urgency was a kind of noise, and noise was not the same as meaning.

"I wondered when you'd call," Nora said.

"Marcus told you."

"Marcus called at four. I've been waiting on you since five." A brief pause. "You were at the site."

"Yes."

"Good. That's where you should have been." Not approval exactly — more the acknowledgment that a thing had been done in the right order.

Tayak set her back against the kitchen counter and looked at the far wall. A postcard from Priya pinned above the radiator: a satellite image of the Chesapeake, the bay a dark blue tongue running north between the peninsulas. She had looked at it so many times it had stopped being a picture and become wallpaper. She looked at it now and saw it again — the water, the land, the thin lines of the rivers feeding in from the west.

"The designation," she said. "You've seen the language."

"Marcus read it to me."

"Colonial-era historical landmark. They're calling it a colonial-era site."

"I heard."

"Nora." She stopped. She had not meant to say it that way — with the weight she had put into it, the weight of the whole day.

Nora let the silence sit for a moment before she answered. "The state can name a landmark," she said. "They cannot name what is underneath it."

Tayak pressed her free hand flat against the counter. The laminate was cool and slightly rough under her palm. "That's not enough. Not legally. The designation creates a period of significance — they've set it at 1687 to 1750. Colonial occupation. If that stands, everything below it becomes secondary context. The tribal recognition petition's land argument depends on the mound being a tribal site, not a colonial one. If they establish the framing first—"

"I know what it does." Nora's voice had not changed pitch. "I've known what that kind of paper does since before you were born."

Tayak stopped talking.

Outside, a car passed on the street below. Its headlights swept across the ceiling and were gone.

"I have something," Nora said. "Not a story. Something written. Something Ida kept before me." She said it the way she might say *the door is unlocked* — a plain statement of a plain fact. "I've held it for sixty-two years. The council needs to hear it before the state reads the petition. Before anyone reads it."

Tayak's hand tightened on the phone. She made herself breathe before she spoke. "What is it?"

"Come at seven in the morning. Bring your notebook."

"Nora—"

"Not on the phone." Firm, but not unkind. The firmness of someone who had made this decision before the call began and was not going to unmake it. "Some things don't travel well on a phone. You know that."

She did know that. She had been taught it in a different context — Carver saying, *never describe a feature over the phone, make them come and look* — but the principle was the same. Some things needed to be held in the same room as the person who needed to hold them.

"Seven," Tayak said.

"Seven. And bring the notebook, not a recorder. I want you to write it down."

"Why not a recorder?"

A short silence. "Because a recorder is for broadcasting. A notebook is for keeping. I want this kept, not broadcast. Not yet."

Tayak pulled her field notebook from her jacket pocket — the current one, number eleven in the sequence, the cover worn soft at the corners from three months of pocket-carry. She set it on the counter in front of her. "All right."

"Good."

She looked at the notebook. She thought about what Nora had said — *something written, something Ida kept* — and felt the particular quality of attention that came when the ground began to give up something it had held for a long time. Not excitement. Something older and quieter than excitement. The feeling of standing at the edge of a feature and knowing, before you had seen it clearly, that it was going to change what you thought you were looking at.

"Is it enough?" she asked. "Whatever you have — will it be enough for the counter-evidence submission? Thirty days, Nora. That's what we have."

The silence this time was longer. She could hear, faintly, the sound of Nora's house — the low tick of a clock, or a radiator, something rhythmic and domestic and very old.

"I don't know," Nora said. "Come anyway."

Tayak looked at the postcard on the wall. The bay. The rivers. The thin lines of water running down to the sea from the high ground inland, from the ridges and terraces and the slight rises above the floodplains where people had always built the things they wanted to last.

"I'll be there at seven," she said.

After she hung up, she stood at the counter for a long time without moving. The notebook was in front of her, its cover worn smooth at the corners. She opened it to the next blank page — sequential, never skip, never erase — and wrote the date at the top, and below it: *Nora. 7 a.m. Something written. Ida kept it before her. 62 years.*

She looked at what she had written.

Then she wrote one more line, smaller, below the others, the way she wrote things she was not yet sure how to classify: *Do not decide what you are looking at until you have looked long enough to be wrong twice.*

She closed the notebook. She set it by the door so she would not forget it in the morning.

She did not sleep well. She had not expected to.

---

The site access road was unpaved past the second gate, and her headlights picked up the ruts from the morning's traffic — tire tracks pressed deep into clay that had softened in the afternoon heat and was beginning to crust again at the edges as the temperature dropped. She parked where she always parked, at the flat ground beside the equipment shed, and sat for a moment with the engine off.

The sky to the west still held color. A thin band of orange above the tree line, going fast.

She got out and walked the field road to the trench.

Wren was there. She had a headlamp on, angled low, and she was working the north face of the feature below the pendant layer with a bamboo skewer and a soft brush, her field notebook open on the plank beside her, a pencil tucked behind her ear. She had been at it for hours, by the look of the backdirt pile. She did not look up when Tayak's boots appeared at the trench edge.

Tayak pulled on her nitrile gloves. She picked up her trowel from the equipment board — her own, the Marshalltown she had carried since her second year under Marant, the handle worn dark at the grip — and climbed down into the trench.

The soil here smelled of wet clay and something older beneath it, the faint iron-and-carbon smell of undisturbed fill that had not seen air in centuries. She had learned to pay attention to that smell. It meant the context was intact.

She found her position at the south face, where the stratigraphy was clearest, and began to read the wall.

The color change was visible even in the failing light: a thin dark lens, maybe three centimeters, running horizontally across the face at a depth of roughly a meter and a half below datum. Not charcoal — the color was too even, too deliberate. Packed clay, darkened by something organic, laid down with intention. Below it, the subsoil was a pale, mottled yellow-gray, undisturbed. Above it, the fill layers climbed in sequence — each one a chapter, each one legible if you read slowly enough.

She pressed the flat of her trowel against the wall, feeling for the texture change where the dark lens met the fill above it. The blade caught the boundary exactly. She scraped a thin line of soil free, a single careful stroke, and examined what fell into her palm.

Fine-grained. No root intrusion. A few flecks of what might be burned clay, or might be nothing. She would need to bag it.

She worked.

The light went out of the sky by degrees. Wren's headlamp threw a small, focused circle on the north face. Tayak's own lamp was clipped to the brim of her field cap, and she adjusted it without thinking, the way she always did when the natural light dropped below the threshold where she could trust her eyes for color.

Neither of them spoke.

A truck moved slowly along the field road — Dawes, doing his rounds, the engine note familiar by now. The headlights swept across the top of the trench and moved on. The sound faded into the tree line.

Tayak scraped another thin line from the wall. The dark lens was thicker here, toward the south face — perhaps four centimeters. Intentional layering. Someone had brought material to this spot and laid it down carefully, not once but in stages. The mound had been built, not accumulated. That was not a new finding, but the evidence was cleaner here than anywhere else in the profile, and she wanted to document it before anything else happened to this site.

She photographed the wall. She measured the lens with her tape and called the numbers to herself, logging them in her notebook without looking away from the face.

Wren said, quietly, "I'm sorry."

She did not explain what for. She did not ask for anything in return.

Tayak did not answer immediately. She scraped another line from the wall — one careful stroke, the trowel blade angled to follow the boundary of the dark lens — and read the color change where the fill above it met the clay below. The fill was a warm brown, the clay beneath it almost white in the lamplight. The line between them was as clear as a sentence break.

"Thirty days," she said.

She kept working.

The trowel scraped against the compacted fill with a sound like dry paper. A beetle moved through the backdirt pile at the trench edge. Somewhere in the tree line, something shifted — a branch, or a bird settling.

Wren did not say anything else. She went back to her bamboo skewer, back to the north face, back to the slow, patient work of reading what the ground had kept.

Tayak photographed the lens again from a different angle. She logged the context number. She bagged the soil she had scraped free, sealed the bag, wrote the number on the label in permanent marker, and set it with the others.

The feature below the pendant layer was real. It was intact. It was older than anything they had documented so far, and it had been laid down by someone who knew what they were doing — someone who understood that the ground would hold what you gave it, that the act of placing a thing carefully in the earth was itself a form of keeping.

She thought about what Nora had said.

*Something written. Something Ida kept before me. Sixty-two years.*

She thought about the notebook on her counter at home, the line she had written small at the bottom of the page: *Do not decide what you are looking at until you have looked long enough to be wrong twice.*

She pressed her palm flat against the trench wall. The clay was cool and slightly damp, the way soil always was at this depth, even in October. It held the shape of her hand for a moment before the impression began to blur at the edges.

She pulled her hand back and looked at what it had left.

Thirty days. And Nora had something — not a story, she had said. Something written. Something that had been kept in the dark for sixty-two years, the way this lens had been kept in the dark for two thousand, waiting for someone to come with the right tools and the patience to read it without deciding too soon what it meant.

She did not know if it would be enough.

She picked up her trowel and kept working.
