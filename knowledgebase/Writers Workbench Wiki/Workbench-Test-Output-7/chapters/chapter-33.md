The third stratum smelled different from the second.

Tayak noticed it before she saw the color change. A deeper, damper note worked up through the usual leaf-mold and clay, something older in it. She had been in the trench since before first light, kneeling on the foam pad Wren had cut from a camping mat, and the sky above the mound was only now going from black to the flat gray of early October.

"Color change," she said.

Wren was in the adjacent unit, three feet to her left. She leaned over the balk and looked. The soil in Tayak's square had shifted from the reddish-brown of the second stratum to something darker, almost charcoal at the center, fading to deep tan at the edges.

"Feature?" Wren said.

"Maybe." Tayak set down her trowel and picked up the brush. "Log it."

She heard the pen dragging across damp notebook pages. She kept her eyes on the dark soil.

The mound was quiet at this hour. The cicadas had stopped two weeks ago and the crickets were thinning out. What remained was the sound of the Mattawoman bottom: a wood thrush somewhere in the tree line, the low tick of cooling metal from the equipment shed. The site felt held, the way a room feels when everyone in it is waiting.

She worked the brush in short strokes, clearing from the outside of the feature toward the center. The dark soil was softer than the surrounding matrix, more yielding under the bristles, and it smelled of something she could not name. Not rot, not mineral. Something between the two. The smell of a place that had been sealed and had made its own air for a long time.

The pale edge appeared at 6:14 by her watch.

She stopped.

A curve. Not the flat edge of a sherd, not the knob of a worked stone. A curve, smooth, with a faint sheen where the brush had cleared it. That sheen meant polished surface, not fracture. She set the brush on the foam pad and looked at it without moving.

Not ceramic. Not bone.

She picked up the bamboo pick and began clearing along the curve's outer edge, working slowly, keeping the pick's tip just inside the soil line so it never touched the object itself. The curve extended two inches, then three, and then she saw the suspension hole. A clean, round perforation at the upper edge, its rim beveled smooth from something that had passed through it for a long time.

Shell.

She sat back on her heels and breathed.

"Wren."

"Yeah."

"Get the camera kit."

She heard the foam case unzip. She did not take her eyes off the pale arc in the dark soil.

By the time Wren handed the camera body over the balk, Tayak had cleared enough to see the shape. Circular, roughly four inches across, lying face-up in the feature. The incised lines were visible even in flat morning light: concentric circles radiating from a central point, their edges filled with dark soil that made them read as clearly as ink. The surface between the lines held that uneven sheen she had seen on other handled shell, smoother in the center where hands had touched it, slightly matte toward the edges where the polish had worn.

She attached the macro lens and did not rush.

The gray foam board went in first, slid carefully under the camera angle to give the gorget a neutral ground. Then the scale bar, ten centimeters, red and white, placed to the right. She did not touch the object. She photographed it as it lay, in the dark soil, in the feature, in the ground where it had been for however long it had been there.

Raking light first. She angled the LED panel low and shot from the north, then the south, watching the incised lines go from subtle to crisp as the shadows deepened in the grooves. The concentric circles sharpened. Inside the innermost ring she could see something else. Not circles. A figure, or the suggestion of one. A raptor, maybe. A spread of wings reduced to essential geometry.

She shot forty frames before she moved the scale bar.

"Can you see it from there?" she said.

Wren had her own notebook open, sketching. "Enough. Shell gorget. Intact."

"Intact," Tayak said. "Log that."

She moved the LED panel to the east and shot the suspension hole in detail: the bevel of the rim, the dark groove worn into it by cord, the faint staining around the perforation that was either soil or something that had been on the cord itself. Ochre, maybe. She would not guess yet.

Pre-contact style. That was the first thing her mind said, and she let it say it, and then she made herself slow down.

The concentric circles and the bird figure were Mississippian in their grammar. She had seen that iconography in every survey text she had studied, had seen it on gorgets from Tennessee, from Oklahoma, from the Cahokia site reports she'd read in Marant's seminar. The Southeastern Ceremonial Complex. The Birdman. The sun-circle. These were forms that had been old for five hundred years before the colonial period.

But the lines were too regular.

She leaned in close enough that her breath would have fogged a cold surface and looked at the incised grooves without the camera. The cuts were narrow and consistent. Not the slightly variable width you got from a stone tool working against the grain of the shell, but the even, controlled line of a steel blade or a steel awl. The kind of tool you got from a trading post. The kind of tool that did not exist here before contact.

She sat back.

Someone made something old on purpose.

The hope she had carried down into the trench, that this was pre-contact, that the stratigraphy was running clean and deep and uninterrupted, pressed against the wall of what she was seeing. She felt it give slightly, like a door that opens onto a different room than the one you expected.

Not useless. Not even close to useless. But not what she had hoped.

The fear was simpler and uglier. The state's lawyers would look at an 18th-century object in a pre-contact mound and call it intrusive. Call it placed. Call it insufficient to establish the unbroken presence she needed the mound to prove.

She photographed for another twenty minutes. Every angle. The feature walls. The soil matrix immediately surrounding the gorget, with its flecks of charcoal and the dark organic staining that suggested a deliberate deposit rather than casual loss. She photographed the suspension hole twice more with the raking light, because the wear pattern in that hole was the most honest thing about the object. It told you the gorget had been worn, had been used, had been someone's.

When she finally lifted it from the ground, she used both hands, fingers spread beneath the shell's cool, slightly rough underside. Its weight surprised her: lighter than it looked, the way shell always was, as if it were mostly air made solid. The front surface, where the incising was, felt silky under her gloved thumb, with a slight drag where the lines crossed. When she turned it over, the back was rougher, unpolished, with faint striations from the grinding that had shaped it.

She tapped it once, very lightly, against her thumbnail.

A dry, ceramic click. Not stone. Not metal. Shell.

She set it in the evidence tray and looked at it in the growing light.

The sky had gone from gray to pale gold while she was working. The wood thrush had stopped. Somewhere down the field road a truck door closed, Dawes probably, doing his morning rounds, and the sound carried flat and clear across the wet grass.

"It's 18th century," she said.

Wren looked up from the notebook.

"The tool marks. Steel. But the iconography is Mississippian. Five, six hundred years old as a style." She kept her eyes on the gorget. "Someone in the 1700s sat down and made something that looked like it came from before contact. Deliberately."

Wren was quiet for a moment. "Why?"

"That," she said, "is the question."

She sealed the evidence bag and wrote the label in permanent marker: *18CH-447, Unit 4, Level 7, Feature 3. Shell gorget, engraved marine shell, intact. 6:14 a.m., 4 October 2024.* She initialed it and handed it to Wren, who countersigned without being asked.

She pulled off her gloves and picked up her phone.

She had Nora's number in her recent calls, dialed four times in the past week, and she pressed it before she had fully decided to.

Three rings. Then the sound of the house: a chair scraping, the particular silence of old rooms with thick walls.

"Tayak." Not a question. Nora always answered as if she had been expecting the call for some time.

"I need to describe something to you." Tayak picked up the evidence bag and turned it in the light. The gorget inside shifted, pale against the plastic. "Tell me if any of it means anything."

"Go ahead."

"Shell pendant. Round, about four inches across. Engraved on the front: concentric circles, and inside the innermost ring, what looks like a raptor. Wings spread flat, reduced down to the basic shape." She paused. "Pre-contact iconography. But the tool marks say 18th century. Someone made it to look old. Made it to look like something from before contact."

The silence on the other end was not the silence of a woman trying to remember. It was the silence of a woman deciding how much to say.

"That's Tessa's," Nora said.

Tayak went still. Beside her, Wren had stopped writing.

"Tessa made it herself." Nora's voice was even, almost careful, the way she got when she was reciting rather than talking. "She made it to look the way the old ones looked, because she wanted it to be the kind of thing that would be recognized. Even by people who didn't know her."

Tayak looked at the gorget through the plastic. The bird figure inside the innermost ring. The deliberate, even lines of a steel tool imitating something five centuries older.

"How do you know that?" she said.

"Because Tessa's granddaughter told her daughter," Nora said. "Who told my grandmother Ida. Who told me." A brief pause. "The gorget is in the story, Tayak. It has been since Ida sat down at that kitchen table in 1962 and told me what I needed to know."

Tayak turned away from Wren. Not because she needed privacy. Wren had heard everything and Tayak didn't mind. She turned because she needed to face something other than another person's face while she processed what Nora had just said.

The field opened out in front of her, the grass still heavy with dew, the light coming in low and gold from the east. The mound's profile rose against the tree line, its gentle curve so familiar now that she sometimes forgot to see it as a thing at all.

"Describe it to me," she said. "What does the story say it looks like?"

"Circles," Nora said. "Rings inside rings. And a bird in the center. She said it was the kind of bird that sees everything at once."

Tayak's hand tightened on the phone.

"The suspension hole," she said. "One or two?"

"One. She drilled it herself. Said it took her three days because she was afraid of cracking the shell."

Tayak exhaled. One hole. She looked at the evidence bag. One hole, bevel-rimmed, with the dark groove of long cord wear and the faint staining she had not yet named.

"What was on the cord?" she said. "Do you know?"

A pause. "Indigo," Nora said. "Blue thread. She dyed it herself. She was particular about the color."

The staining around the perforation. She had photographed it twice with the raking light because it was the most honest part of the object. She had not guessed yet. She had been right not to guess.

She turned back to Wren, who was watching her with the particular stillness of a person who understands that something is happening that she does not fully understand yet.

"The story describes the artifact," Tayak said. Not to Wren. To herself. To the part of her that had been trained to distrust coincidence. "The physical object and the oral account describe the same thing."

"Yes," Nora said.

"That's not, " She stopped. She had been about to say *that's not how it's supposed to work*, and she caught herself, because the sentence was wrong on its face. It was exactly how it was supposed to work. It was how it had always worked, before her discipline had decided that only one kind of record counted.

"Tayak," Nora said.

"I'm here."

"The gorget is in the story because Tessa put it in the story. She made the object and she made the account of the object at the same time. She knew both of them would need to survive." Nora paused, and Tayak heard the shift in her breathing that meant she was choosing words carefully. "She didn't think of them as different things."

Tayak looked at the evidence bag in her hand. The pale shell inside it. The incised lines she had photographed for forty minutes, every angle, every shadow, because she had been trained to document what the ground gave her.

Tessa had done the same thing. Different tools, different medium, different century, but the same act. Make the record. Make it survive.

"I need to ask you something," Tayak said.

"Ask."

"How many of them are there? Objects that are in the story?"

Another silence. Longer this time.

"More than one," Nora said.

"How many more?"

Wren had set the notebook down on the edge of the trench and was not pretending to do anything else.

"That depends," Nora said, "on how deep you go."

Tayak looked at the mound. The third stratum. They were at the third stratum, and Nora was telling her the story went deeper than the gorget, deeper than the ceramic pipe bowl, deeper than the glass bead that had started all of this.

"How deep does the story go?" she said.

Nora was quiet for long enough that Tayak thought the call had dropped.

"To the beginning," Nora said finally. "It goes to the beginning."

Tayak sealed the evidence bag again, though she had not opened it. A reflex. Something to do with her hands.

"I need to come see you," she said. "Not on the phone."

"I know," Nora said. "Come when you can. I'll be here."

She ended the call and stood holding the phone against her sternum, looking at the mound's low profile against the morning sky. The gold light had gone white. The dew was burning off the grass in slow wisps.

"What did she say?" Wren asked.

Tayak looked at the evidence bag. Then at the trench. Then at the sky.

"She said the oral history and the physical record are the same record." She picked up her field notebook. "She said they always were."

Wren was quiet for a moment. "That's, " She stopped.

"Yeah," Tayak said.

She opened the notebook to a fresh page and wrote the date, the time, the feature number. Then she wrote: *Nora confirms gorget identity by oral account. Describes: concentric circles, raptor figure, single suspension hole, indigo cord. Matches physical artifact exactly. Chain of transmission: Tessa, Tessa's granddaughter, her daughter, Ida, Nora. Account predates excavation. Physical record and oral record are the same record.*

She underlined the last sentence. Then she looked at what she'd written and underlined it again.

The question that had lodged in her when she first saw the tool marks, why would someone make something deliberately old, had not gone away. Nora had answered part of it: Tessa had wanted the gorget recognized. Had wanted it to speak a language older than her own century, older than the colonial disruption that had rearranged everything.

But there was a second question now, sitting just behind the first.

If Tessa had put the gorget in the story and in the ground at the same time, if she had understood that both records needed to survive, then she had also understood that one of them might not.

Which one had she trusted more?

Tayak capped her pen and looked at the evidence bag.

The gorget had survived. The story had survived. Both of them had made it to this trench, to this morning, to her hands.

But Nora had said *it depends on how deep you go*, and she had said the story went to the beginning, and Tayak was standing at the third stratum with more below her, and she did not yet know what was waiting at the bottom.

She picked up the evidence bag and her field notebook and climbed out of the trench.

"I need to call Marcus," she said.

Wren looked up. "And then?"

"And then I need to finish the feature log. Council gets it at seven." She paused. "University at seven-oh-one."

Wren almost smiled. She picked up the notebook without being asked.

Tayak was already walking.

The field phone had two bars. Enough. She stopped at the equipment table, a folding aluminum thing that listed slightly east with one leg shorter than the others, and set the evidence bag down where she could see it while she dialed.

Marcus picked up on the second ring.

"Tell me," he said.

"Shell gorget. Marine whelk, engraved. Pre-contact style, but the tool marks say eighteenth century. Someone made something old on purpose."

A beat. "How old is old?"

"Mississippian iconography. Concentric circles, what looks like a raptor figure. That style peaked five, six hundred years before this object was made."

"So whoever made it was reaching back."

"Deliberately. Yes."

She heard him breathe. Not surprise. The particular steadiness of a man absorbing something he had half-expected.

"Nora knows it," Tayak said.

The steadiness broke, just slightly. "She told you?"

"She named it before I described it. She said it's in the story. She gave me the transmission chain: Tessa's granddaughter to her daughter to Ida to Nora." She paused. "The oral account and the physical artifact match. Concentric circles, raptor, single suspension hole, indigo cord through the hole. She knew the cord color, Marcus."

Silence on his end. She could hear wind through the phone. He was outside somewhere.

"That's not coincidence," he said.

"No."

"Does Voss know?"

"Not yet."

"Good." A pause. "Give me an hour before that log goes anywhere. Council at seven, university at seven-oh-one. That's the sequence."

It was not a request. She understood what he was doing: building a sequence, making sure the tribal council heard before the university did, before Voss could position himself as the institutional intermediary between her find and the record she was building. It was the right move. It was also the move of a man who had been burned before.

"One hour," she said.

"I'll call you back." He ended the call.

Wren was standing three feet away, not pretending otherwise.

"Marcus wants the sequence locked before the log goes out," Tayak said.

"Smart."

"Yes." She picked up the evidence bag. The gorget shifted inside it, and she felt the slight weight redistribution, the dry ceramic click of shell against the bag's interior seam. Even through the plastic she could feel the coolness of it. Marine shell held cold the way stone did, a thermal memory of deep water. "Come look at something with me."

She carried it to the equipment table and set it flat. Her camera was still around her neck from the morning's documentation session. She unzipped the side pocket of her field bag and found the small LED raking light she used for incised surfaces, a flat panel the size of a paperback with a gooseneck mount that clipped to the table's edge.

She clipped it. Angled the beam low and lateral across the evidence bag.

Even through the plastic, the gorget's lines jumped. The concentric circles resolved into something more complex than she had registered in the trench: four rings, not three, the innermost ring interrupted at the top by a figure she had logged as a raptor but could now see more precisely. Not a raptor. A raptor becoming something else. Wings spread, yes, but the head tilted at an angle that was not a bird's angle. A transformation figure. The Birdman motif from the Southeastern Ceremonial Complex, ancient in its original form, redrawn by an eighteenth-century hand that had known exactly what it was copying and had chosen to copy it anyway.

Tessa had known what she was doing.

"The lines here," Tayak said, pointing without touching. "See how regular they are? Pre-contact work would be slightly broader, more variable. Stone tools. These are narrow and consistent."

Wren leaned in, notebook open, pen moving. "Steel blade."

"Or a file. Either way, trade goods. Post-contact manufacture." She straightened. "But the design is Mississippian. You wouldn't know that if you hadn't studied it. She studied it."

"Where would she have seen one?"

Tayak thought about Matta, the chapter of Nora's story she had been given, the pipe-maker who had moved through two worlds simultaneously, who had kept accounts under a colonial name and taught his granddaughter to read the land. Tessa was Matta's granddaughter. If Matta had access to trade networks, to the material culture moving through the colonial Chesapeake, "There were gorgets circulating in the eighteenth century," she said. "Metal ones. Officers wore them. They were given to Native leaders as alliance badges." She looked at the figure in the shell. "She would have known the difference between a colonial metal gorget and the older shell tradition. She made the shell version. She made the old thing."

"To say something."

"To say something that the colonial form couldn't say."

Wren wrote that down. Tayak let her.

Her phone buzzed. Not Marcus: a Baltimore area code she didn't recognize. She let it go to voicemail. Whoever it was could wait. She was looking at the gorget and thinking about what Nora had said the night Tayak first called her about the ceramic pipe bowl: *the story doesn't stop at what you can see.* She was thinking about the word *beginning*, the way Nora had said it. Not as a metaphor. As a location.

The voicemail notification appeared. She ignored it.

"I want to open the bag," she said.

Wren looked up. "Here?"

"Not to handle it. Just, I want to smell it."

A beat, and then, because Wren had been trained by her and she had been trained by Cecile Marant, who had taught her that the ground spoke through every sense before it spoke through analysis, Wren said nothing and stepped back.

Tayak unsealed the bag at the corner. Just the corner. She brought it close and breathed in.

Damp soil. The mineral sharpness of disturbed subsoil. Underneath that, something older: a faint organic note, not decay exactly, more like the memory of something once living. The shell itself. The animal that had been the shell, two centuries and more ago, in water she would never see.

She sealed the bag again.

"Log it," she said. "Feature 14, Level 3. Engraved marine shell gorget, probable Busycon whelk, Mississippian-derived iconography, historic-period manufacture. Concentric circles, transformation figure, single suspension hole with wear beveling, indigo fiber residue in the hole channel. Oral account received from Nora Moyaone prior to artifact examination confirms identity, maker, and transmission chain. Account predates excavation."

Wren was writing fast. "Oral account as data?"

"Oral account as data." She watched Wren write it. "That's not a footnote. That goes in the main description."

Wren wrote it in the main description.

Her phone rang again. Marcus this time. She picked up.

"Council knows," he said. "Dorothy Swann wants a meeting when you surface. Harold wants a copy of the field log." A pause. "Cassie says good work."

Something moved in her chest. Not pride exactly. Something more structural than pride: the feeling of a beam finding its load-bearing position.

"Tell them I'll have a full description to the council before the daily log goes to the university," she said. "They see it first."

"Already told them that." Another pause. "Tayak."

"Yeah."

"You should know: Voss called my office this morning. Before your find. He's circling."

She looked at the evidence bag. The gorget inside it. The story Nora had carried for sixty-two years, which was itself the end of a chain that ran back through Ida and Ida's grandmother and Matta's granddaughter and Tessa herself, all the way to the moment Tessa had sat down with a whelk shell and a steel blade and decided to make something that would speak a language older than her own ruined century.

"I'll handle Voss," she said.

"I know you will." He ended the call.

She stood for a moment with the phone in her hand. The morning had gone fully bright, the dew gone, the light flat and direct. Somewhere across the field a crow called twice and stopped.

Wren was watching from the other side of the trench, notebook open across one forearm, pen capped: listening rather than writing, which was the right call.

Tayak thought about what Nora had said. Not the words exactly, though she had been replaying them for an hour: *Tessa's granddaughter told her daughter, who told my grandmother Ida, who told me.* She thought about the structure of it. The architecture. The way Nora had recited the chain not as a story about memory but as a record of transmission, each link named, each handoff located in a relationship, a kitchen, a deathbed, a fire.

The gorget is in the story.

She looked at the gorget.

The gorget was in the story. The gorget was also in the ground. Those were not two different facts. They were the same fact, kept in two different materials.

She had been thinking about this wrong. She had been thinking about the oral history and the physical record as parallel tracks, one running through breath and memory, one running through soil and shell, and she had been thinking her job was to hold them next to each other and check whether they matched, the way you check a translation against the original. But that was not what this was.

This was not a translation.

Nora's chain, Tessa to Tessa's granddaughter to Ida's grandmother to Ida to Nora, was not a description of the mound. It was the mound's own continuation. The mound was a record kept in earth. The oral chain was the same record kept in breath. They were coextensive. They had always been coextensive. The shell in the ground and the name in the story were not evidence of each other. They were each other.

She understood this not as an idea but as a physical fact, the way you understand depth when you are standing at the edge of it.

Wren said, "You all right?"

"Yes." She was. "Hand me the notebook."

Wren passed it across the trench. She opened it to the current page, found the last line of her notes, *Feature 14, Level 3, oral account as data*, and below it she wrote one sentence in her standard field hand, the same deliberate printing she had used since graduate school, the same hand Carver had taught her to use because clarity in a field notebook was a form of respect for whoever came after you.

*Oral chain and stratigraphic sequence are coextensive.*

She looked at it for a moment. It was not a poetic sentence. It was not meant to be. It was the kind of sentence that either meant something or it didn't. It meant something. She knew it meant something because it had changed the way the trench looked when she raised her eyes from the page.

She closed the notebook.

The morning pressed down flat and bright around the site. The smell of turned earth was still sharp: that mineral bite of subsoil exposed to air, the faint organic note underneath it that she had caught when she unsealed the bag, the memory of a living thing in water she would never see. Somewhere in the field a cricket had started up, one persistent note, slightly out of season.

She set the notebook on the foam board next to the evidence bag.

Tessa had made this object and put it in the ground, and Tessa's granddaughter had told someone what it was, and that someone had told someone else, and the chain had held. Through Ida's stroke, through the thinning years when Nora's mother had let the story go slack, through every decade when it would have been easier and safer to let the whole thing dissolve into silence. The chain had held. The object had held. And here they were, both of them, in the same trench, in the same morning, saying the same thing in two different languages.

She picked up the evidence bag and held it level, the gorget centered in her palm.

The transformation figure looked up at her through the plastic: arms spread, the body caught between forms, neither fully one thing nor fully another. She had thought, when she first saw it, that it was about change. She thought now it was about continuity. The same figure, the same arms, the same refusal to resolve into a single shape. Not because it was incomplete. Because it was exactly what it meant to be.

She handed the bag to Wren.

"Get this to the conservation suite before noon," she said. "I want Adaeze to look at the suspension hole before we do anything else. If there's fiber residue still in the channel, she can pull a sample."

"And the field log?"

"I'll finish it tonight. Council gets it at seven. University at seven-oh-one."

Wren took the bag carefully, both hands, the way Tayak had taught her: not gripping, cradling, the way you carry something that has already survived more than you have.

Tayak watched her go. Then she looked back down into the trench.

The dark soil of Feature 14 was still open, the depression where the gorget had lain still faintly visible as a slightly lighter oval in the fill. Beside it, the charcoal flecks she had noted at Level 2 continued down. The mound went deeper. There was more.

She thought about Nora, eighty-two years old in the house on Billingsley Road, who had carried this for sixty-two years and had given it to her over a phone call at half past eight in the morning as though it were the most ordinary thing in the world. The way you give someone something you have been holding for them for a long time.

She thought about what else Nora was holding.

Her phone was in her jacket pocket. She did not reach for it yet. She stood at the edge of the trench and looked at the place where the gorget had been, and she let herself have the moment. Not the professional moment, not the moment she would write into the field log or describe to the council or defend to anyone. The other one. The one that had no field-note language yet. The one that was just the feeling of standing at the edge of something that went all the way down.

Then she reached for the phone.

Nora picked up on the second ring.

"There's more," Tayak said. "Isn't there."

It was not a question.

She told Nora about the depression in the soil, the slightly lighter oval left behind, the charcoal flecks continuing down into Level 4.

Nora was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "Yes. There's more."

"How much more?"

"Enough." A pause. "More than enough."

Tayak looked at the trench. The morning light had shifted while she'd been on the phone, the sun now high enough to throw a hard shadow along the eastern wall, and the stratigraphy showed clean and unambiguous in that light: the dark band of Level 3 fill, the paler subsoil beneath it, and below that a zone she hadn't opened yet, a slightly darker seam she had noted and flagged and told herself she would reach tomorrow.

"Nora," she said. "I need to ask you something."

"Ask."

"The account you have, the full account, everything Ida told you: does it name objects I haven't found yet?"

The pause this time was longer. Not reluctance. Something more like care.

"Yes," Nora said.

Tayak waited. She did not push.

"Come tonight," Nora said. "After your council call. I'll tell you what Ida told me, the way she told it to me. Not on the phone."

Tayak understood. Some things needed a room. Needed the specific gravity of two people in the same air.

"Tonight," she said. "After seven."

"I'll have the light on," Nora said. "And Tayak, " She stopped.

"What."

"Bring your notebook. The one you write in, not the one for the university. The red one."

Tayak looked at the red field notebook on the foam board. Carver had told her to use red because it was easiest to find in a site bucket. She had used red notebooks for eleven years and had never told Nora that.

"How do you know I use a red notebook?"

"Because," Nora said, with the particular patience of someone who has been waiting a long time to say a thing, "your grandmother told me. She said you got it from your teacher and your teacher was right."

Tayak said nothing.

"Come tonight," Nora said. "We'll talk properly." And she rang off.

Tayak sat on the trench edge for a while after that.

The cricket was still going somewhere in the field, that single dry note, slightly out of season, indifferent to the calendar. The smell of the turned earth had not changed. The trench was the same trench it had been twenty minutes ago. But the arithmetic had shifted, and she couldn't un-shift it.

She had been doing the math since Nora first named the gorget. Doing it carefully, the way you do math when you suspect the answer and don't want to arrive at it too fast.

Here was what she knew: Nora's oral account had named the gorget before Tayak found it. The account had named the stratum, the context, the maker, and the object's significance. It had been accurate at Level 3.

If it was accurate at Level 3, it was accurate at Level 4. At Level 5. At whatever lay below that.

Which meant the mound went deeper than her preliminary survey had mapped. Which meant the woman Nora would tell her about tonight, the one at the beginning, the one Ida had called the first one, was real. Which meant she was her ancestor. Not a historical figure. Not a research subject. Not an entry in the artifact catalog or a reference in the preliminary report.

Hers.

She had known this was possible. She had known it since the first stratum, since the glass bead, since Nora had said *Saya* in that particular voice, the voice that meant the name was not a name but a person. She had known it and had kept it at a professional distance, the way you keep a flame at a distance when you are working with something flammable, because the work required distance, because if she let it be personal it would compromise her standing to defend it.

But the math didn't care about her standing.

The math said: you have been excavating your own family's ground. You have been excavating your own family's dead. You have been writing field notes about your own grandmother's grandmother's grandmother's grandmother, and you have been doing it in the careful, neutral, third-person prose of professional archaeology, and the ground has been patient with you about this, but the ground is not going to be patient forever.

She looked at the depression in Feature 14. The slightly lighter oval. The place where the gorget had been.

She thought about Tessa. Not Tessa as a data point, not Tessa as a name in an oral account, but Tessa at seventeen or twenty or thirty, sitting in whatever light she had, working a piece of marine whelk with a steel blade she had gotten through trade, making something deliberately old, deliberately ancestral, copying a form that was already ancient in her time because she needed the weight of the old form, needed the continuity it carried, needed anyone who found it, then or later, in her lifetime or after, to understand that this was not a new thing, not a colonial-era thing, not a thing that had started with contact or ended with it. This was a thing that went all the way down.

And she had put it in the ground and told her granddaughter what it was.

And the granddaughter had told someone.

And the chain had held.

Tayak pressed her palms flat against the trench edge, feeling the cool soil, the slight give of it, the texture of aggregate and organic matter and time. She had done this a thousand times at a thousand sites. She had never felt it the way she felt it now.

She reached for the notebook. Not to write the thought, because the thought had no field-note language yet, but to do the work that still needed doing. Because Wren needed the feature log completed before the conservation suite appointment. Because the council call was at seven and she needed to be ready.

She opened to the current page. Feature 14, Level 3. She wrote the depression dimensions: 22 centimeters at the long axis, 14 at the short, 6 centimeters deep, oriented roughly north-south. She noted the charcoal flecks continuing into Level 4 and flagged the darker seam she had seen in the eastern wall as a probable feature boundary. She noted that the fill in the depression was slightly lighter in color than the surrounding matrix, consistent with deliberate backfill after object placement.

She did not write: *this is my family.*

She did not write: *there is a woman at the bottom of this mound whose name I have not yet been told.*

She did not write: *I have been standing at the edge of this for eleven years and I have only just understood how far down it goes.*

She wrote the dimensions. She wrote the soil color in Munsell notation. She wrote the feature number and the level and the grid coordinates and the time, 9:47 a.m., and she drew a small cross-section diagram of the depression in the margin, the way Carver had taught her, because a diagram in the field was worth three paragraphs of description and because clarity in a field notebook was a form of respect for whoever came after you.

Then she called Wren.

Wren picked up on the first ring. "Still at the site?"

"I need you back here before you go to Adaeze. I need photographs of the depression: the empty feature, not just the artifact. Raking light from the east, same setup as the gorget. And bring the gray board."

"Ten minutes."

"And Wren." She paused. "The field log goes to the council at seven. University at seven-oh-one."

A brief silence. Wren understood the order of precedence. "Understood."

She hung up and looked at the trench one more time.

The mound went deeper. There was a woman at the bottom of it whose name Nora had not said on the phone. There were thirty-one days left before Garrett Slade's bulldozers arrived at the Route 301 connector.

She had work to do.

She stood, brushed the soil from her field pants, and picked up the notebook. The red cover was warm from the morning sun, that flat, dry warmth of an object that has been sitting in light. She tucked it under her arm.

Tonight she would drive to Billingsley Road. Nora would have the light on. They would sit at the kitchen table where Ida had sat with Nora in October 1962, and Nora would tell her what Ida had told her, the whole of it, the way it needed to be told, and Tayak would write it in the red notebook in the same careful hand she used for everything, the hand Carver had taught her and that her grandmother had apparently known about, and it would be the most important thing she had ever written down.

But first she had to photograph the empty depression. First she had to finish the feature log. First she had to be an archaeologist for a few more hours, because the work was still the work, and the work was the only way through.

She crouched at the trench edge and looked at the place where the gorget had been.

The soil held the shape of it still, that faint, lighter oval, the ghost of the object in the fill. In another day or two it would be indistinguishable from the surrounding matrix. The ground would close over it the way the ground closes over everything.

She had photographed it. She had logged it. She had held it in her hands.

She had heard Nora say *Tessa made it herself.*

That was enough. That was more than enough. That was the whole argument, right there in the soil: in the shape of an absence that was also a presence, in the chain of mouths that had carried the story from the 18th century to this morning without a break, in the object and the account arriving at the same trench at the same hour and saying the same thing.

She heard Wren's truck on the track.

She stood up, straightened her jacket, and went to meet her.
