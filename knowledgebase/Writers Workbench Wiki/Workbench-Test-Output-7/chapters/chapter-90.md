The sky above the tree line was the color of old iron, not yet light, not quite dark, and Tayak stood at the edge of the trench with her field notebook tucked under one arm and her hands in her jacket pockets. She had not opened the notebook. She did not think she would.

The site was quiet in the way sites are quiet before anyone arrives — not peaceful, exactly, but held. The mound rose to her left, its grass pale and frost-stiffened in the early cold, the closed-off sections marked by the low cable fence the tribe had put up in September. The open trench ran south from the mound's base, the deepest chamber at its far end, shored with the last of the temporary planking they would pull today. A thin mist came off the turned earth, rising from the darker patch where the soil was richest, where it had not seen air in two thousand years. The mist was not dramatic. It was just moisture leaving the ground.

She had driven out at four-thirty, telling herself she needed to check the pump. The pump was fine. She had stood in the parking area for a few minutes listening to the engine tick as it cooled, then walked the field road in the dark with her headlamp off, following the ruts Dawes's truck had pressed into the clay months ago, now frozen hard and permanent as a decision.

She was afraid.

Not of the work — she had done the work, had spent eleven months doing it, had learned the ground layer by layer the way you learn a language, the way you learn a person, slowly and with the understanding that you will never fully know it. She was afraid of something more specific. She was afraid that returning the artifacts to the earth was not the same as never having taken them out. That the ground would know the difference. That the ancestors would know the difference. That she was still, at the bottom of everything, a person trained to remove things — trained to read the ground as a text, to extract meaning from it, to carry that meaning away in labeled bags and field notebooks — and that no care she brought to today would change what the training had made her.

She had thought about this through the night, lying awake in the small house on Route 6 she'd been renting since October, listening to the freight train that came through at two a.m. She had thought: *Carver would say the ground doesn't need your guilt. It needs your attention.* And she had thought: *Carver would be right. And it still wouldn't be enough.*

The frost on the mound's grass caught the first gray light and held it without glittering. Tayak looked at the trench. The wooden figure was in the conservation case in her truck — she had driven it out herself rather than send it in the van with the rest, which was not protocol but which she had done anyway, and no one had said anything. The copper pendant was in a padded tray beside it. The shell gorgets, wrapped in acid-free tissue, in their own case. The pipe bowl. The antler figure. All of them here, all of them waiting to go back.

She heard the car before she saw the lights. The headlamps swept the field road and went dark, and after a moment Wren's footsteps came across the frozen ground — a particular sound, that deliberate, even tread, the kind that does not announce itself but cannot be mistaken for anyone else's.

Wren stopped at the edge of the trench, two feet to Tayak's right, and looked down into it. She was wearing the same field jacket she'd worn since August, the one with the frayed left cuff. She had a binder under her arm — the reburial inventory, the context sheets, the layer maps, everything they would need to put each object back in the right place. She did not open it either.

They stood there.

The mist rose from the dark earth of the trench, slow and without drama. A killdeer called once from somewhere near the creek, then stopped. The sky was going from iron to ash.

Tayak thought about the first time she had put her hands into this ground — the morning in January, eleven months ago, when the permit had just cleared and she'd driven out at five a.m. in a truck she'd borrowed from the department, and knelt at the mound's edge in the cold, and felt the soil give under her trowel with the particular resistance of undisturbed earth, a resistance that is not refusal but weight, the weight of everything that has been pressing down for a very long time. She had been careful then. She had been careful every day since.

It had not been enough. She knew that. She had taken things out of the ground. She had put them in bags and labeled the bags and driven them to a conservation suite in College Park, and a woman named Adaeze had signed for them at a counter, and they had been photographed and measured and analyzed and described in forty-three pages of field assessment on university letterhead. All of that had been necessary. All of it had served a purpose — had served the purpose of proving, in a language courts and commissions could read, that this ground was what the tribe had always known it to be.

She was glad of it. She was also not sure gladness was the right response.

Wren shifted her weight slightly and the frozen grass crackled under her boot. She did not speak. Tayak did not speak. This was not discomfort between them — there was plenty of that, had been for months, since the text Tayak had not answered and the apology Wren had made in the rain without asking for anything back. This was something different. This was the silence of two people who had agreed, without a word passing between them, that today required something other than words.

Tayak pulled her right hand from her pocket and held it open at her side, palm facing down toward the earth of the trench. The air was very cold. She could feel the cold rising off the soil, or thought she could.

*I am going to do this right,* she thought. *I am going to do this as carefully as I took it apart. And it will not be the same. And I am going to do it anyway.*

The sky lightened another degree. Somewhere beyond the tree line, a crow called — once, twice, then silence.

Wren opened the binder.

The shell gorgets went in first.

Tayak had chosen that order deliberately — working from the shallowest layer down, reversing the sequence of excavation, so that the earth closed in the same order it had originally built itself. She and Wren had argued about it briefly, three days ago, over the context sheets. Wren had suggested deepest first, to avoid disturbing the lower layers during the final closure. Tayak had said no. The ground had laid these things down from the bottom up, over centuries. They would go back top to bottom, each layer receiving its object before the next layer was sealed. The ground's logic, not theirs.

Wren had not argued further. She had made a note on the inventory sheet and reordered the column.

The larger gorget came first — the one from stratum three, the one with the spiral incised deep enough that the groove still held a dark line of burial soil Adaeze had not fully cleaned, because Tayak had asked her not to. The polished face was cool through the nitrile gloves, slightly concave, smooth as old bone. Tayak held it for a moment in both hands before she crouched at the edge of the trench. The carved spiral caught the pale morning light and threw it back without warmth.

She photographed it in situ — the camera on the bracket, the scale card beside it, the unit number visible in the frame. She did this the same way she had done it eleven months ago when the gorget came out: two angles, one overhead, one oblique. The shutter sound was the same sound it had always been. Then she lifted the camera off the bracket and handed it up to Wren, and picked up the gorget, and placed it face-up in the prepared soil at the correct depth, in the correct orientation, north-south as the context sheet showed.

The soil received it without ceremony. That was the right word. Without ceremony — meaning without drama, without sound, without any acknowledgment from the earth that something had been returned to it. The gorget simply lay there, in the dark soil, looking the same as it had looked in the conservation tray.

Tayak photographed it one final time. Then she began to backfill.

Wren handed down the screened soil in the bucket — the same soil that had come out of this layer, kept separate and labeled, sifted clean of root intrusion. Tayak worked it in with her gloved hands, pressing it gently around the gorget's edges, then filling above it in gradual increments, tamping each inch down with the flat of her palm before adding the next. The soil was cold and slightly damp and smelled of iron and old leaves. Under her palm it gave at first, springy, then firmed as the layers settled. She did not rush. She had promised herself she would not rush.

The smaller gorget — stratum four, older, the one with the weeping eye motif barely legible under the soil staining — went into the layer below. The chalky smell of old shell rose briefly when she unwrapped the tissue, a dry, mineral smell, faintly like the inside of a very old cabinet. She held it the same way she had held the first, in both hands, before she placed it. She photographed it. She backfilled.

Wren worked the inventory sheet without being asked — checking off each object, noting the time, the depth, the orientation. Her handwriting was small and precise. She did not annotate beyond what the form required.

The pipe bowl was next.

Tayak had not expected the pipe bowl to be difficult. It was the object she felt least personal connection to — a beautiful piece of carved steatite, dark-fired, the bore still faintly blackened with ancient residue, but an object whose story she knew only through Matta's name in the chain, and Matta was not her direct line, or not that she could trace. She had thought the pipe bowl would be the straightforward one.

She was wrong.

When she lifted it from the padded tray and held it in her palm, it warmed quickly — the soapstone taking her body heat with a speed that startled her, so that within thirty seconds the bowl felt as if it had been sitting in a living hand for hours. She stood at the edge of the trench holding it and felt her throat close.

She did not cry. She photographed it. She placed it in the prepared depression in the correct layer, at the correct depth, oriented with the bowl mouth facing south as the original context sheet showed. She photographed it again. She backfilled.

Wren said nothing. She checked the sheet.

The antler figure came out of its case and Tayak turned it in the light before she crouched. Under the LED work lamp Wren had angled into the trench, the outer cortex of the antler showed its centuries of mineralization — warm brown shading to orange at the edges, the inner core a chalky gray where the soil had worked into the porous channels. The carved lines were shallow but deliberate, the kind of deliberateness that comes from someone who knew exactly what they were making and had made things like it before. When Tayak rubbed her thumb lightly across the surface, a fine pale powder came away on the glove. She did not wipe it off.

She photographed it. She placed it. She filled the layer.

The mound's interior was closing. Above her, the sky had gone from ash to a thin, cold blue. A red-winged blackbird called once from the direction of the creek and fell silent. The frost had gone off the grass while she worked, and the mound's surface now showed a darker patch where the trench had disturbed the soil — richer in color than the surrounding turf, slightly steaming in the cold air, as if the earth were breathing.

She climbed out of the trench and stood at the edge and looked down at the closed layers. Then she looked at Wren.

"The pendant," she said.

Wren opened the padded case.

The copper pendant lay on its foam bed, mottled green and brown, the malachite patina thick and uneven across the face. At the edges, where Adaeze had worked carefully to remove a later crust of corrosion, there were bright scar-like areas of exposed metal — not bright exactly, but less dark, showing the hammered texture of the original cold-working, slightly rough under the surface oxidation. Under the work lamp, one edge caught the light and showed a flash of dull reddish copper, the color of something that had been pressed and shaped by hands two thousand years ago and had not entirely forgotten it.

Tayak did not pick it up immediately. She looked at it for a long moment.

She thought about the woman at the bottom of the chain. The woman whose name Nora had held for sixty-two years and spoken yesterday, here, at this trench, in a voice so quiet Tayak had had to lean in to catch it. The woman who had placed this pendant in the earth when her daughter died, because grief needs somewhere to go and she had chosen the ground. The woman who was Tayak's ancestor — not metaphorically, not culturally in the broad sense, but directly, in the chain of blood and story that ran from that burial up through every name Nora had said yesterday, through Yanu and Ranu and Sena and forward through two thousand years of people who had kept the name alive by saying it.

Taya.

She picked up the pendant.

The cold-hammered surface was rough through the nitrile gloves, slightly uneven, nothing like cast metal. She turned it once in her hands. She photographed it — two angles, scale card, unit number. Then she lowered herself into the trench for the last time.

The deepest chamber was narrow. The wooden shoring they had used during excavation was already out — removed yesterday, as protocol required, so that only soil and the original stone-set walls of the feature remained. The space smelled of damp earth and something older underneath, something she had no word for. She had to crouch low to reach the correct depth.

She placed the pendant face-up in the prepared soil at the base of the feature, in the original orientation the context sheet showed — the suspension hole at the top, the pendant's face toward the sky it would not see again. She pressed it gently into the soil until it was level. The dark earth came up around its edges.

She photographed it one final time.

Then she sat back on her heels and looked at it for a moment — the green-brown disc in the dark soil, smaller than her palm, older than every institution that had ever tried to claim this ground. The work lamp threw a hard light across it. One edge still showed that faint reddish flash.

Tayak had not let anyone else carry the case since she had loaded it herself the night before. The figure was wrapped in acid-free tissue inside a padded interior, and she unwrapped it now in the confined space of the chamber, working carefully in the inadequate light. The black walnut had gone dark with age and soil staining — deep brown at the grain, almost black in the recessed tool marks, the carved lines still precise after however many centuries had passed over them. The surface smelled faintly of the Paraloid consolidant Adaeze had applied, a chemical tang that sat over the deeper smell of old wood and old earth and something she could only call time.

Seven cuts on the raised arm. She had counted them eleven months ago, kneeling in this same ground, when the figure first came out of the soil. She counted them again now. Seven.

She did not photograph the figure in the conservation case. She photographed it in her hands, in the chamber, with the pendant visible in the soil below it. Then she set the camera aside.

She looked at the figure for a long time.

Her hands were steady. She was aware of this because she had been afraid they would not be, and they were, and the steadiness felt less like composure than like something her body had decided to do without consulting her. Her face was doing something else. She did not try to manage it.

She set it upright first, the way the context sheet showed it had been found — seated, the raised arm angled slightly outward, the seven cuts facing up. The earth was cold. It was the same cold she had felt eleven months ago, the same weight, the same particular resistance of ground that has been undisturbed for a very long time and is not refusing you but is simply heavy with everything it has held.

She filled the chamber.

She worked in silence, both hands, pressing the screened soil in gradual layers, tamping each one down before adding the next. Wren handed the buckets down without being asked and took the empties back up without comment. The work was slow. Tayak did not rush it. The soil built up in the chamber in the same order it had originally lain — darker at the base, lighter and sandier above, following the stratigraphic sequence the field maps showed, each layer going back to the depth where it had come from.

When the chamber was full to the level of the surrounding stratum, Tayak climbed out of the trench for the last time.

She stood at the edge. The trench was now a shallow depression, the filled chamber indistinguishable from the surrounding backfill except by color — the disturbed soil still darker, still slightly steaming in the cold morning air. In a season, the grass would cover it. In two seasons, you would have to know where to look.

Wren was writing on the inventory sheet. The time. The depth. The final orientation of each object. Her pen moved without hesitation.

Tayak pulled off her right glove and crouched at the trench's edge and pressed her bare hand flat against the closed earth.

The soil was cold and slightly damp and smelled of iron. Under her palm it was firm now — the layers had settled as she worked, the springiness gone, the earth solid and close-grained and real. A thin mist rose from the darker patch, slow, without drama.

She kept her hand there.

She kept her hand there and did not move.

The cold came through her palm and up into her wrist. The soil was firm against her skin — not the resistance of something closed against her, but the resistance of something full. She had felt that distinction before, in eleven months of working this ground, and she felt it now.

"Taya."

Her voice came out low and level. She had not planned how it would sound. It sounded like a name. It sounded like something returning to the place it had come from.

"Yanu."

"Let's close the trench," she said.

The backfill went in by shovel and by hand.

Tayak had screened the excavated soil into three piles in September — the topsoil stripped from the upper twenty centimeters, the construction-phase fill below it, the darker organic-rich matrix from the base layer — and they had been sitting under canvas tarps at the field's north edge through October and into November, waiting. She had known, when she covered them, that this was what they were waiting for.

They started with the base-layer soil. Dark, almost black, with a clay-rich weight that clung to the shovel blade and required a deliberate shake to release. It smelled the way the trench had smelled at its deepest — iron and wet leaves and something older underneath, the particular mineral sharpness that Tayak associated now, and would always associate, with the two-thousand-year-old ground surface. She moved it carefully, in layers, tamping each pass with the flat of the shovel before adding the next. Wren worked beside her without instruction, matching the rhythm, not rushing. They did not speak about what they were covering. They did not speak about anything.

The construction-phase fill went in next — lighter in color, sandier, with small stones and flecks of crushed shell that caught the flat November light. Tayak worked it in with her hands as much as with the shovel, pressing it down into the corners of the chamber void, feeling for air pockets the way Carver had taught her, because an air pocket became a collapse and a collapse became a sinkhole and a sinkhole became a question she did not want anyone asking in ten years. She found two pockets and worked them out. Her knuckles ached in the cold. She did not stop.

Wren carried the topsoil in the buckets they had used all season — the orange five-gallon ones with the cracked handles, the yellow one that leaked if you tilted it past forty-five degrees. She set each bucket at the trench edge and Tayak spread the soil with her hands, working it level, reading the grade by feel and by eye. The topsoil was looser than the fill below, richer, and it went down darker than the surrounding turf because it was still damp from yesterday's rain. In a few weeks the color would even out. In spring the grass would begin to come back through it, the native fescues and the plantain and the clover that had been there before, their seeds still in the soil, waiting.

At some point — she could not have said when — Tayak became aware that her face was wet.

Not recently wet. The kind of wet that had been happening for a while and had not required her attention until now, the way a sound becomes audible only when you stop expecting silence. She did not stop working. She did not wipe her face. She pressed another layer of topsoil flat with both hands and felt the slight spring of it settling, the resistance giving way as the fill below compacted under the weight.

Wren was six feet to her left, spreading soil along the trench's eastern edge with a trowel. She did not look up. She had not looked up in a while, which meant she had already noticed and had made a decision about it, and the decision was the right one.

They worked for two hours. The sun moved behind a bank of cloud and the light went flat and grey, the kind of November light that made distances hard to judge. The blackbird had gone quiet. From the creek bottom came the sound of wind in the dry cattails — a long, papery rush that rose and fell without settling. Somewhere on the county road a truck slowed and then accelerated, the gear change carrying cleanly in the cold air, and then it was gone.

They lifted the shoring boards out of the trench walls and stacked them against the equipment trailer. They pulled the pump intake hose and coiled it. They broke down the temporary screen frames — the two-by-two lumber Elspeth had built in September, the quarter-inch mesh still holding a fine dark dust in its corners — and laid them flat in the trailer bed. Tayak folded the canvas tarps that had covered the soil piles, the canvas stiff with cold and heavy with absorbed damp, and loaded them without thinking about the fact that she was loading them, her hands doing the work while some other part of her attention stayed at the closed trench.

She carried the last tarp to the trailer and set it down and turned around.

The trench was gone.

That was not quite right — the ground was not perfectly smooth, not yet, there was still a slight depression along the chamber's long axis where the fill had not fully settled, and the color of the turned soil was darker than the surrounding turf, still visibly disturbed. But the opening was gone. The chamber was closed. The equipment was out. The shoring was out. There was only earth, and the slight darkness of recently turned earth, and the low grass-covered rise of the mound itself beyond it, and the line of bare oaks at the field's edge with their branches stripped and their roots going down.

Tayak stood at the trailer and looked at it.

She had wanted this. She had known, for eleven months, that this was what the work was for — not the opening, not the discovery, but the closing. The return. She had filed the paperwork for it and fought for the authority to do it and stood in front of the commission and argued that the tribe had the right to make this decision and no other body did. She had wanted it completely and without ambivalence, and she had been right to want it, and it was done.

She had not known that it would feel like this.

It was not the same as the first grief — the one she had carried for eleven months, the one that lived in the name she had said into the ground this morning, the one that was two thousand years old and also nine years old and also the age of a woman who had stood in a colonial assembly with ink drying on her palm. That grief had a shape she knew. She had learned to carry it without putting it down.

This was something else. The opening had been a wound and also a window. She had looked through it for eleven months. She had seen things through it that she could not have seen any other way — the pendant face-up in the dark clay, the wooden figure with its raised arm, the names going down through the layers like a root system, each one connected to the one below. The opening had let in light and air and her hands and her trowel and her notebook and Wren's careful eyes and Nora's voice on the phone at four in the morning and the name that was not a common word. It had let in everything.

And now it was closed, as it should be, as she had wanted, and the light was out, and the air was out, and the window was a wall again, and the wall was the point, and she had not known until this moment that closing the right thing could feel like losing it.

She stood at the trailer for a long time.

Wren came to stand beside her. Not close — a foot of space between them, the way Wren always managed distance, precise and undemanding. She was looking at the closed ground too.

The darker patch was already beginning to lighten at its edges as the surface dried in the cold air. By afternoon it would be hard to see from twenty feet. By spring it would be invisible. By the time anyone who had not been here today walked past this field, there would be nothing to see but a low rise covered in grass, the way there had always been a low rise covered in grass, the way there would be a low rise covered in grass for as long as the ground held.

Tayak's face was still wet. She became aware of this again, the way you become aware of the cold when you stop moving. She lifted her hand and wiped her face with the back of her wrist, her glove leaving a faint smear of dark soil across her cheekbone. She did not try to fix it.

"It looks like ground," she said.

Wren said, "Yes."

Neither of them said anything else for a while. The wind moved through the dry grass at the mound's edge. The flat grey light held. A killdeer called from somewhere near the creek — its two-note cry, the one that sounded like a question with no particular answer expected — and then it called again, and then it was quiet.

Tayak pulled her notebook from her coat pocket. She opened it to the last entry — the closing log, the artifact return sequence, the final provenience notes, the time and date and weather and the names of the people present. She read it through once. She had written it carefully this morning, before the ceremony, in the way Carver had taught her: write what you did, not what you felt, because what you felt would be in the writing whether you put it there or not. The entry was correct. The record was complete.

She closed the notebook.

The closed ground was already, even now, beginning to look like the earth around it. Not identical — not yet — but moving toward it, the way a scar moves toward skin, the difference diminishing season by season until only someone who knew where to look could find the line.

She knew where to look.

She put the notebook in her pocket and picked up the last of the equipment — the line level, the measuring tape, the two trowels — and carried them to the trailer and set them down. She heard Wren close the trailer's rear gate, the latch clicking twice before it caught.

Tayak looked at the closed mound one more time. The low rise. The bare oaks beyond it. The pale sky above the tree line, the clouds moving slowly east. The darker patch at the trench's edge, shrinking as she watched, the edges going the color of the surrounding turf.

She turned away.

She walked to the truck, opened the driver's door, and got in. She sat for a moment with her hands on the wheel, not starting the engine. Through the windshield she could see the mound — the whole of it, the low grass-covered rise, the way it sat in the landscape like something the landscape had always included, which it had, which it always would.

The loss of the opening sat in her chest alongside everything else she was carrying. It did not displace any of it. It simply took its place in the stack, the way layers do, each one resting on the one below, the weight distributed, the whole thing holding.

She started the engine.

The truck idled for a minute, maybe two, the heater ticking as it woke. Then Tayak turned it off.

She got out. She did not have a reason she could have named. She walked to the back of the truck and dropped the tailgate and sat on it. The metal was cold through her field pants. She put her hands in her lap and looked at the mound.

Wren came around from the passenger side and sat beside her. Not close. The same foot of space. She did not ask.

The sky had gone the color of old pewter, the clouds thickening from the west, and the light was flat and even and without shadow. The mound sat in it the way it had always sat — the low rise, the dry grass, the bare oaks standing back from it as if they knew to give it room. The darker patch at the trench line had shrunk to a seam. Another hour and the cold air would take the rest of it.

Tayak looked at it and thought about Yanu, who had pressed her palms to this ground and said a name into it. She thought about Ranu, who had done the same thing at Yanu's grave, learning the gesture from watching her, the way you learn a thing you were not taught. She thought about the girl with the cup — the seventeen-year-old with dry eyes and a carelessly made vessel and a grief she did not know where to put — and about Yanu giving her something to do with her hands, a ceremony that was also an instruction: *say the name, press the palms flat, give the ground what it can hold*. She thought about Tessa standing here alone in the third year, her brother already past her on the road, placing the gorget in the dark earth and reciting the names of the people who had stood here before her, adding her own name to the end of the list without knowing whether the list would ever be recited again. She thought about Matta, who had sold pipe bowls to an English factor for eleven years to buy the cover that let him keep doing what the cover was supposed to replace. She thought about Saya with ink drying on her palm. She thought about Ida at a manual typewriter in the summer of 1962, typing six pages and then deliberately leaving the most important thing off the page, writing *I will say it in person when the time comes* because she understood that some things are not for paper.

She thought about her grandmother, who had picked up the phone on the second ring at four in the morning because she had been awake since four in the morning, who had held a name for sixty-two years without saying it on the phone, who had come to the site with Kimi driving and stood at the edge of the trench and said the name into the ground in a voice Tayak had never heard her use before — not louder, not softer, but aimed, the way you aim something you have been carrying a long time and finally know where to set it down.

None of these people had known whether anyone would need what they were keeping. That was the thing. Tessa had not known. Matta had not known. Saya had buried the petition in oilskin and had not known if anyone would ever find it, and someone had, two hundred and fifty years later, in a trench in the rain. The girl with the cup had not known that the woman from upriver would preserve her exact words — *you say the name, the name is everything* — and teach them to her daughter, and that the daughter would teach them to hers, the ceremony traveling forward through households and seasons and centuries without anyone organizing it, without anyone deciding it was important enough to keep, just person after person doing the thing they had been shown because it was the thing you did, because the ground was there and the names were there and you said them.

The wind moved through the dry grass at the mound's edge. The killdeer had stopped. From somewhere beyond the tree line came the low drone of a plane, descending toward somewhere, fading west.

Tayak did not say any of this aloud. It was not the kind of thing you said aloud. It was the kind of thing you sat with on the cold tailgate of a truck in the flat grey light until it settled into you the way layers settle, each one resting on the one below, the weight becoming familiar, the whole thing becoming the ground you stand on.

She was aware of Wren beside her. The foot of space between them. Wren's hands in her lap, her field jacket zipped to the chin, her eyes on the mound. Wren had been here for four months. She had read the context before the central feature. She had fitted the pump intake hose in the rain and said nothing about it. She had apologized in the trench without asking for anything back. She had been a careful, precise, undemanding presence for four months, and she was being that now, and Tayak was grateful for it in a way she had not found words for and did not try to find words for now.

After a while, Wren said, quietly, "I'm glad I was here."

The words went out into the flat grey air and the mound received them or did not, and the grass moved, and the clouds moved slowly east.

Tayak did not answer immediately. She looked at the seam of darker earth at the trench line, already nearly gone. She looked at the low rise, the way it sat in the landscape like something the landscape had always included. She thought about the name — not the legal chain, not the evidentiary chain, not the chain of custody logged in Field Notebook 11 — but the human one. The chain of people who had held the names and passed them forward without knowing whether anyone would ever need them, each one choosing to hold on anyway, in the dark, without a guarantee, because the ground was there and the names belonged to it and someone had to be the one who remembered.

"I am too," she said.

The wind came through the grass again. The bare oaks stood back from the mound the way they always had. The pale sky held its light, even and without shadow, the clouds continuing east.

Neither of them spoke again.

The darker patch at the trench line was almost gone now, the surface color evening out, the disturbed earth drawing back toward the color of the surrounding turf, the seam diminishing, the difference shrinking season by season the way a scar shrinks toward skin until only someone who knew where to look could find the line.

Tayak knew where to look.

She had always known.

She sat on the cold tailgate with her hands in her lap and the mound in front of her and the flat grey light holding everything equally — the closed ground, the dry grass, the bare oaks, the pale sky, the woman beside her, the chain of names going down through the layers like a root system, each one connected to the one below, the whole thing holding — and she did not move, and she did not speak, and the ground was there, and she was there, and the ground knew she was there, and she knew the ground knew, and that was enough, and it had always been enough, and it would be enough long after she was gone, the names traveling forward without her, the way they had always traveled, person to person, hand to hand, mouth to ground, the way water finds its level, the way a root finds dark, the way a name, said once into the right place, does not stop.
