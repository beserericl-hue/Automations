The fourth stratum smelled different from the ones above it.

Tayak had learned to trust that. The earth changed character as you went down, each layer carrying its own chemistry, its own slow decay. The third stratum had smelled of iron and old root-rot, the particular damp of ground that had been worked by human hands and then sealed. This one, two inches deeper, carried something older. Mineral. Almost cold, the way a stone cellar is cold even in August.

She was on her knees in the trench, back aching, the morning light coming in at a low angle that was already failing her. It was not yet nine. She had been here since six.

The trowel moved in short strokes, pulling loose soil toward her, and she transferred it to the screen bucket with a brush no wider than her thumb. Wren had offered to stay. Tayak had sent her to run the east transect instead, because she needed this part alone, and because she did not yet know what she was looking for, and because the moment you put a second person in a trench you started performing for them, started narrating, started deciding what things meant before you had held them long enough to know.

Her field notebook lay open on the plank beside her, red cover gone dark with morning damp. She had written nothing in the last forty minutes.

The brush found resistance.

Not rock. Rock pushed back differently, with a solidity that traveled up through the handle. This was a slight, specific drag, the way a brush catches on something smooth that the surrounding soil does not want to release. She stopped. She set the brush down and pressed the pad of her index finger against the place, reading through the nitrile glove.

Curved. Flat. Cool even through the glove.

She picked up the brush again and worked outward from the point of contact in small arcs, the way Cecile Marant had taught her. Never toward the object, always away from it, let it come to you. The soil fell back in dry, fine increments. A rim appeared. Then a surface. Then the shallow channel of an incised line, filled with something darker than the surrounding shell.

She sat back on her heels and looked at it for a long moment without touching it.

A gorget. A shell disk, maybe nine centimeters across, lying face-up in the matrix as if it had been set there deliberately, which it had, which someone eight centuries ago had done with intention and care. The surface was not the pale, almost chalky white of the gorget from the third stratum. This one had gone the color of old ivory, yellow-cream deepest in the recessed areas where the incising cut below the polished face.

The design was not the cross-and-circle she had expected. It was a bird, or something that had once been a bird and had become, over the hands that made it, something more than a bird. The wings were spread in a way that suggested both flight and ceremony, the feathers rendered as a series of curved parallel lines that swept outward from a central body and then curled back on themselves. A forked mark ran beneath what might have been an eye. The whole image had the quality of something that meant more than it depicted, the way a word in another language sometimes carries a weight that the translation cannot hold.

She told herself to stop. She was tired and reading into it.

But the carving style was not the style of Tessa's gorget, which she had documented eleven days ago in this same trench, eighteen inches higher. That one had been simpler: a suspension hole, a pair of concentric rings, a restraint that spoke of a maker working within a tradition but not at its center. This one had been made by someone who knew exactly what they were doing and for whom the making was a form of argument.

She photographed it in place before she touched it, macro lens close enough to catch the faint reddish residue clinging to the deepest incisions. Iron-oxide, probably, rubbed into the lines to make them read. The camera's shutter clicked in the quiet of the trench and she checked the image on the screen, tilting the camera to confirm the raking light was doing its work. The engraved lines showed clearly. The pigment traces showed. The suspension hole at the top edge showed the rounded, worn smoothness of long use, not the sharp edges you got from a fresh drill, but the softened lip of something that had hung on a cord for years, maybe decades, before it was placed here.

She took eleven photographs. Then she sat back and pressed her hands flat against her thighs and made herself breathe.

The stratigraphy was clean. She had been watching it for three days as she worked down through the fourth layer, and it had not been disturbed. No intrusion cuts, no mixing, no evidence of later hands reaching in. The matrix directly above this object was consistent with the rest of the stratum: fine-grained loam with a high clay content, the kind of compaction you got from deliberate fill placed all at once rather than gradual accumulation. Someone had dug down to this depth, placed the gorget, and filled in above it. That was a burial act. That was intention.

But intention was not a date. And a date she could defend was the only thing that mattered right now.

She looked at the incised lines again. The forked-eye mark. The curved feathers turning back on themselves. She had seen this iconographic vocabulary in publications, in museum catalogs, in the Smithsonian's online collection. Mississippian, broadly, which meant the Southeastern Ceremonial Complex, which meant a horizon that ran from roughly 900 CE to 1600 CE and was most fully expressed, in terms of this specific visual language, in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries.

That was not a date she could put in her field notes. That was a hunch built on pattern recognition, and Voss would take a hunch and turn it into a methodological failure in print.

She picked up the notebook and wrote: *Stratum 4. Approx. 9:07 a.m. Shell disk artifact, face-up, undisturbed matrix. Diameter est. 9 cm. Incised design, possible pigment traces in grooves. Suspension hole, worn edges consistent with use-wear. Iconographic style: pending comparative analysis. Dating: pending stratigraphic confirmation and radiocarbon from associated organics. Do not overclaim.*

She underlined *do not overclaim* with a pressure that left a groove in the paper beneath.

Her hands were steadier than she felt. That was something. Thirty-one hours without real sleep, a legal situation she had not fully resolved, a university watching her with the careful attention of an institution deciding whether to step back or step in, and her hands were still steady.

She looked at the gorget again. At the bird that was more than a bird. At the lines that someone had cut with a tool she would never find, in a tradition she could only partially read, for a purpose she could name in general terms but not in the specific, intimate terms that the maker would have used.

She reached for her phone.

She photographed it again first. Not because she needed more images. Eleven was enough for any publication. But the act of photographing gave her hands something to do while her mind settled. She switched the camera app to manual focus and moved the lens to within four inches of the suspension hole, close enough to see the mineral crust that had formed in the groove where the cord had once rested. The hole's lip was smooth on both sides, rounded by years of friction, the shell worn to the color of old teeth. She took the shot. Then she moved to the opposite edge and took another, angling the phone so the raking light from the east caught the incised lines across the bird's wing.

The shutter sound was the only noise in the trench.

She set the phone down on the acid-free foam pad beside the gorget and picked up the red notebook. Carver's rule: red because you can find it in a site bucket. She had never once lost a red notebook, which she took as evidence that some advice was simply correct.

*Stratum 4, Sub-layer B. Context 4-07. 9:14 a.m.*

She wrote the depth: forty-one centimeters below the established datum for Stratum 3. She wrote the orientation: face-up, incised surface toward the sky, suspension hole pointing roughly north-northeast, which was toward the creek. She noted the matrix: fine-grained loam, high clay content, no visible intrusion cuts from above. She drew a quick plan-view sketch, four inches square, showing the gorget's position relative to the quadrant stakes. She was not a gifted illustrator but she was a precise one. The sketch showed what it needed to show.

*Diameter: approx. 9.2 cm. Thickness: est. 4-5 mm at edge, slightly greater at center. Color: ivory to light tan, with darker patina in recessed areas. Incised design: central figure, possible raptor, wings spread, forked-eye motif below figure. Parallel curved lines on wings, curling back on themselves at outer edge. Pigment trace visible in deepest incisions, reddish-brown, iron-oxide probable. Suspension hole: single, near upper edge, approximately 5 mm diameter. Hole edges: worn, rounded, consistent with extended use-wear. No obvious breakage or repair.*

She paused and looked at what she had written. Then she added:

*Iconographic style: complex, high-investment manufacture. Stylistic comparanda pending. Do not overclaim.*

She underlined the last sentence. Then she called up to the surface.

"Wren."

A pause. The sound of someone setting something down carefully.

"Coming."

"The rest of the surface is intact. The abrasion is localized to the hole. That's use-wear."

She handed the pen back and they both looked at the gorget.

The bird's wings curved outward from the body and then turned back, the feathers rendered as a series of lines cut with something fine and hard and patient. The forked mark beneath the eye caught the morning light differently from the rest of the incised surface, slightly deeper, the iron-oxide residue clinging in the groove like a stain that had been put there on purpose. Which it had. Someone had rubbed pigment into these lines to make them read at a distance, to make the image visible to someone who was not holding the gorget close but seeing it worn, hanging on a cord, resting against a chest or throat.

This had not been made to sit in the ground.

Tayak set that thought aside. It was true but it was not useful right now.

She picked up the phone again and switched to the camera, fitted the clip-on macro lens she kept in her vest pocket, and moved through the four standard angles: plan view from directly above, then north edge, south edge, oblique from the northwest to catch the relief across the wing lines. She placed the color calibration card and the metric scale in each frame. She checked the histogram on each shot to confirm the exposure was not blowing out the highlights on the polished surface. The gorget's ivory-tan shell had a faint sheen where the polish had survived, and overexposed images would lose the surface banding, the subtle striations from the original whelk's structure that were still visible if the light was right.

She got the light right. It took three attempts on the oblique angle, adjusting the small LED panel she used for raking shots until the incised lines cast shadows thin as thread.

Wren watched without commenting. She had learned, in two months of working with Tayak, that the documentation phase was not a time for conversation. It was a time for the artifact to be recorded as it was, not as anyone wanted it to be. That was the rule. The rule applied regardless of what either of them was thinking about, regardless of what had been said or not said in the past twelve hours, regardless of the text message Tayak had not yet acknowledged.

Tayak had not yet acknowledged it because she did not know what to do with it, and she had learned long ago that the worst decisions she had ever made were the ones she made before she was ready to make them.
