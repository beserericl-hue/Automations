The fire had burned to coals.

Saya could tell without opening her eyes — the heat against her face had changed from the bright push of flame to the slower, deeper warmth of a coal bed, the kind that would last until someone woke to feed it. Around her she heard the breathing of the camp: Okwi beside her, slow and even; her daughter Mika two body-lengths away on her own mat, one small hand flung out from under the deerskin; and farther, behind the hanging mat that divided the house, old Pakan's irregular rasp.

She did not move.

The petition was folded against her sternum, tucked inside her wrap where her body heat had kept it through the night. She could feel its edges — the paper was stiff with cold despite her warmth, the fold lines sharp as a blade's back. She had written the draft herself, in the careful English she had learned from the Dutch trader Hendrick's wife and then sharpened through years of watching colonial men write things down and then act as if the writing made the things true. She had learned that lesson. She intended to use it.

Three days.

The survey party was three days from the mound. She knew this because Lenne's son had come in two nights ago, half-frozen, with the news that a chain carrier and two witnesses had been seen at the Patuxent crossing, moving south with a warrant in the surveyor's saddlebag. She had not told Okwi the source, only the fact. Okwi had said nothing, which was how he expressed the things that frightened him.

Two days' travel to the agent's house at Port Tobacco, if the creek ice held. One day to be received, or turned away.

The arithmetic was possible. She had been telling herself this since the night before last, and she almost believed it.

She opened her eyes.

The house was dark except for the coals, which threw a low red light across the earth floor, the bark walls, the stored baskets of dried corn hanging in their net above the sleeping platform. Smoke had settled in the upper reaches of the house, a grey ceiling that caught the ember-glow and held it. The air smelled of oak smoke and the rendered bear fat Mika had gotten on her blanket last week, and underneath both, faint and persistent, the smell of the ink on the petition — a sharp, faintly sour smell, like green acorns, that she had come to associate with the colonial world's way of doing things.

She reached up and touched the bead.

It lay at the base of her throat, against the hollow where her collarbone met. Glass, blue as a winter sky just before dark, smooth and cold from the night air. Her mother had worn it on a cord of twisted plant fiber, and when her mother died the cord had been replaced twice, but the bead had not been removed. Saya had worn it so long she could not feel the cord anymore, only the occasional cold weight of the bead itself when she moved quickly or when the temperature dropped and the glass held the cold longer than her skin could warm it.

Her mother had gotten it from a Lenape woman at a trading meeting on the Potomac, years before Saya was born. The Lenape woman had said it came from across the water, made in a city Saya could not imagine, carried in a ship's hold to a Dutch trading post and then west and then south through a dozen hands until it reached the Potomac and her mother's throat. Saya had always found this fact both astonishing and unsurprising. Things traveled. They ended up where they ended up.

She pressed the bead once against her collarbone, then let it go.

Beside her, Okwi breathed. He was lying on his back, one arm across his chest, the other flung wide in the way he slept when he was genuinely at rest and not merely lying still to avoid a conversation. She had learned the difference years ago. Right now he was truly asleep, which meant he had not spent the night calculating as she had. This was either a sign of trust or a sign that he had already decided the outcome and was resting in preparation for what came after.

She did not know which. She had stopped asking.

He would say, if she woke him: *You have already decided. Why wake me to agree?* And he would be right, and she would be angry that he was right, and they would waste an hour they did not have. So she let him sleep.

The petition pressed against her ribs as she sat up, careful not to disturb the sleeping mat. The paper crinkled once — a small, dry sound, loud in the quiet house. Mika did not stir. Pakan's rasp continued behind the hanging mat.

Saya sat still for a moment, her wrap around her shoulders, her feet not yet on the cold earth floor. The fire needed feeding. She would feed it before she left, or Mika would wake to a cold house and there would be no one to explain why.

But first she sat.

She had written the petition in the formal English that colonial documents required, the kind that began *humbly sheweth* and named the petitioners as *your humble petitioners*, as if the act of asking required you to make yourself small before you could be heard. She had copied the phrases from a document she had seen two years ago, a petition from a group of English planters asking the assembly for relief from a disputed survey. She had thought: if they can use this language to protect their land, I can use it to protect ours. The thought had felt clean and sharp at the time.

It still felt clean. She was less certain about the sharp.

The petition described the mound and the ground around it. It named the bounds in the English way — *beginning at a marked white oak standing on the north side of the creek, thence running north forty perches to a blazed chestnut* — because she had learned that the colonial assembly did not recognize land described in any other terms. It used the word *reservation*, which she had found in an older treaty document, because that word had legal weight in the assembly's records. It asked that the ground be recognized as belonging to her people before any patent could be issued, before any surveyor's chain could be drawn across it.

She had written it three times. The draft against her chest was the second version, with corrections in the margin where she had crossed out *humbly pray* and written *respectfully petition*, because she had decided that *pray* was a word she would not give them.

The third version, the fair copy, was not yet written. She would need a steady hand and a warm fire and enough time to let the ink dry before folding, because iron-gall ink in cold weather ran thick and slow and blotted if you hurried it.

She had all of that at the council fire. She did not have it here.

She put her feet on the ground.

The earth was cold through her moccasins, the kind of cold that had been in the ground since November and would not leave until April. She stood slowly, her knees stiff from the night on the mat, and reached for the stack of split oak beside the fire. She laid two pieces across the coals, careful and quiet, and watched the fire catch — first a thin curl of smoke, then a small tongue of flame, then the steady burn that would hold until morning.

Mika's hand, still flung out from under the deerskin, was close enough to the new warmth that in a few minutes it would be comfortable. Saya watched it for a moment.

Then she straightened, tucked the petition more firmly against her chest, and pulled her wrap tight.

Three days. Two days' travel. One chance.

She moved to the door of the house and pushed the mat aside and stepped out into the dark.

The cold hit her face like a hand — not a blow, but a firm, flat pressure that pushed the warmth of the house back into her skin and replaced it with something that smelled of wet leaves and frozen mud and the distant, low smell of the tidal creek two hundred yards to the east, where the ice would be thin at the edges and solid in the middle and she would have to test each step. The sky above the bare oaks was the dark grey of an hour before dawn, the stars fading at the edges but still bright overhead, and her breath rose in front of her face and disappeared.

She stood in the door of the house for one breath, two.

Then she turned toward the council fire and walked.

The path ran north along the creek, and Saya knew every root of it.

She moved at the pace her father had called the wolf-trot — not a run, not a walk, but the ground-eating rhythm a person could hold for half a day without breaking. Her moccasins found the frozen mud between the exposed oak roots and she did not look down. She had walked this path in the dark since she was old enough to follow her mother, and her feet remembered what her eyes could not see.

The cold was a living thing this morning. It pressed through her stroud-cloth mantle and found the gaps at her wrists, her throat, the place where her wrap crossed her chest. Beneath the wrap, folded against her sternum and held there by the pressure of her own arm, the petition crinkled faintly with each stride — a small, papery sound that she felt more than heard, like a second heartbeat running slightly wrong.

She breathed through her nose and watched her breath go white in the grey air before the trees swallowed it.

The petition had taken her four nights to draft. The ink had thickened in the cold and she had held the small clay pot near the coals to keep it flowing, and still the quill had caught and spat, leaving a dark blot above the words *humbly sheweth* that she had not been able to correct without smearing the line below. She had written around it. The words were there. *Your humble petitioners, being the people long settled upon the tract of land lying along the north branch of the creek known to the English as —* She had the English names. She had learned them from the surveyor's assistant who had come through two summers ago, a young man with a sunburned neck who had been willing to talk for a meal of roasted corn and dried fish, and who had not understood what she was memorizing.

*Metes and bounds.* She turned the phrase in her mouth as she ran, feeling the shape of it. *The bounds of the said tract beginning at a marked white oak standing on the north side of.* She had the distances in chains and perches, copied from the young man's field notes while he slept. She had the formulaic opening, the honorific address — *To His Excellency the Governor and the Honorable Council and Burgesses in Assembly met* — copied from a petition she had seen at the trading post, a planter's grievance about a neighbor's hogs, a document that had received a formal response within the month.

She was not a planter. She knew what that meant. She ran anyway.

The creek ice had cracked in the night, pushed up in long grey ridges where the water beneath had shifted. She crossed at the stepping stones below the bend, testing each one before she put her weight down. The stones were slick with a skin of new ice, and she went slowly here, arms out, the petition pressing against her ribs. One wrong step and the paper would be wet before she reached the mound.

On the far bank she stopped and listened.

The woods were still. Bare oak branches crossed the white sky in every direction, their smallest twigs precise and black as iron-gall ink. A woodpecker worked somewhere to the east, a dry mechanical hammering. A crow called once and went quiet. She heard nothing that sounded like men, no axe-blows on bark, no chain dragging through underbrush, no voices calling measurements to one another across the frozen ground.

Three days. Maybe less.

She had heard from the trader's wife — a Nanticoke woman who had married into the English settlement at the river bend and who told Saya things she was not supposed to know — that the new surveyor's party had left the county seat two mornings ago. The surveyor, a chain carrier, and two witnesses. Moving slowly in this cold, stopping to blaze trees, recording the measurements in a leather-bound book that would become a warrant and then a patent and then someone else's land. Moving toward the mound.

She knew what blazed trees meant. She had seen the scarred oaks along the southern boundary of the Calvert grant, the axe-marks dried and dark, the bark curled back from the wound. Once those marks were on the trees around the mound, the paper that followed would name the ground as someone else's and the paper she carried against her chest would mean nothing.

She started running again.

The mound appeared through the trees the way it always did — not suddenly, but gradually, the land rising in a shape too deliberate for accident, too gentle for a hill. The oaks grew around it but not on it, as though they understood something about the ground. Snow had settled in the hollows of the slope, white in the grey morning, and the frozen grass at the crown was the color of old bone.

Saya stopped.

She had not planned to stop. Her body simply did it — her feet planted on the frozen path, her breath coming in short white bursts, her eyes on the mound's crown where the grass bent in a wind she could not feel down here among the trees.

Her grandmother's grandmother had known what was in this ground. She had told Saya's grandmother, who had told Saya's mother, who had told Saya on a night she had been too young to understand and too old to forget. *This ground is not empty.* That was how the telling always began. *This ground is not empty, and neither are you.*

The bead at her throat had grown cold during the run. She pressed two fingers against it through the fabric of her wrap — the glass bead, blue as a winter sky, worn smooth by her mother's skin before it had worn smooth against hers. Her mother had never taken it off. Saya had not taken it off since the morning they buried her.

She stood at the mound for one breath. Two.

Then she ran.

The winter camp was another mile north, tucked into the hollow where the creek bent west and the ridge cut the worst of the wind. She smelled it before she saw it — woodsmoke first, then the deeper smell of a corn stew that had been on the fire since before dawn, rich and starchy, the smell of people living through winter with what they had put by. A dog barked twice and was hushed. Children's voices, high and quick, cut off when they heard her feet on the frozen path.

She came out of the trees into the firelight.

Three elders sat near the council fire, wrapped in their mantles, their faces turned toward her. Old Pakan on the left, his eyes already narrowed, already deciding. Wekam in the center, who had known her father. Lena on the right, who had helped her draft the first petition two years ago, the one that had gone unanswered.

Saya pulled the petition from inside her wrap and crouched down on the frozen ground.

She had no table. She set the folded paper between two stones to keep it flat, and the cold air pressed it smooth, and she looked up at the elders and said, in English first and then in her own language, what she needed from them before the day was done.

The fire had burned down to a flat bed of coals by the time Saya ducked through the lodge entrance, and the three elders were already there — which meant someone had told them she was coming, which meant someone had been watching the path. She filed that away and straightened up inside the smoke-dark warmth.

Cheta sat closest to the coals, his blanket pulled to his chin, a man who had been old for as long as Saya could remember. Nessa sat across from him, her hands busy with something in her lap, sinew or cord, her fingers never still. And Pakan — Pakan sat at the back, where the firelight barely reached him, his face arranged into the particular stillness he wore when he had already decided.

Saya had known Pakan's face her whole life. She did not let herself look at it yet.

She unslung the wrap from her shoulders, shook the cold off her hands, and knelt on the packed earth beside the fire. The petition was folded against her chest, inside her deerskin. She drew it out and felt the air in the lodge close around it — that raw, faintly acid smell the paper always carried, like iron left in rain, which still surprised her even now. She had handled colonial documents enough times to know the smell meant nothing. It still felt like a warning.

She spread the petition on the ground near the coals, weighting each corner with a stone from the ring of the fire. The paper was rag-made, stiff with cold, and it crackled as it flattened. The ink was dark against it — her own hand, copied twice already, the letters as clean as she could make them without a proper writing table.

"I need your marks," she said. She spoke in their language, not in English. "And your names as the agent will write them. And your word that the boundary described here is the boundary you know."

Cheta leaned forward and squinted at the document. He could not read English, but he had watched enough colonial surveyors work to understand what the lines and measurements meant — a tract described in chains and perches, beginning at a marked white oak on the north side of the creek, running south by southwest a hundred and sixty perches to a second marked oak, then east. His eyes moved along the text the way a man reads a path he has walked before.

"The oak on the north side," he said. "Which oak?"

"The one your father blazed. There is a second blaze on it now, from the last survey party. Theirs is higher. His is lower."

Cheta was quiet for a moment. Then he nodded once, reached for the small pot of iron-gall ink Saya had set beside the document, and held out his hand for the quill. She gave it to him. He dipped it carefully — she had told him, the first time, that you dip and then you wait one breath before you touch the paper, or the ink runs — and he made his mark in the space she had left. Not his name in English; that would come from the agent's hand. Just the mark that was his, the shape he had used on every document since the first treaty paper thirty years before.

The quill scratched. In the cold, the ink was thick, and the line came out heavier than she would have liked, but it was there.

Nessa set down her sinew without being asked. She was younger than Cheta by a decade, and she had been the one, four winters ago, to send her eldest son to learn the colonial court's procedures from a sympathetic Quaker trader in Annapolis. She understood what a petition was and what it was not. She understood, better than most, what it cost to write one and what it cost not to.

"The boundary is correct," she said, before she even took the quill. "I walked it with your mother the summer before she died."

Saya's hand went to the bead at her throat. She did not mean it to — it was a reflex, the way a tongue finds a sore tooth. The bead was smooth and cold from the run, just now beginning to warm against her fingers.

"I know," Saya said.

Nessa made her mark below Cheta's. Her line was lighter, more careful, the quill barely touching the paper.

Two marks. Saya looked at the space below Nessa's and then, finally, at Pakan.

He had not moved. The firelight caught the left side of his face and left the rest in dark, and his eyes were on her — not on the document, not on the coals, but on her face, the way a man looks at someone he is about to disappoint and wants them to know he is not sorry for it.

"Pakan," she said.

"I heard you." His voice was unhurried. "I heard everything you said."

"Then you know what I'm asking."

"I know what you're asking." He shifted his weight, and his blanket — stroud cloth, deep red, traded from a Dutch merchant two seasons back — pulled across his shoulders. "I'm telling you it won't work."

"You don't know that."

"I know what I've seen." He looked at the document on the ground, and for a moment something moved across his face — not contempt, exactly. More like grief that had hardened into certainty. "I watched your father carry a paper to the assembly. I watched your uncle carry a paper to the assembly. I watched the Mattawoman carry a paper to the assembly. You know what those papers did."

"This one is different. The boundary is—"

"The boundary." He said the word the way you repeat back something a child has said. "They will survey what they want to survey. They will mark what they want to mark. And when you stand before them with your paper, they will tell you that a Piscataway woman's petition does not carry the weight of three elder witnesses." He paused. "Or two."

The lodge was very quiet. Dry corn kernels were parching somewhere outside — she could hear them popping faintly against a clay pot — and a dog moved past the entrance, its claws clicking on frozen mud.

"Then give me three," Saya said.

Pakan looked at her for a long moment. "No," he said. "I won't put my mark on something that will be used to teach our children that the assembly can be trusted."

She could have argued. She had the arguments ready — she had been carrying them since before dawn, turning them over on the run, testing their weight. She knew what Pakan had seen. She knew what her father's paper had done, and her uncle's. She knew the assembly was not to be trusted, had never been to be trusted, would likely not be trusted now.

She also knew that the surveyors were three days away, and that two marks were what she had.

She picked up the quill and set it back in the ink pot. She lifted each stone from the corners of the petition, one by one, and the paper curled back toward its fold as if relieved. She folded it along the creases she had already made, three folds, the marks facing inward, and held it in both hands while the ink finished drying.

"I hear you," she said to Pakan.

He nodded once. It was not approval. It was acknowledgment that she had listened, which was, from Pakan, as much as she would get.

She tucked the document back inside her wrap, against her chest, beside the bead. The paper was cold from the ground. It would warm.

Cheta cleared his throat. "How long before the surveyors reach the creek?"

"Three days," Saya said. "Perhaps less."

He made a sound — not quite a word — and pulled his blanket tighter. Nessa had already picked up her sinew again, her fingers moving, her eyes on Saya with an expression that was not pity and not hope but something between the two, the look of a woman who has done what she can and is watching to see if it will be enough.

Saya stood. The smoke in the upper part of the lodge stung her eyes, and she dropped her gaze to the coals, where the light was cleanest. Two marks. The petition of your humble petitioners, humbly sheweth, bearing two marks and one absence, addressed to His Excellency the Governor and the Honorable Council and Burgesses in Assembly met, and worth whatever a Piscataway woman's word was worth in the year of their Lord sixteen hundred and ninety-three.

She ducked out through the entrance into the cold.

The air hit her face like water. She stood for a moment outside the lodge, letting her eyes adjust from firelight to the flat grey of a winter morning, and that was when she saw it — a shape at the edge of the tree line, just beyond the last lodge, standing still enough that she might have taken it for a birch trunk except that birch trunks did not breathe.

Okwi.

He had followed her after all.

The elders had gone back to their lodge. The fire had dropped to coals, throwing a low red light that barely reached the tree line.

Saya heard him before she saw him. Not footsteps — Okwi did not make footsteps when he chose not to — but the particular quality of stillness that meant someone was standing at the edge of a thing, deciding whether to enter it. She had known that stillness for eight years.

She did not look up from the documents.

"You followed me," she said.

"I was cold." He came into the firelight and crouched, not beside her but across from her, so the petition lay between them on the frozen ground. His face was unreadable in the way that meant he had already decided not to argue. She had learned to tell the difference between the silence that preceded a fight and the silence that had already swallowed one.

He looked at the two marks on the document. He did not ask about the third.

The fire shifted, and a knot of oak popped and sent up a brief column of sparks. Somewhere in the camp a dog barked once and went quiet. The cold pressed down on everything, flattening sound, and Saya could hear the faint scratch of her own breathing.

"What will you do," Okwi said, "when it fails?"

She had expected the question. She had expected it for months, which was why hearing it now, finally spoken, felt less like a blow and more like the moment a splinter works free.

"Write another," she said.

He looked at her. The firelight caught the left side of his face and left the right in shadow, and she thought, not for the first time, that he was a man who contained more than one opinion about everything.

"And when that fails?"

"Write another."

He was quiet. A green log at the fire's edge hissed and spat. The smell of woodsmoke and something savory — fat from the evening's pot, still warm in the coals — moved through the cold air and was gone.

Then Okwi looked at her throat. Not her face. The bead.

It lay in the hollow of her collarbone, dark blue glass, worn smooth from her mother's years of wearing it and her own. In the firelight it had no color at all, only a small, faint gleam, like a coal that had almost gone out.

"Your mother," he said, "would have done the same thing."

Saya waited.

"I cannot decide," he said, "whether that is a comfort or a warning."

She reached up and touched the bead with two fingers. It was cold from the night air, and then, as it always did, it began to warm against her skin. Her mother had worn it to every council, every trade meeting, every conversation with every colonial official who had looked through her as though she were river mist. Her mother had worn it until the year she stopped going to those meetings, and after that she had worn it in the house, and after that in bed, and then she had given it to Saya and said nothing about why.

"Both," Saya said. "It is both."

Okwi looked at her for a moment. Then, without ceremony, he sat down on the frozen ground beside the documents. He pulled his mantle tighter across his shoulders — good stroud wool, dark blue, the color of deep water, traded for three canoe paddles two winters ago — and reached across the petition to straighten one corner that had lifted in the cold air.

He did not say: *I will help you*. He simply picked up the quill she had set aside and held it near the fire's edge, warming the nib, waiting for the iron-gall ink in the small clay pot to loosen.

Saya watched him. She thought about saying something and decided against it.

The quill was a goose feather, trimmed at an angle with his small knife, the same knife he used on canoe ribs. He tested the nib against his thumbnail, found it satisfactory, dipped it carefully — not too deep, cold ink was treacherous — and looked up at her.

"Which copy," he said. "The clean one."

She smoothed the fresh sheet on the ground between them, weighting its corners with the same four stones she had used before. The paper was rag stock, expensive, the last she had from the trader at the landing, and she had been saving it for this. In the firelight it looked almost warm, almost cream-colored, though she knew in daylight it would be grey and a little damp from the cold.

"Here," she said, and pointed to the opening line.

He leaned forward and began to copy. His hand was not a scrivener's hand — the letters came slowly, each one deliberate, the quill scratching faintly against the paper's surface. But they came true. He had learned to write from the same source she had, the same patient winter afternoons, the same ink-stained fingers, and his hand on the page was careful in the way his hands were always careful, the way they were careful with a canoe rib or a sleeping child.

*To His Excellency the Governor and the Honorable Council and Burgesses in Assembly met — Your humble petitioners humbly sheweth —*

She watched the words take shape and felt something she could not name. Not hope exactly. Not its absence either. Something in between, the way the sky looks in the hour before dawn when it is not yet light but it is no longer dark.

The fire popped again. Saya reached past Okwi and adjusted the ink pot closer to the coals, where it would stay fluid. Their arms did not touch.

Outside the firelight, the winter camp breathed around them. The low hiss of wind through bare oak branches. The distant sound of a child turning in sleep. The cold pressing down on the frozen ground, on the leaf litter, on the blaze marks some county surveyor would cut into the trees three days from now if she did not move fast enough.

She looked at the bead again. Then she looked at the petition taking shape in Okwi's careful, unhurried hand.

Her mother would have done the same thing.

She picked up the draft and began to read ahead, checking each line against what Okwi was writing, so that when he reached the boundary description — *beginning at a marked white oak standing on the north side of the creek, running thence south forty perches to a hickory* — she would be ready to call it out to him, word by word, and he would set it down, and the ink would dry, and it would exist in the world in a way that no surveyor's chain could simply overwrite.

That was the theory.

She held the draft closer to the fire and read.

The fire had burned down to coals by the time the last line dried.

Saya held the page close to the heat, tilting it to catch the orange glow, reading the words she had written and rewritten until her hand ached. *Your humble petitioners humbly sheweth that the said tract of land, beginning at a marked white oak standing on the north side of the creek called Mattawoman, thence running south forty perches to a bounded hickory, thence east one hundred and twenty perches—* She had copied the boundary language from the surveyor's own warrant, turned it back on him like a blade. The ink had gone dark and fixed. She set the page down on the flat stone she had been using as a desk.

Okwi pressed his palm along the bottom edge, holding it flat while the last strokes finished drying. He had not spoken in a long time. His hands were a canoe-builder's hands — the knuckles scarred from adze work, the skin cracked at the joints from winter water — and they held the page with the same steadiness he used when he was waiting for pine pitch to set.

"It's done," she said.

He did not lift his palm yet.

The fire popped. A dog somewhere in the camp whined once and went quiet. From the nearest lodge she could hear the slow grind of someone working dried corn in the dark — a sound like a wheel turning, patient and low, the kind of work you did when you couldn't sleep.

Okwi lifted his hand. The page held its shape.

Saya picked it up and read it again, all the way through, her lips moving on the formal English phrases she had learned from the trading post factor's clerk and then practiced alone in the forest until they sat right in her mouth. *Your humble petitioners humbly sheweth.* She had never felt humble in her life, but she knew what the words were for. They were a door. You shaped your mouth into the door and walked through.

"Three days," she said.

Okwi looked up.

"Three days until the survey party reaches the mound." She set the page down again and held up her fingers, counting against the firelight. "Two days to reach the agent's house at Port Tobacco, if I move fast and the creek crossings are passable. One day to be received, or not received." She dropped her hand. "The arithmetic is possible."

"Possible," Okwi said. He said it the way he said most things — flat, with the weight of everything he wasn't saying underneath.

"It is possible."

"I know it is." He reached behind him and pulled a folded square of oilskin from the pack he'd set against the log. She had not seen him bring it. He must have gone back to the lodge while she was copying. It was his best piece — the kind he used to seal the seams of a new canoe before she launched, rubbed with rendered bear fat until it was nearly black and would turn water for years. He unfolded it on the ground between them.

She watched him. "That's your canoe stock."

"I know what it is."

She did not argue. She rolled the petition carefully, the paper stiff and faintly crackling at the edges where the cold had gotten into it, and laid it in the center of the oilskin. Okwi folded the skin around it — once, twice — and pressed the seams flat with his thumb. He handed it back to her.

She tucked it inside her deerskin wrap, against her chest, over her heart. The oilskin was cold at first, the same cold the glass bead always carried before her body warmed it. She felt the bead press against the wrapped document — her mother's bead, smooth and hard and faintly blue in firelight, the one thing she had never taken off. The document and the bead lay against her breastbone together.

She looked at Okwi.

"Pakan will tell the others you went without a full council," he said.

"Pakan already told me."

"He'll say the petition is invalid. Two marks instead of three."

"The assembly doesn't know how many marks it should have." She pulled her outer mantle tighter. "They'll count the marks they see."

Okwi looked into the coals. The light made his face unreadable — all planes and shadow, the line of his jaw, the set of his mouth. He had a way of going still that was not the same as giving up. She had learned the difference years ago.

"Your mother," he said, "went to the factor at Piscataway Creek with a list of names and a piece of paper she'd written herself, and he received her and thanked her and did nothing for eight months."

"I know."

"And then the land was gone anyway."

"I know, Okwi."

He was quiet. Then: "She would have gone again."

Saya looked at him. She couldn't tell, even now, whether he meant it as a comfort or a warning, and she thought perhaps he didn't know either.

She stood. The cold hit her immediately — the fire's warmth had been closer than she realized, and the air beyond its reach was raw and wet, smelling of leaf rot and woodsmoke and the distant iron smell of the creek. The camp was dark. A few coals glowed between the lodges where other fires had burned down. The bare oak branches above her were black against a sky that was just beginning to show the faintest gray at its edges, east, behind the tree line.

"I'll be back before the surveyors arrive," she said.

Okwi did not stand. He sat with his hands loose on his knees, looking up at her, and she could see in his face the exact shape of what he was not saying — the question he would not ask because asking it would make it real.

She touched the oilskin through her wrap, one quick press of her palm against her chest, and walked into the dark.

The path was narrow and frozen, the leaf litter crackling under her moccasins with each step, her breath rising white in the cold air. She moved fast, the way she had learned to move in winter forest — not running yet, but close, her body already finding its rhythm. Behind her the camp's last fire threw a thin orange line between the trees, and then the path curved and the light was gone.

The mound would be to her left as she passed it. She would not stop this time.

Ahead of her, somewhere in the dark, was the agent's house at Port Tobacco, and the assembly's records, and the men who would read her petition and decide whether the words *humbly sheweth* were enough to hold a boundary line against a surveyor's chain.

She did not know if they would receive her.

She did not know what Okwi would do if she was not back in three days.

She ran.
