The fire had burned down to coals.

Saya lay still and listened to the house breathe — the slow drag of Okwi's chest beside her, the smaller sounds of the two families sleeping along the far wall, someone's child turning under a fur robe with a soft grunt. The smoke had settled low, the way it always did in the last hours before dawn, and it lay against her face like a hand. She did not move.

The petition was folded against her chest, tucked inside her deerskin wrap where her body had kept it through the night. She could feel the paper — limp now, faintly damp from her skin, the edges soft where she had handled it too many times. Three days. She ran the calculation again the way she had run it a dozen times since she lay down, as if the numbers might change. The survey party would leave the county seat on the first clear morning, which meant today or tomorrow. They moved by horse on the main road south, then on foot through the pines. Three days to the mound if the ground stayed frozen. Perhaps two if it thawed.

She needed one day less than that.

Outside, a crow called once and went quiet. The cold came through the bark walls in thin fingers, finding the gaps where the reed mats had pulled loose at the corners. Saya had stuffed dry grass into those gaps herself two weeks ago and they had held until the last hard freeze split the lashing. She had meant to re-lash them. She had not.

She turned her head and looked at Okwi.

He slept on his back with one arm thrown wide, the way he always slept, as if even in sleep he needed room. His face in the coal-light was still — the long jaw, the small scar at the corner of his mouth from the time he had tried to teach her to use a drawknife and she had demonstrated that she already knew how. The memory moved through her and she let it go. There was no time for it.

She touched the bead at her throat.

It hung on a cord of twisted plant fiber, dark and soft from years against her mother's skin and then years against her own. The bead itself was glass — deep blue, the color of deep water, with a faint spiral of white worked into it that caught light in a way she had never seen in any other bead. Her mother had received it from a Lenape woman at a trading meeting when Saya was very small, and Saya remembered watching her hold it up to the light and turn it, and she remembered her mother saying nothing, just looking. She had not taken it off since the day they buried her mother.

She pressed it once between her fingers and then sat up.

The cold hit her across the shoulders. She reached for the blue stroud cloth she had folded beside her sleeping place — heavy wool, the color of a bruise, taken in trade two years ago — and pulled it around herself over the deerskin wrap. The petition crinkled once as she moved and she pressed her hand flat against it until it stopped. Okwi did not stir.

She found her moccasins in the dark by feel, double-soled, stuffed with dry grass that had compressed and gone flat. She would need to re-stuff them before she walked. She did this slowly, pulling fresh grass from the bundle she kept near the wall, working it in with her fingers, her breath coming out in small pale clouds in the air above the dying coals.

The petition.

She drew it out and held it in both hands. The paper was rag-laid, and she had learned to read its faint chain lines by holding it to light, the way the man who had helped her write it — a trader's clerk in Charles County, a nervous young Englishman who had copied her words into the formal language required — had shown her. *To His Excellency the Governor and the Honourable Council and Burgesses of the Province of Maryland. The humble petition of the Piscataway people of the Mattawoman and Choptico towns.* She did not need to read it. She had the words by heart. But she held it anyway, feeling the slight give of the damp paper, and thought about what it would look like when the clerk read it aloud in the assembly room — whether the words she had labored over would sound different in an Englishman's mouth, stripped of everything she meant by them.

*That your petitioners have maintained their towns and burying places upon the said lands from time out of mind, and that the proposed survey and re-granting of the said tracts would deprive them of the same.*

*Burying places.* The clerk had suggested the phrase. She had used it because she understood it would carry weight in a colonial document, because the English marked their own dead and would not willingly disturb them, and because it was true — the mound was there, it had always been there, and the surveyors would walk right past it with their Gunter's chain and their compass and their hatchet and blaze a line through it if no one stopped them.

She folded the petition back along its creases and tucked it inside her wrap again.

Okwi was awake. She knew it before she looked up — his breathing had changed, gone shallow and deliberate, the way it changed when he was watching her and deciding whether to speak.

"You should eat something," he said.

"I will eat when I come back."

"You should eat before you go."

She looked at him. He had not moved, still on his back, arm still thrown wide, but his eyes were open and fixed on the smoke hole above them where the stars had gone grey with the first edge of dawn.

"There is corn mush," he said. "From last night."

"I know there is corn mush."

"I am only saying."

She stood, pulling the stroud cloth tighter. The cold floor pressed up through her moccasin soles, the frozen earth unyielding. Across the house, one of the children shifted and settled. The pine kindling she had laid beside the fire the night before was still there, and she knelt and fed three pieces to the coals, watching the resin catch with a sharp, sweet smell that cut through the old smoke. The fire took.

"Three days," she said. She was not speaking to Okwi. She was speaking to the fire, to the calculation, to herself.

"Two," Okwi said. "If Hollis rides out this morning."

She turned. He had sat up now, his elbows on his knees, watching her with the expression she could never quite name — not doubt, not fear, something more careful than either.

"You think he rides today."

"The ground is frozen hard. The road will be good." He paused. "I think he rides today."

She had thought the same thing and had not said it because saying it made it real. She stood and looked at the fire and let it be real.

"Then I go now," she said.

Okwi said nothing. He reached for his own moccasins.

"You are not coming," she said.

"I know."

"Then why—"

"Because I am going to watch you leave." He said it simply, without ceremony. He pulled on his moccasins and stood, and he was a head taller than she was and the firelight made his shadow long across the bark wall behind him. "And because if you fall through the ice on the creek I want to be close enough to hear it."

She felt the thing move through her that she could not afford — the pull of it, the particular warmth of a man who made her furious and made her laugh and was right about the ice. She turned away before it could settle.

"The agent will see me," she said.

"Yes."

"He has seen me before."

"He has."

"And if he will not—"

"Then you will make him." Okwi's voice was even, not soothing, just stating a fact about her the way he stated facts about wood grain and water current. "You will stand in his doorway until he does."

She picked up the small bundle she had packed the night before — dried venison wrapped in bark, a gourd of water that had frozen solid and would need to be thawed at the first creek she crossed, her knife in its sheath. She tied the bundle and slung it over her shoulder. The petition pressed against her ribs.

She looked at Okwi once more.

"Three days," she said.

"Two," he said. "Go fast."

She pushed through the door mat and the cold took her.

The sky above the bare oaks was the color of iron, not yet light, the stars gone and the sun not yet come. Her breath rose in front of her face and dissolved. The camp was quiet — a dog lifted its head from near the next house and then put it down again. The frozen mud of the path cracked once under her foot and then held. She moved south through the trees, the holly dark and glossy on either side of the path, the greenbrier reaching from the undergrowth with its small thorns that caught at the stroud cloth and let go.

Somewhere behind her a crow called.

She walked faster, and then she ran.

The path south was a deer trail first and a footpath second, worn to bare dirt in summer but now frozen into ridges that caught her moccasin soles and turned her ankle if she let her mind drift. She did not let her mind drift. She kept her eyes on the ground three steps ahead and ran.

The cold was not the damp cold of November, the cold that soaked through wool and settled in the joints. This was January cold, dry and hard, the kind that cracked lips and stiffened fingers and made the inside of the nose ache with each breath. She had stuffed her moccasins with dry grass that morning and she could still feel her feet, which she counted as good.

The oak branches above her were bare and black against the iron sky, and when the wind moved through them they rattled like thrown sticks. No leaves, no cover, no sound to hide her own footfalls. Just the creak and crack of frozen litter underfoot and her own breath rising in small clouds that the cold took and dissolved.

She rehearsed as she ran.

*To His Excellency the Governor and the Honourable Council and Burgesses of the Province of Maryland.* The words came in English and she let them come, placing them one after another the way she placed her feet — deliberately, without stumbling. *The humble petition of Siosaia and the people of the Piscataway residing at Choptico.* She had argued with herself about *humble.* The clerk had insisted on it. She had given it to him because the word cost her nothing and the petition cost her everything, and a word that cost nothing should not be the thing that killed it.

*Humbly sheweth.* She had not liked *sheweth* either. But the clerk had said the assembly expected the form, and she had learned long ago that the form was not the thing — the thing was what the form contained, the words inside the words, the argument that lived inside the ceremony.

*That your petitioners have for many years past peaceably occupied and improved the tracts of land lying along the branches of the Potomac.*

She crossed a small hollow where the ground dipped and the cold air pooled, and the temperature dropped another degree, enough that she felt it on her cheekbones. A fox barked somewhere to the east — once, sharp, then nothing. She kept moving.

*That the said tracts contain the burying places of your petitioners' ancestors, and that the proposed survey and re-granting of the same would deprive them thereof.*

*Burying places.* She touched the petition through the stroud cloth as she ran, feeling the folded edge of it against her ribs. The paper had gone limp in the night air, slightly cool against her skin even through the wool. She had not meant to carry it this close to her body, but when she had packed the bundle that morning she had found she could not put it in the bag with the venison. It needed to be where she could feel it.

The path climbed a low ridge and she climbed with it, her thighs burning, her breath coming harder now. At the top she paused for three counts, not to rest but to listen. Wind. Branches. A woodpecker somewhere to the west, drilling into dead wood with a sound like a slow knock on a door. No horses. No chain carriers with their Gunter's chain and their hatchet and their field book, marking trees with blazes, turning the forest into lines on paper, turning her home into someone else's patent.

Two days, Okwi had said. If Hollis rides this morning.

She ran down the far side of the ridge.

The creek came up faster than she expected — the path curved around a stand of holly, the leaves dark and waxy and incongruously bright in the grey morning, and then the ground dropped sharply and she was at the bank. The creek was perhaps twelve feet across, the center still running black and fast, but the edges had frozen out two feet on each side, the ice grey-white and opaque, not the clear ice of a hard freeze but the clouded ice of a thaw that had refrozen. She did not trust it.

She moved upstream twenty paces to where a large beech had fallen across the water, its roots still partially in the bank, its bark peeling in long papery strips. She crossed on the trunk, arms out, moving quickly, and the wood was solid under her feet. On the far side she dropped back to the path and ran.

The petition pressed against her ribs. She thought about the agent's face — the way his eyes moved when she spoke, not to her eyes but to her mouth, as if he were checking her English for mistakes he could use. She had given him no mistakes. She had spoken carefully and he had listened carefully and at the end he had said he would see what could be done, which meant he would do nothing unless she gave him a reason that was better than the reason he had to do nothing.

The witnessing was the reason. A witnessed document, signed by men whose names the assembly knew, was harder to ignore than a woman standing in a doorway. She had one name already — the Reverend Addison, who owed her a debt she had never called in and was now calling in. She needed two more. The council at the winter camp would give her two more, if she reached them before the day was gone.

*If Hollis rides this morning.*

She pushed the thought down and ran faster.

The mound came up on her left without announcement, the way it always did — the path curved around a stand of old hickory, and there it was, rising from the flat ground at the base of the slope, perhaps eight feet high at its crown, its sides covered in the same leaf litter and frozen mud as the surrounding forest floor. In summer you could walk past it and take it for a natural rise if you did not know. In winter, stripped of its fern cover, it was clearly shaped — too regular, too deliberate, the curve of it too considered.

She stopped.

She had not planned to stop. She had planned to pass it and keep running, because she was running against a clock she could not see and every pause was a cost. But her feet stopped and she stood at the base of the slope and looked at it, and she let herself look.

The frost had settled on its crown overnight, and in the grey morning light the top of the mound was faintly white, the frozen grass standing stiff and pale. A crow sat on the highest point, watching her with one black eye. It did not move.

She thought of her mother, who had told her: *this is where the old ones are.* Not as instruction. As fact, the way you told a child that the river ran south or that hickory burned hotter than oak. *This is where the old ones are, and they have been here longer than the English have had a word for long.*

She thought of the survey team — the county surveyor on his horse, the chain carriers on foot behind him, the hatchet for blazing trees. She thought of the field book where they wrote the lines, the compass that turned the land into degrees and chains, the patent that would come after with its formal legal English and its *tracts and parcells* and its new owner's name.

She thought: *not this.*

The crow lifted from the crown of the mound, spread its wings once, and was gone into the bare branches above.

She ran.

The winter camp was three miles south, set back from the river in a stand of pine and beech that broke the wind from the northwest. She smelled the fires before she saw the houses — pine smoke first, sharp and resinous, and then the deeper smell of hickory coals, and then the particular smell of a camp in winter, which was woodsmoke and cured hide and the faint sweetness of dried corn. The smell hit her and something in her chest loosened, some tightness she had been carrying since before dawn.

The path widened as it approached the camp and the frozen mud gave way to bare earth beaten hard by many feet. Two dogs came out to meet her, tails moving, and she let them smell her hand without slowing. The first house was dark, its smoke hole quiet. The second had a thin thread of smoke rising. The council house was at the center of the camp, larger than the others, its bark walls double-layered against the cold, the smoke hole above it breathing steadily.

She could hear voices inside.

She stopped at the entrance and pressed her hand flat against the petition through the stroud cloth, feeling the folded paper, the slight give of it, the faint dampness where the cold air had found it. She had carried it four miles through frozen forest. She had crossed a creek on a fallen beech. She had stopped at the mound and looked at it and run on.

She pulled back the door mat and went in.

The fire was high, the smoke layered under the low roof, and the faces around it turned toward her — six of them, seven, the elders and the headmen she had come to find. Their faces were lit orange from below by the fire and shadowed above by the smoke, and they looked at her the way people look at someone who has run four miles through January cold, which is to say they looked at her as if they already knew what she was carrying.

She pulled the petition from inside her wrap and held it out, and the damp paper caught the firelight and the iron gall ink showed dark and deliberate on the page, the formal English words she had argued over and accepted and carried here against her ribs.

"I need two witnesses," she said. "Before midday."

The eldest man — Tomakin, who had known her father — looked at the document and then at her face.

"Sit down first," he said. "Eat something."

She almost laughed. She thought of Okwi saying *there is corn mush* in the grey dark of the house before dawn, his eyes on the smoke hole, his voice even and careful. She thought of him standing in the doorway to watch her leave, his shadow long on the bark wall, saying *go fast* as if it were a simple thing.

She sat. She did not eat. She spread the petition on the ground between herself and the fire, and the faces leaned in, and outside the council house the cold pressed against the bark walls and the day was already moving.

Tomakin did not read quickly. He held the petition close to the fire, tilting it so the light fell across the lines, and his lips moved slightly as he worked through the formal English. The paper had gone limp from the cold and the damp of her running, and he handled it carefully, the way you handle something that might tear.

The others waited. Saya watched their faces.

There were three elders she needed: Tomakin, who had spoken for the community at the last treating with the governor's men; Wekis, who was older and said less and whose mark carried weight because of it; and Pakan, who sat farthest from the fire with his fur robe pulled close and his eyes on the ground, as if he had already decided something and was waiting for the rest of them to catch up.

Tomakin finished reading. He set the petition down on the ground between them, its corners curling slightly in the heat.

"You wrote this," he said. It was not a question.

"With help," Saya said. "Thomas Brooke's clerk. He wrote the formal parts — *tracts and parcells, metes and bounds.* The substance is mine."

"The boundary," Tomakin said. He pressed one finger to the page, not touching the ink. "Here. You have it running from the old field to the branch and then south to the ridge. Is that right?"

"That is what my father described to me. That is what his father described to him."

"And the mound."

"The mound is inside the boundary." She had argued with Brooke's clerk about this for an hour, the two of them standing in the cold outside the trading post with the petition between them, the clerk insisting that *Indian burying place* was the term the assembly would recognize, Saya insisting on something more precise. In the end she had accepted his language. It was his assembly. "It is described as a landmark. *To the Indian burying place on the ridge, thence south by southeast.* That is how the surveyors will read it. They use such things as markers already — I have seen it in the county records."

Tomakin looked at her. "You have seen the county records."

"Thomas Brooke let me look. He owed my father a debt."

Wekis, who had not spoken, made a sound that was not quite a laugh. He was a small man, very old, his face deeply lined, and he had a habit of running his thumb along the edge of whatever was in his hands, as if reading by touch. He was doing it now with the hem of his robe.

"The governor's survey team," he said. "Three days."

"Three days, if the weather holds. Two if it clears."

Wekis nodded once, slowly, the way a man nods when he has been told something he already knew.

Saya reached inside her wrap and brought out the quill and the small stoppered bottle of ink she had carried from Brooke's trading post, wrapped in a scrap of stroud cloth to keep it from freezing. The ink had thickened in the cold. She uncorked it and held it near the fire for a moment, watching the surface of it shift. The sharp iron smell rose with the warmth.

"I need your marks," she said. "Both names in English, if you can write them. If not, your marks and I will write the names beside them."

Tomakin looked at the petition again. He looked at it for a long time.

"If I put my name on this," he said, "I am saying that this English document speaks for our people."

"You are saying that this land is ours. The document is only the way the assembly will hear it."

"And if they do not hear it?"

Saya said nothing. She had thought about this in the dark before dawn, lying awake while Okwi slept and the cold pressed through the bark walls and her daughter breathed small and even on her sleeping mat. She had thought about it on the path through the frozen forest, her feet finding the deer track by feel, the petition against her ribs. She had thought about it at the mound, standing at its base in the grey light, the crow watching her from the crown. She did not have an answer that would satisfy him, because there was no such answer. There was only the petition and the survey team three days out and the choice between doing this and doing nothing.

"Then we will have tried," she said.

Tomakin picked up the quill. She steadied the ink bottle while he dipped it, and he signed his name in careful English letters at the bottom of the page — *Tomakin* — the letters large and deliberate, each one a small act of will. He handed the quill to Wekis.

Wekis could not write his name in English. He pressed the quill to the page and made his mark instead, a small cross with a curved line beneath it, and Saya wrote *Wekis* beside it in her own hand, smaller and faster than Tomakin's signature, the letters slightly uneven from the cold in her fingers.

Two marks. She needed three.

She looked at Pakan.

He had not moved from his place at the far edge of the fire. His robe was bear hide, old and dark, the fur worn thin at the shoulders. His hands were in his lap. He had watched the whole proceeding without expression, his eyes moving between the petition and her face and the fire, and now he met her gaze and held it.

"No," he said.

She waited. She had learned, watching her father in council, that the first word was rarely the whole of it.

"The assembly will not honor this," Pakan said. "Not because the boundary is wrong. Not because the document is badly written. Because you are a Piscataway woman carrying a piece of paper, and they will look at you and see a woman carrying a piece of paper, and they will write their answer in their journal and file it away and send the survey team anyway. I have watched them treat with us for forty years. I know what they honor and what they set aside."

The fire crackled. Somewhere outside, one of the dogs barked once and was quiet.

"You may be right," Saya said.

Pakan looked at her. He had expected argument, she could see it — the slight shift in his face when she did not give him one.

"Then why carry it?"

"Because the survey team is three days out. Because when the patent comes — and it will come, with or without this petition — I want there to be a document in the assembly journal that says we were here, we named this land, we described this boundary, and we asked them to honor it. I want there to be a record they have to look at before they look away." She paused. "I am not asking them to be just. I am asking them to know what they are doing."

Pakan was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the wind moved through the pine tops, a low sound, almost like water.

"My mark will not change what they do," he said.

"No."

"Then I will not put it there."

She had known, she realized, since she came through the door and saw him sitting apart from the others, that this was how it would go. She had hoped otherwise. She had carried the hope four miles through January cold, the way you carry something fragile — carefully, without looking at it directly, because if you look at it directly you will see how small it is.

She picked up the petition and folded it along its crease, the damp paper soft under her hands. Tomakin's name, Wekis's mark. Two witnesses. The customary three was what Brooke's clerk had told her the assembly expected. *Three witnesses of standing,* he had said, *or the petition will be questioned.* She had nodded and thought: I will get three. She had been sure of it the way you are sure of things you cannot afford to doubt.

She tucked the folded document back inside her wrap, against her ribs, and pressed her hand flat over it for a moment. Through the stroud cloth she could feel the slight give of it, the faint dampness, the edges of the fold.

The bead at her throat was warm from her skin. She did not touch it.

"Thank you," she said to Tomakin. To Wekis. She did not say it to Pakan, because he had given her nothing, and she would not pretend otherwise.

Tomakin caught her eye as she stood. His face said something she could not quite read — not pity, not apology. Something older than either. He had known her father. He had known what her father had tried and what it had cost him.

She pulled the door mat aside and went out into the cold.

The camp was stirring now, the morning moving toward midday, thin light falling through the bare beech crowns. Her breath came white in the air. She stood for a moment with her hand still pressed over the petition, feeling the two marks through the cloth — Tomakin's careful English letters, Wekis's small cross — and she thought: *it will have to be enough.*

She did not believe it. But she thought it, which was the closest she could come.

She turned south, toward home, toward Okwi, toward the house where her daughter was learning to read English words off a scrap of paper by firelight. Two marks. Three days. The survey team on its way through the frozen county with its chain and its compass and its hatchet for blazing trees, and somewhere in its field book a line that would pass, if she did not stop it, right through the mound on the ridge.

She started walking. Then she ran.

He was standing at the edge of the firelight when she came through the door.

Not the elder house — she had come home, her own house, the one she and Okwi had built three winters back with bent saplings and overlapping sheets of bark, the walls blackened now with two seasons of smoke. He had come ahead of her somehow, or perhaps he had never gone to Pakan's at all. She did not ask. He was standing near the fire with his arms at his sides and his bear-hide robe pushed back off his shoulders, and he was watching her the way he watched the river when the weather was changing — not with alarm, just with attention.

She came in and let the door mat fall behind her.

The fire was hickory, low and red, the coals throwing more heat than light. The smell of pine smoke from the kindling still clung to the air. On the sleeping platform, their daughter was curled under a raccoon robe, her breathing slow and even. Saya stood for a moment and listened to it.

Then she crossed to the fire and crouched and spread the petition on the reed mat between them.

Two names. Tomakin's careful letters. Wekis's cross.

She did not say anything. Neither did he.

Okwi looked at the document for a long time. He had the kind of face that was hard to read quickly — broad and still, the lines around his eyes from squinting over water. He looked at the two marks and then he looked at her.

"Pakan," he said.

"No."

He nodded once, as though this confirmed something he had already set aside.

She reached for the quill she had brought from Brooke's clerk — a goose feather, the nib trimmed sharp — and the small stoppered jar of iron gall ink. She set them beside the petition. In the firelight the ink looked black as river mud. She had carried the jar inside her wrap all the way from the elder house, pressed against her ribs beside the petition, and it was still faintly warm from her body.

"I need to copy it," she said. "The final version. The clerk said the assembly wants a clean hand."

Okwi looked at the quill. He looked at the petition. He did not look at her.

"What will you do," he said, "when it fails?"

The fire shifted. A coal dropped and sent up a brief spray of sparks that vanished before they reached the smoke hole.

She had expected this question less than she had expected his silence. She had braced for argument — the argument they had already had three times, in three different registers, the one that ended with him going quiet and her going out to split wood. This was not that.

"Write another," she said.

He looked at her then.

"Another petition."

"Another petition. Or a letter to the governor's council. Or a boundary description filed with the county court. Something that puts our name on the land in their records. Something they have to look at before they look away."

"And when they look away from that one."

"Then I write another."

He was quiet. Outside, the wind came through the pine tops — that low sound, almost like the river, almost like breathing. The camp was settling into evening. Somewhere two fires over, she could hear the low voices of women, the occasional knock of a wooden bowl.

Okwi's gaze moved to the bead at her throat.

She felt it — not his hand, he had not moved, just his eyes. The bead was warm from her skin, as it always was, as it had been every day since her mother pressed it into her palm and closed her fingers over it. A glass bead, deep blue, the color of deep water in summer. She had never taken it off. In winter she wore it inside her wrap and could feel it against her sternum, small and smooth, a fixed point.

"Your mother," Okwi said, "would have done the same thing."

She waited.

"I cannot decide," he said, "if that is a comfort or a warning."

The fire crackled. Her daughter shifted under the raccoon robe and made a small sound and was still again.

Saya looked at her husband for a moment. He was not smiling. He was not not smiling. He had the expression he wore when he was working out a problem in a canoe hull — not resigned, not hopeful, just attending to the thing in front of him with the full weight of his attention, because the thing in front of him was real and it mattered and there was no use pretending otherwise.

She did not answer him. She did not think he had meant it as a question.

She uncorked the ink jar and set it carefully on the reed mat, well clear of the petition. She smoothed the paper — the damp had made it slightly limp, the edges soft, and she pressed it flat with her palm and held it until it settled. Then she took out the blank sheet she had carried folded inside the first, the clean one Brooke's clerk had given her without being asked, and she set it beside the draft.

Okwi sat down.

Not across from her. Beside her, close enough that she could feel the warmth off his robe. He reached out and turned the draft petition so the light fell better across it, angling it toward the fire without touching the ink. Then he held it steady while she dipped the quill.

*To His Excellency the Governor and the Honourable Council and Burgesses of the Province of Maryland.* She wrote it in her clearest hand, the letters small and even, the way the clerk had shown her. *The humble petition of the Piscataway people residing at—*

She stopped.

Okwi was reading over her shoulder. She could feel him reading. He had never learned to write English but he could read it slowly, working letter by letter the way you work a trail you have not walked before.

"At the mound on the ridge above the branch," she said, without looking up. "That is what I will put."

"The assembly will not know which branch."

"The survey team will. They have been walking this county for three weeks. They know every branch."

He said nothing. She took it for assent and kept writing.

The fire burned down slowly. She wrote and he held the draft steady when her hand needed a reference and smoothed the clean sheet when the damp made it curl at the corner. He did not speak again for a long time. When she reached the boundary description — *beginning at the great white oak marked by our people in the time of our fathers, running north by east to the burial mound on the ridge, thence—* she slowed, and he turned the draft a fraction more toward the light without being asked, so she could read the line she had already written once.

Her daughter slept. The smoke layered under the roof, stinging faintly. The bead at her throat was warm.

She did not know if her mother would have done the same thing. She did not know if it was a comfort or a warning either. She thought it was probably both, the way most true things were both — the way the petition was both necessary and likely to fail, the way the mound on the ridge was both theirs and already in a surveyor's field book, a line waiting to be drawn through it.

She kept writing.

Outside, the temperature was dropping. She could hear it in the way the fire pulled — a slight draw of cold air under the door mat, the coals brightening when it came. Three days. The survey team with its Gunter's chain and its compass and its hatchet for blazing trees, moving through the frozen county, marking oaks, piling stones at corners, writing names in their field book over the names that were already there.

She reached the close of the petition — *and your humble petitioners, as in duty bound, will ever pray* — and set down the quill.

Okwi looked at what she had written.

"It is a good hand," he said.

She capped the ink jar. Her fingers were stained black at the tips, the cold having made them stiff, and she pressed them together and felt the ink crack slightly at the knuckles.

"I need one more name," she said.

He did not answer immediately. She heard him breathe.

"I know," she said. "I know you think it will not matter."

"I think it will not matter to them," he said. "It may matter to someone else."

She looked at him. He was looking at the petition — at the clean copy, her best hand, the boundary description, the two marks already on the draft waiting to be transferred. His face was still. The firelight moved over it.

"Someone else," she said.

"Later," he said. "Someone who needs to know what we tried."

She picked up the quill again. She held it out to him.

He looked at it for a moment. Then he took it.

The ink on his name was still wet.

Saya watched it dry. The letters were uneven — he had never learned to form them and she had not taught him, and the marks he made were somewhere between letters and the notches he cut into canoe ribs to measure depth, but they were his, and they were there, and that was enough.

She set the petition flat on the reed mat and did not touch it.

Okwi put the quill down and looked at his hand. The iron gall had gone into the creases of his knuckles, black against the firelit skin, and he turned his palm over once and then set it on his knee as if deciding it was no longer his concern.

"How long," he said.

"Until it sets?" She judged the fire, the cold pull under the door mat, the slight curl at the corner of the page where the damp kept working at it. "Not long. The cold slows it."

He nodded. He reached out and held the bottom edge of the page flat without being asked, two fingers only, not touching the writing.

She counted while she waited. Not aloud yet — she ran it first in her head, the way you run a trail before you walk it in the dark. Three days until the survey team reached the ridge. Two days' travel to the agent's house at the tobacco landing, if she kept to the deer paths and did not lose the light on the second afternoon. One day to be received — or not received, which was its own kind of day, longer and colder than the other kind.

The arithmetic was tight. It was possible.

She picked up the oilskin.

It was a good piece — Okwi's best, the piece he kept for the hull seams of the canoes he was proudest of, rubbed with rendered bear fat until it was nearly black and supple as worn leather. She had asked him for it that morning and he had gone to his bundle without speaking and come back with it folded in his hand and given it to her and gone back to sharpening his drawknife as if she had asked him for nothing more than a coal to start the breakfast fire.

She set the petition face-up on the oilskin and checked the ink one more time. Dry. She folded the paper once along its length, carefully, the way the clerk had shown her — crease flat, no wrinkling at the fold — and then again across, so it was a tight rectangle small enough to press against her body. She set it in the center of the oilskin and folded the skin around it the way you wrap a child against rain: first the long sides, then the short, then the whole bundle rolled once more so no seam faced outward.

She pressed it with both palms. She could feel the slight rigidity of the paper inside, the give of the oilskin, the faint smell of the bear fat and beneath it the sharper smell of the ink.

She tucked it inside her robe, against her chest. The bead was already there — had been there all evening, warm from her skin, pressing its small weight against her sternum through the layers of stroud cloth and deerskin. The oilskin bundle settled beside it. The bead pressed against the document.

She sat with that for a moment.

"Three days," she said aloud.

Okwi looked at her.

"Three days until they reach the ridge. Two days' travel to the agent's house. One day to be received." She said it evenly, the way you say a thing you have already decided about. "The arithmetic is possible."

"Possible," he said.

"You have a different word."

"I have the same word." He picked up the drawknife from where it had been resting against the wall and turned it in his hands — not using it, just holding it, the way he held tools when he was thinking. The blade caught the firelight. "Possible is the word I have."

She stood. Her daughter stirred on the sleeping platform and settled again, one arm thrown out over the edge of the fur robe, fingers open. Saya looked at her for a moment — the slack face, the easy breath, the way children slept as if the world had agreed to hold still for them.

She pulled the fur robe up over the outstretched arm.

Okwi had not moved. He was watching her with the same expression he used when he was reading a stretch of river he had not canoed before — not worried, not calm, just attending.

"If I am not back before the third morning," she said, "go to the mound. Take the boys from Choptico if they will come. Stand on the ridge and make them see you."

"They have seen us before."

"Make them see you again." She pulled her own robe tighter and knotted the cord at her throat. The oilskin bundle shifted slightly with the movement and settled. "It will not stop the survey. But it will be in their field book. It will be a thing they had to write around."

He looked at the drawknife in his hands.

"Saya."

"I know."

"I am not saying—"

"I know what you are not saying." She looked at him. The fire had burned low and the light was reddish now, the coals thick and steady, and his face was all planes and shadow. The smoke layered under the bark roof above him, slow and grey. "I am also not saying it."

He set the drawknife down. He stood — one motion, the way he always stood, without apparent effort — and he crossed the few feet between them and put his hand flat against the oilskin bundle through her robe, just for a moment, as if checking that it was real and solid and there.

Then he stepped back.

"The path to the landing fords the branch twice," he said. "The second ford will be iced at the edges. Stay to the right bank past the holly stand — the bottom is firmer there."

"I know the ford."

"You know it in summer."

She almost said *I know it in winter too* but she stopped herself, because the truth was she had not walked it in January, and he had, more than once, running canoe timber to the landing in the cold months when the buyers paid more for delivery. She nodded.

"Right bank past the holly," she said.

He nodded once. That was all.

She lifted the door mat and the cold came in — a hard, clean cold, the kind that had teeth in it, the frost already thick on the ground outside, the stars sharp through the bare branches overhead. Her breath went white. The frozen leaf-litter crunched once under her moccasin and then she was past the firelight and into the dark, the oilskin bundle pressed against her chest, the bead pressed against the bundle, both of them warm from her body and the fire she was leaving behind.

She did not look back.

The question that moved with her into the dark was not whether the agent would receive her — she had made her peace with the range of answers he might give. The question was what Okwi would do on the third morning if she had not come back. Whether he would go to the mound as she had asked. Whether standing on the ridge with the boys from Choptico would put anything into any field book that would last longer than a season.

Whether any of it would matter to anyone but the someone he had named — the later someone, the one neither of them would ever meet.

She walked faster. The holly stand was a dark mass ahead, its leaves catching the starlight, and she angled right toward the firmer ground, the ford still a mile ahead, the agent's house two days beyond that, and the survey team already somewhere in the frozen county, moving through the bare oaks with their chain and their compass and their hatchet, writing names over the names that were already there.
