# 05 — Chapter 1 — Present (parallel / post-test)

*5,903 words · 5 sub-chapters · craft_passes=1.*

---

The envelope had been sitting on her desk for twenty minutes.

Tayak knew this because she had watched the clock on her laptop tick from 9:47 to 10:07 while she graded nothing, answered no emails, and drank half a cup of coffee that had gone cold sometime around 9:52. The envelope sat between her keyboard and the edge of the desk, cream-colored, the Maryland Historical Trust seal printed in the upper left corner in a blue so official it looked like a warning.

She had waited two years for this. She was afraid to open it.

The fear was specific. She had named it, the way she named everything — a habit from her grandmother, who believed that unnamed things grew. If the permit said no, she had no fallback. The highway expansion was real. The construction staging area was already visible from Route 301, orange machinery sitting in a cut-over field like something that had always been there, waiting. Eleven weeks. She had priced the legal challenge and could not afford it. She had asked the university for emergency research funds and been told to resubmit in the next cycle. The mound would be graded into the shoulder of a road that no one in Charles County had asked for and that would save commuters approximately four minutes on their drive to the Beltway.

So: if the envelope said no, the mound was gone. And if the mound was gone, then everything she had spent two years arguing — that the site was a continuous burial ground, that the stratigraphy would show unbroken presence going back past contact, past the colonial erasure, past the period when her people had been officially declared extinct by men who had never met them — all of it went with it. Paved. Mitigated. A word she had learned to hate: *mitigation*, meaning we documented it before we destroyed it, meaning the loss was managed, meaning it was acceptable.

She picked up the envelope.

The paper was heavier than she expected, or maybe her hands were lighter. She turned it over. The flap was sealed with a strip of adhesive that gave cleanly when she ran her thumbnail under it, as if it had been waiting too.

Inside: seven pages, stapled, with a cover sheet that read *Maryland Historical Trust Excavation Permit — Conditional Authorization* and below that the site number she had assigned two years ago, *18CH-447*, and below that her name, *Dr. Tayak R. Moyaone, Principal Investigator*, and below that a single line in bold that read:

*Authorization is hereby granted.*

She set the pages down on her desk.

She sat with that for a moment. *Granted.* She had expected to feel more. Relief, maybe, or the particular brightness that came when a thing you had fought for finally arrived. What she felt instead was a tightness in her chest that she could not immediately identify, and she realized after a moment that it was the same feeling she got before fieldwork — not fear exactly, but the awareness that she was standing at the edge of something she could not yet see the bottom of.

She picked the pages back up and read.

The permit was thorough and, in its thoroughness, almost beautiful in the way that bureaucratic language sometimes was — dense, precise, indifferent. *Site 18CH-447, Charles County, Maryland. Survey and excavation of upland mound feature, parcel coordinates attached as Exhibit A. Principal investigator to maintain field notes in accordance with the Maryland Archaeology Standards and Guidelines. Chain of custody to be established for all recovered materials. Human remains, if encountered, to be treated in accordance with applicable state and federal law, including consultation with affiliated tribal nations as determined by the Office of the State Archaeologist.* She had written most of these conditions herself, two years ago, in her research design. Seeing them reflected back in official language felt like hearing her own voice played on a recording — recognizable, but slightly wrong.

She turned to page four.

The clause was in section 7(b), under *Artifact Disposition and Custody*. It was not long. It read:

*The State of Maryland retains right of first refusal on any artifact or assemblage deemed to be of significant historical value as determined by the Maryland Historical Trust. In the event that the Trust exercises this right, the Principal Investigator will be notified in writing within thirty (30) days of the Trust's determination, and the relevant materials will be transferred to state custody pending further disposition.*

She read it twice.

Then she set the pages down again and looked out the window. Her office was on the third floor of the archaeology building, and the window faced a stand of red oaks that had been there since before the building, their roots lifting the sidewalk in two places that the grounds crew patched every spring and the roots broke again every fall. The leaves were just beginning to turn, a rim of yellow on the uppermost branches where the light hit longest.

*Significant historical value.* She knew what that phrase meant in practice. It meant whatever the Trust decided it meant. It meant a copper pendant could be significant. A glass trade bead could be significant. A ceramic pipe bowl, a shell gorget, a document wrapped in oilskin and buried with care — all of it could be significant, and if it was, the state could take it. Could take it, catalog it, put it in a climate-controlled drawer in Annapolis, and let it sit there for twenty years while someone wrote a grant application to study it.

Or they could put it in a display case with a label that said *Indigenous artifact, Charles County, pre-contact period*, and charge eight dollars for adults, five for children, and call it public education.

The clause was not something she had anticipated. She had read the enabling statute, had spoken to two other archaeologists who had worked under similar permits — both had said the same thing, that the state almost never invoked such language — but she had not expected to find it here, in this permit, applied to this site. *Almost.* That word had been doing a lot of work for two years, and now that she was looking at the clause in print, it seemed thinner than it had.

She thought about calling Marcus. He would want to know. He had been in this fight as long as she had — longer, in some ways, because he had been fighting for recognition before she had her PhD, before she had a university affiliation to put on a letterhead. He would read the clause and go quiet in the particular way he went quiet when something was wrong, and then he would tell her not to sign.

She thought about the highway staging area. Orange machinery in a cut-over field. Eleven weeks.

She turned to the last page of the permit.

The signature line was there, with a date field beside it, and below it a line of small print that read: *By signing below, the Principal Investigator acknowledges receipt of and agreement to all conditions set forth in this permit, including but not limited to the provisions of section 7(b).*

She found a pen in her desk drawer — a ballpoint with the university logo, the kind that appeared in desk drawers without anyone ever buying them. She uncapped it. She looked at the signature line for a moment. She thought about her grandmother's house in La Plata, the kitchen table, the smell of coffee and cedar, the quilts folded over the back of every chair. She thought about the mound itself, which she had visited only once with survey equipment, walking the perimeter in the early morning while dew was still on the grass, feeling the slight rise underfoot that most people would have walked across without noticing.

Two thousand years, maybe more. That was what the surface survey suggested. Stratigraphy she hadn't reached yet. Layers she could not name.

She set the pen down without signing.

She picked up her phone. Marcus's number was at the top of her recent calls. She looked at it for a moment, then pressed it.

He picked up on the second ring. She could hear a whistle in the background, boys shouting — the lacrosse field.

"Give me one second," he said.

She waited. The whistle blew again, sharp and short. Then his footsteps on gravel, the background noise dropping away.

"Okay. Tell me."

She read him the clause. Not a summary — the actual language, word for word, the way you read a contract when you want someone else to hear what you heard. *The State of Maryland retains right of first refusal on any artifact, object, or material recovered from the permitted site that the Maryland Historical Trust determines to be of significant historical value, pending review and consultation under applicable heritage law.*

Silence.

"Marcus."

"I heard you."

"What does that mean to you?"

"It means," he said, and she could hear him choosing the words carefully, the way he did when he was about to say something he knew she didn't want to hear, "that you do all the work, you take all the risk, and then the state decides what it gets to keep."

"That's one reading."

"That's the only reading. *Significant historical value* — you know what that phrase means in practice? It means whatever they want it to mean. It means the moment you pull something important out of that ground, they have a mechanism to take it."

Tayak looked at the permit. The signature line was at the bottom of page four, below the liability language and the insurance requirements and the section on annual reporting. A clean line. Her name would fit on it easily.

"The highway breaks ground in eleven weeks," she said.

"I know when the highway breaks ground."

"Then you know what happens to the mound if I don't sign this."

"I know what you're telling me could happen."

She set down the permit and picked up the pen. Not to sign — just to hold it. "Marcus, there are human remains in that mound. There are funerary objects. There are two thousand years of stratigraphy that no one has touched. If the construction crews move in before I have a permit, before I have even one documented unit, we lose everything. The chain of custody, the provenience, the —"

"I know what we lose."

"Then help me see the alternative."

He didn't answer right away. She heard wind on his end, the distant sound of the boys on the field.

"You could go back to the Trust," he said. "Challenge the clause. Ask for modified language."

"I asked. Three times over eighteen months. This is the language they'll give me. This is the permit."

"Then we take it to the council. We vote on whether to proceed under these conditions."

"There isn't time for a council vote. Not a proper one. You'd need two weeks' notice, a full quorum —"

"I know our own bylaws, Tayak."

"Then you know we can't do it in time."

Another silence. She could picture him on the edge of the field, one hand in his jacket pocket, watching the boys run drills without seeing them.

"The clause is a trap," he said. "You sign this, you're handing them a mechanism they will use. Not might use. Will. The moment you find something they want — and you will find something they want — that clause is a door they walk right through."

"And if I don't sign, the mound gets paved. That's not a trap, Marcus. That's a grave."

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"Make it so the only moral choice is the one you've already decided to make."

The words landed. She held the pen and didn't say anything.

"I'm not doing that," she said.

"You're doing exactly that." His voice wasn't angry — it was tired in the specific way of someone who has had this argument before, in other rooms, with other people who were also right. "You've already decided. You called me to tell me, not to ask me."

She wanted to argue. She looked at the permit instead — at the site coordinates in the upper right corner, a parcel in Charles County she had been staring at on maps since she was twenty-three years old. The mound sat on a low upland ridge above a tidewater creek, sheltered on three sides by second-growth pine. She had walked that ridge in every season. She had stood on the mound's crown in January with frozen mud on her boots and felt the shape of it under her feet — the deliberate, unmistakable shape of something built.

"The alternative is it disappears," she said. "Under four lanes of highway. Under fill and base course and asphalt. And we never know what's there. We never have the evidence. We never have the record."

"And if you sign and they invoke the clause? We have the record but not the artifacts. We have the science but not the standing. And the council looks at what you did — what you agreed to without us — and we're fighting two battles instead of one."

She put the pen to the signature line.

"I hear you," she said.

"Tayak —"

"I hear everything you're saying."

She signed her name. The pen moved quickly, the way it does when you've been holding off for long enough that the act itself is almost a relief. Dr. Tayak Moyaone. The date. Her institutional affiliation. She pressed down hard enough that the signature would be clear on the copy beneath.

"So what are we doing?" Marcus said.

She looked at her name on the page. The ink was still wet.

"I'm going to call the Trust and confirm receipt," she said. "And then I'm driving to La Plata."

"You haven't answered my question."

"I'm going to excavate the site before the highway gets there. I'm going to document everything, properly, with full chain of custody and complete field notes. And if the state tries to invoke that clause, we'll fight it then. With evidence."

"That's not a plan. That's a hope."

"Sometimes that's what you have."

He made a sound — not quite a laugh, not quite a sigh. "Call me when you've talked to Nora."

"I will."

She ended the call. She sat for a moment with the signed permit in front of her and the phone face-down on the desk and the particular quiet of a room where a decision has just been made.

She had not told him she had already signed.

She told herself there had been no good moment to say it. She told herself the call was over and it didn't matter when he found out, only that he would find out, and by then she would have something to show him — a unit opened, a context documented, something real and recoverable from that ridge above the creek.

She told herself this. She picked up the permit, folded it along its original creases, and slid it into the envelope she'd carried it in all morning.

Outside her window, the campus moved through its Tuesday. A student crossed the quad with a coffee cup and a backpack that pulled one shoulder lower than the other. Two pigeons disputed a crust of something near the library steps. The sky was the flat white of a Maryland October morning that hasn't decided yet what it wants to do.

Eleven weeks.

She put the envelope in her bag, picked up her keys, and went to find Priya.

---

The geology corridor smelled the way it always did — floor wax and something faintly sulfurous, a ghost of whatever Priya's students had been burning off in the lab that morning. Tayak heard her before she saw her: the quick, flat slap of Priya's sandals on linoleum, a sound she had learned to recognize across three buildings.

"You have a face," Priya said, coming around the corner with a stack of printed cross-sections under one arm and a travel mug that had long since stopped being ironic. The mug said WORLD'S OKAYEST GEOLOGIST. "What happened?"

"I got the permit."

Priya stopped. She looked at Tayak the way she looked at core samples — carefully, without rushing to a conclusion. "And?"

"And I got the permit."

"Tayak."

"There's a clause." Tayak shifted her bag on her shoulder. The permit was in there, folded once, already soft at the crease from being handled. "State right of first refusal on anything deemed of significant historical value."

Priya set the cross-sections on the windowsill beside her. The window looked out onto the parking structure, and the afternoon light came through at an angle that turned the concrete a dull amber. "They'll use it."

"I know."

"The moment you find something that matters — and you're going to find something that matters — they'll use it."

"I know, Priya."

"So you signed it anyway."

It wasn't a question. Priya had a gift for that — for saying the thing that sounded like a question but wasn't, so that you had to answer it as a statement. Tayak had once found it charming. "The highway breaks ground in eleven weeks. If I don't have a permit, there's no excavation. If there's no excavation, they grade the site in the spring and we get nothing. Not even the chance to document what's there."

"So you signed it."

"Yes."

Priya picked up her travel mug and drank from it. The mug was printed in a font that had been cheerful once. "You know what the word 'significant' means in that clause."

"It means whatever the state decides it means."

"It means whatever the state decides it means when they want something." Priya leaned against the windowsill. She was shorter than Tayak by several inches, and she had a way of taking up exactly the space she needed and no more. "It's not a scientific term. It's an administrative one. You can't argue against it on the merits because it doesn't have merits. It has discretion."

"I know all of this."

"I know you know it. I'm saying it anyway because you look like someone who needs to hear it out loud."

Tayak almost said something sharp. She swallowed it. "It's like the LBW rule," she said instead. "Technically defined. Practically, it's whatever the umpire thinks."

Priya looked at her. "That's the worst cricket analogy you've ever made, and you've made some terrible ones."

"I thought it was pretty good."

"You're deflecting."

"I'm absolutely deflecting."

Priya smiled, briefly, and then stopped smiling. "Does Nora know?"

The corridor was quiet. Somewhere down the hall a door opened and closed, and footsteps went the other way. "She knows I've been working toward the permit for two years," Tayak said. "She knows the site."

"That's not what I asked."

Tayak looked at the floor. The linoleum was the color of old mustard, worn pale in the center of the corridor where a thousand students had walked the same line. "I'm driving down today."

"Good." Priya picked up her cross-sections. "Tell her everything."

The word sat in the air between them for a moment after Priya had already turned toward the lab door. Everything. Tayak stood with her bag on her shoulder and the permit inside it and thought about what everything would mean, said out loud in Nora's kitchen.

She had told Nora about the site. She had told her about the highway, the permit application, the two years of correspondence with the state office in Crownsville. She had told her about the survey work — the ground-penetrating radar pass they'd done in the fall, the anomalies in the data, the way the stratigraphy looked on screen like a layered argument waiting to be made.

She had not told her what she expected to find.

Not the full depth of it. Not the scope. Not the thing she had said to no one, not even Wren, because saying it would mean committing to it — that the mound might go back further than any documented Piscataway site in the county, further than the colonial-era deposits she could defend in a grant application, further than anything the state would accept without a fight. That if the radar anomalies meant what she thought they meant, the stratigraphy went down through centuries of continuous use, layer by layer, each one a life.

She had not told Nora that she thought the mound might be two thousand years old.

Down the hall, Priya's lab door swung shut with a soft, definitive click.

Tayak stood there another moment. Then she shifted her bag again and walked toward the stairs.

---

The construction staging area appeared before she expected it.

She had been watching for it — had told herself she wouldn't, then watched anyway — and still it caught her off guard, the way bad things do when you've spent the drive bracing for them. A harvested cornfield on the east side of 301, the stalks cut to shin height and the earth between them the color of old rust. And in it, parked in two loose rows like something casual, like something that had every right to be there: four excavators, a grader, and a line of orange barrel drums already staked along what would become the right-of-way. A portable site office, white and box-shaped, sat at the field's edge with a generator cable running into it. The generator was running. She could see the faint shimmer of exhaust above the roof.

Half a mile. That was all.

She drove past without slowing, because slowing felt like acknowledgment, and she was not ready to acknowledge that the machines were real. In her peripheral vision they stayed longer than they should have, the orange drums especially, the color designed to be seen and not ignored.

The permit was in her bag on the passenger seat. She had put it there instead of the back seat for reasons she hadn't examined.

Route 301 ran south through stands of loblolly pine that pressed close on both sides, their trunks straight and pale and close-spaced, the kind of woods that looked solid from a car window and turned out, if you walked into them, to be full of light. She had walked into woods like these as a child, following Nora, who never explained where they were going until they arrived. Past the pines came a Dollar General and a church with a reader board that said PRAYER IS THE ORIGINAL WIRELESS NETWORK and then more pines and then a cluster of new townhouses with vinyl siding the color of old mustard, their small yards not yet seeded, the red clay still raw and bare.

She had driven this road three hundred times. She knew every gas station, every speed trap, the exact place where the radio lost the college station and picked up the country one. She knew the mile marker where her grandmother's county began. She had driven it in the dark after her parents died, in the back of a car she didn't know, toward a house she would not leave for three years. She had driven it north to college and not looked back. She had driven it south every Thanksgiving and Christmas and sometimes in between, when she needed something she couldn't name and knew where to find it.

She had never driven it with a signed permit in her bag.

*Tell her everything*, Priya had said.

She had been rehearsing the conversation since College Park. She had tried several openings. *Nora, I found something.* No — Nora would say she already knew. *The permit came through.* True but insufficient. *I need to tell you what I think is in the mound.* Closer. But the word *think* was wrong. She didn't think. She had the survey data, the ground-penetrating radar, the soil core samples, the comparative stratigraphy from three other sites. She knew. She had known for eight months and had not said it plainly to anyone, not even Priya, not even in the phone calls with Marcus that had grown longer and more careful as the permit process dragged on.

What she knew was this: the mound had been used continuously, or near-continuously, for a very long time. The radar showed at least six distinct depositional events, each separated by a layer of undisturbed soil. The deepest layer was old. How old, she couldn't say without excavation. But old enough that saying it out loud felt like making a claim she had to be ready to defend in a room full of people who would want her to be wrong.

She had not said this to Nora because she did not know how Nora would take it. Which was, she understood now, a way of saying she was afraid of what Nora already knew.

The gas station outside La Plata was a BP with a hand-lettered sign in the window advertising crab seasoning by the pound. She pulled in without planning to, her hands making the turn while her mind was still on the road. She parked away from the pumps, in the side lot where the truckers left their rigs overnight, and turned off the engine.

The heat came in immediately. Late September, southern Maryland, and the air still had weight to it — not summer's full press but the memory of it, the humidity that came off the tidal creeks and didn't leave until November. Through the windshield the sky was the pale, washed-out blue of a day that had been overcast in the morning and cleared reluctantly.

She opened the glove compartment.

The map was folded the way she always kept it, the way her father had taught her: quadrant-fold, creases sharp, the cover sheet facing out so you could read the title without unfolding. USGS topographic survey, Charles County, Maryland, 1954. The paper was the color of cream that had been left out too long, soft at the folds, the blue of the contour lines faded to something closer to gray. She had found it at an estate sale in Waldorf six years ago, in a box of old National Geographics, and had known immediately what it was.

She unfolded it across the steering wheel.

The mound was there, as it always was, in the upland section northeast of the creek. The USGS surveyors had done their job with care: every elevation was marked, every drainage feature noted, the roads of 1954 laid in thin black lines across the landscape. The mound showed as a closed contour loop, a small oval rising perhaps twelve feet above the surrounding ground, sitting on a slight ridge above a seasonal wetland.

They had not named it.

Everything else on the map had a name. Farms, churches, crossroads, the creek itself. The mound sat in the middle of all that named territory with its contour lines drawn accurately around it and no label, no designation, not even the generic *tumulus* or *earthwork* that appeared on other USGS sheets from the same era when surveyors encountered such features. Just the elevation. Just the shape of the thing, recorded and set aside.

She had thought about this for six years. She had two explanations and she didn't like either of them. The first was that the surveyors had not known what it was. The second was that they had.

She ran her thumb along the oval of the contour line. The paper was cool under her finger despite the heat in the car, the way old paper always felt — like it was holding something in.

The permit was in her bag. The clause was on page four, paragraph seven, in the language that Marcus had called a trap and that she had signed anyway, because the alternative was watching those orange machines move half a mile east and not stop. *The State of Maryland retains right of first refusal on any artifact or assemblage deemed of significant historical value, as determined by the State Historic Preservation Officer in consultation with relevant parties.* Relevant parties. She was a relevant party. So was Garrett Slade, who was the State Historic Preservation Officer, who had taken eleven months to respond to her initial application and another seven to schedule the review.

She folded the map back along its creases and put it on the passenger seat, on top of her bag, on top of the permit.

Nora's house was four miles south. She had been sitting in this parking lot for eight minutes.

She thought about what Priya had said, and about the word *everything*, and about the eight months she had spent not saying the thing she knew plainly to the person who most needed to hear it. She thought about the machines in the cornfield, their engines cold for now, waiting for a start date that was eleven weeks out. She thought about the deepest layer of the mound, the one the radar had shown as a faint, dense anomaly at the base of everything, and the way the data looked when she had first pulled it up on her laptop at two in the morning — the way her hands had gone still on the keyboard, the way she had sat without moving for a long time.

She started the car.

She did not know what Nora would say. She did not know how much Nora already knew, or how she knew it, or what she had been waiting for Tayak to ask. She had been not-asking for a long time. She was four miles away from having to stop.

The screen door had never quite fit its frame. Tayak had been meaning to fix it since she was seventeen, and it still announced her the same way it always had — a sharp wooden knock, then the spring's complaint, then silence.

Nora didn't look up from the quilt.

"You got it," she said.

Not a question. Tayak stood in the kitchen doorway with her bag over one shoulder and the permit folded inside it and said nothing for a moment, because there was nothing to say to a statement.

"How did you know?"

"You'd have called if they'd said no." Nora smoothed a seam with the heel of her hand. The quilt covered her lap and spilled onto the linoleum in a wash of deep red and indigo, a pattern of interlocking diamonds that Tayak had grown up thinking was decoration. She was no longer sure. "Sit down. Show me."

The kitchen smelled the way it always smelled — coffee and the faint sweetness of the dried corn Nora kept in a clay pot on the windowsill, the corn going soft and wrinkled in the late-summer heat. A box fan in the window pushed the air around without cooling it. Tayak sat across from her grandmother and laid the permit on the table, spreading the pages flat.

Nora set down her needle. She picked up the permit and read it the way she had always read things — slowly, without expression, starting at the top and not skipping. Her hands were stiff with arthritis, the knuckles swollen, but she turned each page with care. She wore her reading glasses on a cord of braided sinew that Kimi had made her three years ago and that she refused to replace with anything newer.

She read the clause. Tayak watched her face. Nothing moved.

Nora set the permit down and aligned its edges with the edge of the table, the way she aligned everything.

"What do you think?" Tayak asked.

"I think you already signed it."

"I did."

Nora nodded once, as if this confirmed something she had expected rather than something she was hearing for the first time. She picked up her needle again. The quilt's pattern was a series of diamonds inside diamonds, each one a slightly different shade of red, and Tayak could not tell whether she was looking at something very old or something Nora had invented.

"The clause," Tayak said. "The right of first refusal. If we find anything the state decides is of significant historical value —"

"I read it."

"They could take it."

"I read it," Nora said again, not unkindly.

Tayak put her hands flat on the table. Outside, a car passed on the street, its bass thumping once through the walls and gone. The box fan turned. The corn sat in its pot.

"Then tell me what you think."

Nora pulled a length of red thread through the quilt and bit it off close. She looked up at Tayak over her glasses.

"I think you should start at the north edge," she said. "Not the center."

The room went very still.

"The north edge," Tayak said.

"Yes."

"How do you know there's a north edge worth starting at?"

Nora looked at her for a moment — not evasively, not guiltily, just steadily, the way she had looked at Tayak across this same table when Tayak was nine years old and asking questions that Nora had decided she was not yet old enough to hold. Then she folded the quilt. She did it methodically, bringing the corners together, smoothing each fold with her swollen hands, working with the patience of someone who had been folding things carefully for a very long time.

She stood, set the quilt on the counter beside the clay pot, and moved to the stove.

"You want coffee?"

"Nora."

"It's already made. I made it an hour ago." She lifted the pot. "It'll be strong."

Tayak watched her grandmother's back — the slight curve of her spine now, the deliberate way she moved between the stove and the cabinet, taking down two mugs without being asked. Her hair was white and pinned up with a wooden clip, and the back of her neck showed the same deep brown as her hands, the skin creased with age and sun.

She had made the coffee an hour ago. Before Tayak had arrived. Before Tayak had called. Before Tayak had decided, sitting in a parking lot four miles north, that she was ready to stop not-asking.

Tayak took the mug when Nora set it in front of her. The coffee was black and bitter and too hot to drink, and she wrapped both hands around it anyway.

"Thank you," she said.

Nora sat back down. She picked up her needle. She did not look up.

Outside, the late-September heat sat on the yard like something that hadn't yet accepted its season was ending. Somewhere down the street a child was calling out to someone, the words indistinct, just the shape of urgency. Tayak sat with her coffee and her permit and the question her grandmother had not answered, and she understood, with the particular clarity of things you have always half-known, that the silence was not an oversight.

It was a decision. It had been a decision for a long time.

She drove north with the permit in her bag and the question still in her hands — not the question of what was in the mound, but the older and more unsettling one: how long had Nora known, and why had she waited for Tayak to come and ask?
