# 04 — Chapter 1 — Present (sequential / pre-test)

*6,941 words · 5 sub-chapters · craft_passes=0.*

---

The envelope had been sitting on her desk for twenty minutes.

Tayak knew exactly how long because she had looked at the clock when she set it down — 9:47 — and she had looked at it again just now: 10:08. In between she had checked her email twice without reading anything, refilled her coffee mug, and stood at her window watching a maintenance crew drag a leaf blower across the quad in slow, indifferent arcs. The morning was already thick with June heat, the kind that pressed against the glass and made the brick buildings on the far side of the quad look slightly soft, slightly unreal.

The envelope was standard letter size, cream-colored, with the Maryland Historical Trust seal in the upper left corner — a small blue crab inside a shield that she had always found both charming and slightly absurd. Her name was printed in a clean sans-serif font. *Dr. Tayak Moyaone, Principal Investigator.* The envelope was thin. She had told herself for two years that a thin envelope was a good sign, that a rejection would come with forms and explanations and appeals language. She had also told herself that she had no idea what a thin envelope meant and that she was a scientist and should stop reading omens into paper.

Her office was the smallest in the department — she had been given it as a junior hire four years ago and had never been offered anything larger, though two people with less seniority now had windows on both sides. One wall was floor-to-ceiling shelves, every inch occupied: site reports in gray binders, ceramic reference volumes with cracked spines, a row of topographic maps she had rolled and rubber-banded and labeled in her own handwriting. The desk itself was a controlled wreck — a laptop, a hard plastic field clipboard with a half-finished site sketch clipped to it, a mechanical pencil she had been looking for yesterday, two coffee rings on a legal pad. The permit envelope sat in the only clear space, dead center, as if she had placed it there deliberately to give it authority.

She had.

She picked it up.

The paper was heavier than she expected, the kind of card stock that state agencies used when they wanted their correspondence to feel official rather than perfunctory. She slid her finger under the flap, tore it cleanly, and pulled out three pages stapled at the corner.

*Maryland Historical Trust. Office of Archaeology. Permit for Archaeological Investigations.*

*Site designation: 18CH447.*

*Principal Investigator: Dr. Tayak Moyaone.*

*Permit Status: APPROVED.*

She read it twice. Then she set it on the desk and pressed both palms flat against the edge, the way she sometimes braced against a trench wall when the ground shifted.

Two years. Two years of Phase I surveys and Phase II evaluations and letters to the Maryland State Highway Administration explaining, in language she had spent weeks calibrating, why a low rise in a soybean field in Charles County warranted more than a cursory look before SHA's Route 6 corridor study turned it into a drainage berm. Two years of Elliot Voss asking her, in his careful, almost-kind way, whether she was certain this was the best use of her pre-tenure time. Two years of Marcus telling her the council supported her and then not returning her calls for three weeks at a stretch. Two years of her grandmother saying nothing at all about the mound — not one word — which was its own kind of pressure, the specific weight of a silence that knew more than it was saying.

The permit was real. She had it.

She made herself read the full document, slowly, the way she had trained herself to read site reports — not skimming for the conclusion but moving through each clause in order, because the conclusion was always shaped by what came before it. The first page was standard: her credentials, the site designation, the approved scope of work. Phase III data recovery excavation. The language was dense and single-spaced, with COMAR section numbers running down the margin like a column of small, patient guards.

Page two covered curation requirements. All recovered artifacts to be deposited with an approved Maryland repository within ninety days of fieldwork completion. Acid-free bags and boxes. Digital catalog with specified fields. Field notes, maps, photographs, digital data — all of it. She had expected this. She had budgeted for it.

Then she reached the third page.

The heading read: *Terms and Conditions of Permit — Attachment A.*

She almost missed it. The clause was in the middle of a paragraph about reporting deadlines, written in the same dense font as everything else, as if it were simply one more administrative requirement among many. She read it once and then read it again, more slowly.

*The Maryland Historical Trust retains right of first refusal on any artifact or collection of artifacts recovered under this permit and subsequently determined by the Trust to be of significant historical or scientific value, as assessed under criteria established in COMAR 34.04.02. In the event the Trust exercises this right, the Principal Investigator and affiliated institution shall be notified in writing within sixty days of the Trust's determination. Transfer of such materials shall occur within thirty days of notification.*

She sat back.

The morning sounds of the building filled the silence around her — a door closing somewhere down the hall, the distant rhythm of the leaf blower outside, the HVAC system doing its slow, asthmatic work. Her coffee had gone cold. She looked at the Maryland Historical Trust seal at the top of the first page, the blue crab in its shield, and felt something she could not immediately name.

It was not surprise. She had known, in the abstract, that state permits carried ownership language. She had read enough cultural resource management reports to know that "significant historical or scientific value" was standard phrasing, the kind of clause that sat in permit templates for decades without ever being invoked. She had told herself it was boilerplate.

But she also knew what was in that mound. Or she suspected she knew. And suspicion, in her experience, was just knowledge that hadn't been confirmed yet.

*Right of first refusal.*

The state could look at what she pulled from the ground — what her people had put in the ground — and decide it belonged in a state repository. Not with the tribe. Not with the community that had maintained oral connection to this site for generations. With the Trust. With the same apparatus that had, for most of its history, treated Native material culture as data to be collected rather than heritage to be returned.

She thought about the highway. Route 6 corridor study. SHA's project timeline had a thirty-month window, which meant if she didn't excavate under permit, the site would be evaluated under Section 106 by a SHA contractor who had never heard of 18CH447 and whose job was to clear the path, not protect it. She had watched that happen to three sites in Prince George's County in the last decade. She had written two of the protest letters herself, and both had gone nowhere.

She picked up the mechanical pencil she had been looking for yesterday and turned it over in her fingers.

She could call Marcus. He would tell her to sign it, because Marcus always weighed the possible against the perfect and came down on the side of getting something done. She could call Elliot Voss, who would tell her the clause was standard and that she was overthinking it, and he would be right about the first part and wrong about the second. She could call her grandmother, who would say nothing useful on the phone and everything necessary in person, which was why Tayak was already planning to drive to La Plata this afternoon.

She looked at the permit for another moment.

Then she pulled the signature page toward her, uncapped a pen — a real one, ballpoint, because the agency still required a wet signature on the hard copy in addition to the DocuSign she had already submitted — and signed her name in the space above *Principal Investigator.*

Her handwriting was small and precise, each letter fully formed. She had her grandmother's hands.

She signed it without letting herself hesitate, because hesitation was a luxury she had spent two years not having. The alternative was losing the site. The alternative was SHA's contractor standing in that soybean field with a backhoe and a thirty-day window and no one who knew what they were looking at. The alternative was the ground giving up what it held to a drainage ditch on the shoulder of a county road.

She would sign it. She would excavate. And she would deal with the clause when the time came.

She folded the permit into thirds, slid it into her bag between her field clipboard and the rolled topographic map she had been carrying for two years — the 1967 USGS quad sheet for the La Plata area, the one that showed the mound as an unlabeled contour anomaly, a small closed loop that looked like nothing unless you knew what you were looking for — and stood up.

The feeling she could not name had not gone away. It sat in her chest, slightly to the left of center, the way a stone sits at the bottom of a stream: not moving, not going anywhere, just present.

She picked up her keys.

She called Marcus from the parking lot, standing beside her car with the door open and the heat already rising off the asphalt in slow, visible waves. It was not yet ten o'clock.

He picked up on the second ring.

"You got it," he said. Not a question. He had been waiting.

"I got it."

"Then why do you sound like that?"

She looked across the lot toward the building she had just left — a four-story block of beige concrete with a Maryland flag hanging limp in the still air. "There's a clause."

A pause. "Read it to me."

She had the permit out of her bag before he finished the sentence. She found the page, found the paragraph, and read it to him, flat and even, the way she read site reports: *right of first refusal, significant historical or scientific value, COMAR 34.04.02, sixty days, thirty days.*

When she finished, Marcus was quiet.

She waited. A pickup truck pulled out of the lot, its exhaust hanging in the heat.

"Don't sign it," he said.

"I've already signed it."

Another pause, longer. "Tayak."

"The highway breaks ground in eleven weeks."

"I know when the highway breaks ground."

"Then you know I didn't have a choice."

"You had a choice." His voice was careful, the way it got when he was working to keep it that way. "You had the choice to call me before you signed."

She leaned against the car door. The metal was hot through her shirt. "I'm calling you now."

"That's not the same thing and you know it."

She did know it. She said nothing.

"That clause is a trap," Marcus said. "You pull something significant out of that mound — and we both know you will — and the state walks in with a sixty-day letter and takes it. Not us. Not the community. The Trust. The same people who spent thirty years telling us we weren't a real tribe."

"I know what the Trust is."

"Then why did you sign?"

"Because the alternative is SHA's contractor standing in that field with a backhoe," she said. "I've watched that happen. Prince George's County, twice. The Accokeek site. You were at the Accokeek meeting."

"I was at the Accokeek meeting."

"So you know what happens when we don't excavate under permit. The site gets a Section 106 review by someone whose job is to clear the path. They write *not eligible* in the box and the machine moves."

"I know how Section 106 works."

"Then tell me the alternative." She heard her own voice go flat. "Tell me the legal strategy that protects 18CH447 in the next eleven weeks without a signed permit."

He didn't answer immediately. Through her phone she could hear him breathing, and behind that the faint sound of his office — the hum of a window unit, something being set down on a desk.

"The clause gives them everything," he said finally. "You excavate, you find something significant — and Tayak, if what you've told me about that site is accurate, everything in it is significant — and then the Trust invokes the clause and we spend the next three years fighting them for repatriation through NAGPRA while the artifacts sit in a repository in Annapolis labeled and catalogued and completely out of our hands."

"I know."

"You know, and you signed anyway."

"I signed anyway."

Another silence. A red-winged blackbird landed on the chain-link fence at the edge of the lot, balanced for a moment, and was gone.

"Marcus," she said. "The mound being paved over is a worse trap."

He made a sound she had heard before — not quite a laugh, not quite agreement. The sound he made when he thought she was right about something he wished she was wrong about.

"You should have called me first," he said.

"I know."

"We needed to make this decision together."

"I know." She kept her voice level. "But the permit window was closing and I made a call. I'll deal with the clause when the time comes."

"How?"

"I don't know yet."

"That's not a plan."

"It's what I have."

He was quiet for a moment. Then: "When are you going to La Plata?"

"This afternoon."

"Your grandmother know you're coming?"

"She always knows."

"Tell her I said hello." A pause. "And Tayak — next time, you call me before. Not after."

"Understood," she said.

She ended the call and stood for a moment with the phone warm in her hand. The sun was full on the parking lot now, and the air smelled of cut grass from somewhere across the road and, faintly, of the fertilizer coming off the soybean fields to the south. Somewhere in the tree line at the lot's edge, cicadas were already going, a sound like a slow electrical charge building toward something.

She had not told him she had already signed before she called.

She got in the car.

The geology corridor smelled the way it always did — old coffee and the particular mineral tang of rock samples stored in open drawers, a smell Tayak had never been able to name but always recognized. She was moving fast, permit folded into her bag, keys in her hand, when she heard her name.

"Tayak."

Priya Suresh was leaning out of her office doorway with a coffee mug that said SOME PEOPLE JUST NEED A HIGH-FIVE IN THE FACE, a gift from her graduate students that she used without irony. She was in field clothes — worn canvas trousers, a University of Maryland pullover with a small bleach stain on the left cuff — which meant she had been in the core lab since early morning.

"You got the permit," Priya said. Not a question either. Apparently everyone already knew.

"Word travels."

"Marcus texted me." Priya stepped back from the doorway. "Come in. You have five minutes."

Tayak had three, but she went in.

Priya's office was the size of a generous closet and organized with a precision that made Tayak's own office look like a dig site. Rock cores stood in labeled racks along one wall. A topographic map of the Chesapeake watershed covered most of the desk, weighted at the corners by a compass, a stapler, a smooth piece of serpentine, and a coffee-ringed copy of the *Journal of Sedimentary Research*. Priya dropped into her chair and looked at Tayak over the rim of her mug.

"Show me."

Tayak set the permit on the desk. Priya read it the way geologists read everything — quickly, without expression, going straight for the thing she wanted. She found it in under a minute.

"There." She tapped the page with one finger. "Right of first refusal. Significant historical or scientific value." She looked up. "They'll use it."

"You sound like Marcus."

"Marcus is right."

"Marcus is always right after the fact." Tayak picked the permit up and put it back in her bag. "Before the fact he was telling me to hold off on the Phase Three application until after the tribal recognition decision."

"Would that have been so terrible?"

"The recognition decision could take another two years. SHA breaks ground in eleven weeks." Tayak sat on the edge of Priya's desk because there was no other place to sit. "The mound gets paved over or I excavate under a permit with a clause I don't like. Those are the options."

Priya turned her mug in both hands, thinking. She had a habit of going very still when she was working something out, a stillness that Tayak had mistaken for disapproval when they first met and had since learned to read correctly.

"The clause is standard language," Priya said. "I've seen it in mineral rights agreements. The question isn't whether it's there. The question is who decides what counts as significant."

"The Trust."

"And who sits on the Trust's review board?"

"Voss," Tayak said. "Among others."

Priya made a sound. "How is Elliot these days?"

"He sent me a very warm email this morning congratulating me on the permit."

"Ah." Priya set her mug down. "And how long before he realizes your findings are going to make his Chesapeake trade-network monograph look thin?"

"He may already realize. The warmth of the email suggested some anxiety."

Priya almost smiled. "So. You have a permit, a clause, Voss on the review board, eleven weeks, and Marcus angry with you."

"Marcus isn't angry. He's disappointed. He's worse when he's disappointed."

"And Slade?"

The name sat in the room for a moment.

"Slade signed the permit as the state's representative," Tayak said. "He was very professional about it."

"He's always professional." Priya picked up her mug again. "That's what makes him dangerous. An unprofessional opponent makes mistakes. Slade doesn't make mistakes."

"He made one," Tayak said. "He let me excavate."

"You think that was a mistake on his part?"

She had thought about this in the car on the way over, and she thought about it again now, and she still didn't have a clean answer. "I think he calculated that the permit with the clause was safer for the state than letting SHA pave the site and dealing with the publicity. Kimi's been building the social media pressure for eight months. A bulldozed burial mound plays badly on video."

"So he gave you the permit to control the outcome."

"He gave me the permit because he thought the clause was enough control."

Priya looked at her steadily. "Is it?"

Tayak didn't answer.

"Right," Priya said. "Different question. What do you expect to find?"

"Stratified deposits. At least three distinct occupation phases. Possibly four." She kept her voice even. "There's a low rise at the south end of the site that the Phase Two evaluation barely touched. The magnetometry data suggests a feature complex — pits, possibly a burial area. The surface scatter alone runs from late colonial contact period down to —" She stopped.

"Down to what?"

"Down to something older than anything that's been formally documented in Charles County."

Priya was quiet for a moment. Outside the office door, two undergraduates passed in the corridor, their voices dropping as they went by, the way students' voices always dropped near faculty offices, as though the rooms were listening.

"How old?" Priya said.

"I don't know yet. The magnetometry isn't specific enough. But the anomaly depth at the south end —" Tayak looked at the topographic map on the desk, the familiar contour lines of the watershed, and felt the thing she had been carrying since she left the parking lot shift slightly in her chest. "It's significant. Whatever it is, it's significant."

"Then Slade's clause is a problem."

"Yes."

"You already knew that when you signed."

"Yes."

Priya looked at her for a moment with the particular expression she reserved for graduate students who had given her a correct answer by a flawed method. Then she picked up a mechanical pencil from the desk and turned it in her fingers.

"You know what I don't understand about cricket?" Tayak said.

"You understand cricket perfectly well."

"The tea break. I've never understood the tea break."

"The tea break is the best part of cricket. It is the only civilized thing about any sport." Priya set the pencil down. "You're deflecting."

"I'm deflecting."

"Does Nora know about the site?"

The question arrived the way Priya's questions usually did — direct, without softening, aimed at the thing Tayak had been not quite looking at.

"She knows I've been working toward the permit," Tayak said. "She knows it's Charles County. She knows the general area."

"That's not what I asked."

"I know it's not what you asked."

"Does she know what you expect to find?"

Tayak looked at the topographic map. The Potomac watershed, the tributaries, the low contour lines of Charles County's bottomland — ground she had walked since she was a child, ground her grandmother had walked before her, ground that had been in her family's memory for longer than any county map acknowledged.

"No," she said.

"You're driving down today?"

"This afternoon."

Priya picked up her coffee. "Tell her everything."

The word sat there. *Everything.* Tayak turned it over the way she turned artifacts — looking for the edge, the break, the place where the thing had been used or damaged or deliberately made.

She had told Nora about the permit application. She had told her about the Phase Two evaluation, the magnetometry survey, the SHA timeline. She had told her the site number — 18CH447 — and explained what the trinomial meant, the way she always explained things to Nora, carefully and completely, because Nora always listened carefully and completely and asked the questions that mattered.

She had not told her about the anomaly depth. She had not told her what the depth suggested about the age of the lowest deposits. She had not told her that she had lain awake two nights ago running the numbers in her head and arrived, both times, at a figure that made her hands go still.

She had not told Nora any of that because she had not yet decided what it meant.

"I'll tell her," Tayak said.

Priya nodded once, the way she nodded when she believed something and wanted it on record. "Good." She looked back at the topographic map. "You know what the clause actually means, don't you? Not legally. What it means."

Tayak stood up from the desk edge. "It means if I find what I think I'm going to find, the state gets to decide what it's worth."

"And who it belongs to."

"Yes."

"So tell Nora everything," Priya said. "Before you open that ground. Because once you do —"

"I know."

"— the clause starts running."

Tayak picked up her bag. The permit shifted inside it, dense and folded, the Terms and Conditions attachment still creased from where she had pressed it flat to read it in the parking lot. She thought of Marcus's voice: *That's not a plan. It's what I have.*

"Thank you for the coffee you didn't give me," she said.

"Any time." Priya was already turning back to her cores. "Drive safe. And Tayak —"

She stopped at the door.

"Whatever's in that mound," Priya said, without looking up, "it waited two thousand years. It can wait for you to have the right conversation first."

Tayak walked out into the corridor. The mineral smell of the geology building followed her a few steps, then the air changed — the warm, cut-grass heaviness of the Maryland summer coming through the propped-open door at the far end — and she kept walking, toward it.

Route 301 south out of College Park ran through the kind of country that couldn't decide what it was. Strip malls gave way to soybean fields, then a Wawa, then a stretch of loblolly pines with a cell tower rising out of them like a bad idea someone had already committed to. Tayak kept both hands on the wheel and the radio off.

The bag sat on the passenger seat. She had moved it there from the back when she stopped for gas at the Beltway — she didn't want to think about why. The permit was inside it, seventeen pages of Maryland Historical Trust letterhead with the state seal watermarked faint behind the text, and the Terms and Conditions attachment folded separately, and her own signature at the bottom of page sixteen, above the MHT representative's, both of them in the slightly too-dark ink of a PDF printed and signed and scanned back in the same afternoon.

She had two years of her life in that bag. Maybe more. The Phase One survey, the Phase Two evaluation, the magnetometry, the correspondence with Marcus about tribal consultation letters, the three rounds of revisions to her research design, the argument with Voss about whether the university's name went first or the tribe's on the principal investigator line. Two years of working the SHA timeline like a second job, because the highway project had a federal nexus — MDOT money, a federal permit — which meant Section 106 review, which meant the state couldn't just pave over 18CH447 without going through the Trust, which meant there was a window, and the window had a closing date, and the closing date was why she had signed a permit she didn't entirely trust.

She passed a Baptist church with a marquee sign out front: *GOD KNOWS YOUR ADDRESS.* She thought about the anomaly depth.

The numbers were not complicated. The magnetometry showed a strong ferrous anomaly at the south end of the mound feature, roughly a meter and a half below present surface, with a secondary anomaly at approximately two point eight meters. The first was consistent with late precontact or early contact period deposits — sixteenth, seventeenth century, maybe. The second was not consistent with anything that had been formally documented in Charles County. Two point eight meters of undisturbed stratigraphy in a low-rise earthen feature on a wooded ridge above a Potomac tributary meant something had been placed there, deliberately, a very long time ago.

She had run the numbers twice. Both times her hands had gone still.

A pickup truck with a landscaping trailer swung out in front of her and she braked without thinking, the bag sliding forward on the seat. She steadied it with her right hand, the way you'd steady something that might break.

---

The construction staging area appeared before she expected it.

She had known it was there — she had seen it on the SHA project map, a roughly rectangular parcel marked *Contractor Access/Equipment Storage* in the orange of preliminary work. Knowing it was there was different from seeing it. The field had been a soybean field last spring. Now it was bare clay, orange-brown and flat, and across it sat four pieces of equipment: a grader, two excavators, and something she didn't have a name for, a low machine with a wide steel drum, parked in a line like animals waiting to be fed. A chain-link fence ran along the road edge, and a white sign read *MDOT SHA PROJECT 4-447-0031-3 — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY.*

The project number matched the one on page three of her permit.

She drove past slowly. The machines did not move. The clay field was maybe half a mile from the mound — she couldn't see the tree line from here, but she knew the ridge. She had walked that ridge in October with Wren, taking surface readings, and the leaves had been coming down and the air had smelled of wet wood and old soil, and Wren had said, *There's something under here*, and Tayak had said, *I know*, and neither of them had said anything else for a while.

The machines were orange. The clay was orange. The permit in her bag was white.

She drove on.

---

The gas station outside La Plata was a Shell with a Subway attached, the kind of place that had been there since the nineties and would be there until something more profitable replaced it. She pulled in without planning to and sat with the engine running for a moment, then turned it off.

The heat came in immediately. Southern Maryland in June had a particular weight to it — not just warm but pressing, the air thick with cut grass and something underneath that, something biological, the smell of the marsh half a mile east where the land dropped toward the Wicomico. A red-winged blackbird was working the scrub at the edge of the parking lot, its shoulder patches the color of a stop sign, and it did not care about her at all.

She reached into the glove compartment and took out the map.

It was a USGS topographic survey, 1954, the Charles County quadrant, folded into eighths and refolded so many times the creases had gone soft. She had found it at an estate sale in Waldorf her first year of graduate school — a box of old county maps, two dollars for the lot — and she had kept this one because of what it showed, or rather because of what it didn't.

She unfolded it across the steering wheel.

The contour lines were printed in brown, thin and precise, the way the USGS drew them — every ten feet of elevation change rendered as a line, the land's shape translated into geometry. She found the ridge without looking for it, the way you found a face you knew in a crowd. The mound sat at an elevation of roughly ninety feet above sea level, a small rise above the creek drainage, and the 1954 survey showed it as a closed contour — a loop of brown line indicating an elevation higher than the surrounding ground, unlabeled, with no annotation.

There was a symbol for a burial mound in the USGS cartographic standards. The mapmakers had not used it.

They had drawn the contour. They had seen the rise. They had decided not to name it.

She traced the closed loop with her thumbnail, not pressing hard enough to mark the paper. The creek ran below it, the same creek she had walked in October. The ridge was the same ridge. The ground was the same ground it had always been, under the soybeans, under the clay, under the chain-link fence half a mile north.

She thought about what she would say to Nora.

*Grandmother, I found the site.* Too flat — Nora already knew she'd been working toward it.

*Grandmother, I got the permit.* True but incomplete. The permit was not the point.

*Grandmother, there's something in the mound that's older than anything on record in this county, and I signed a document that gives the state the right to take it, and I don't know yet if I made the right call, and I need you to tell me —*

Tell her what? What did she need Nora to tell her?

She folded the map back along its old creases and held it in both hands. The red-winged blackbird had moved on. The Subway sign hummed. A contractor van pulled in and a man in a yellow safety vest got out and went inside without looking at her.

She had been rehearsing this conversation for two hours and she still didn't know what she was actually asking. She knew what she would report: the permit, the clause, the timeline, the SHA project number that matched the staging area she had just driven past. She could give Nora all of that. She was good at giving Nora information.

What she could not figure out how to give her was the thing underneath the information. The thing that had been shifting in her chest since the parking lot in Annapolis, that had moved when Priya asked *Does she know what you expect to find?* and had not settled since.

She put the map back in the glove compartment.

She thought: *Nora already knows.* Not the anomaly depth, not the magnetometry numbers, not the SHA project code. But the ground itself — the ridge, the creek, the closed contour the mapmakers had drawn and refused to name. Nora had grown up two miles from that ridge. Her grandmother had grown up two miles from that ridge. The ground had been in the family's memory for longer than any county survey acknowledged.

*Whatever's in that mound, it waited two thousand years.*

She started the car.

The question she could not shake — the one that had nothing to do with the permit or the clause or the SHA timeline — was whether Nora had always known what was waiting, and had been waiting herself for Tayak to be the one to ask.

Nora's house sat on a quarter-acre lot on Billingsley Road, behind a row of white oaks that had been there longer than the road itself. The porch light was on even though it was still afternoon — Nora kept it on from May through September, had done so for as long as Tayak could remember, some habit from a time before the neighborhood filled in and the nights got less dark.

Tayak parked in the gravel strip beside the house and sat for a moment with the engine off. Through the kitchen window she could see the light was on and the shape of her grandmother at the table, bent over something in her lap. The window glass was old, slightly wavy, the kind that made the world inside look like it was underwater.

She got out, took the permit folder from the passenger seat, and went up the porch steps.

She did not knock. She had not knocked at this door since she was eleven years old.

The kitchen smelled of coffee and something older — the cedar chest in the back room, maybe, or the particular smell of the house itself, which was wood and old fabric and the faint sweet rot of the white oaks outside. Nora was at the table with a quilt spread across her lap and onto the chair beside her, a mass of deep blue and ochre fabric in a pattern Tayak had seen her working on for three months. Her reading glasses were pushed up on her forehead. She looked up when Tayak came through the door, and she said, without any preamble:

"You got it."

Not a question. The same tone she used when she told you the weather.

Tayak set the folder on the table. "This morning."

Nora reached for it without hurry, the way she reached for things — no wasted motion, no performance of eagerness. Her hands were thick-knuckled now, the arthritis worse this spring, but they moved with the same deliberateness they always had. She opened the folder and began to read.

Tayak sat down across from her and watched.

Nora read the way she did everything: completely. She turned the first page, then the second, then the attachment — the Terms and Conditions, single-spaced, the font slightly smaller than the rest, the kind of text designed to be signed without reading. Nora read it. Her face gave nothing away. The quilt pooled in her lap, the ochre squares catching the afternoon light through the wavy glass.

She set the permit down on the table and smoothed it flat with one hand, the way you smoothed a letter you were going to keep.

Tayak said, "What do you think?"

Nora looked at the permit for another moment. Then she said, "I think you should start at the north edge. Not the center."

The kitchen was quiet. Outside, a cardinal was doing something emphatic in the white oaks.

"The north edge," Tayak said.

"Yes."

"Nora." She kept her voice even. "How do you know there's a north edge worth starting at?"

Nora looked up then. Her eyes behind the pushed-up glasses were dark and steady, the same eyes Tayak had been trying to read her whole life.

She did not answer.

Instead she reached down and began to fold the quilt — the long, practiced fold that halved it, then halved it again, her thick-knuckled hands finding the edges without looking. The ochre and blue disappeared into itself. She set the folded quilt on the chair beside her with a care that was almost ceremonial.

"Do you want coffee?" she said.

"Nora—"

"The pot's fresh. I made it an hour ago." She was already getting up, one hand on the table for balance, moving toward the counter with the careful deliberateness of someone whose hips hurt but who had decided not to make it anyone else's problem.

Tayak looked at the permit on the table. At the Terms and Conditions page, which Nora had read and smoothed flat and left face-up, the dense single-spaced text visible, the clause about significant historical value right there in paragraph four where anyone looking could find it.

She looked at her grandmother's back.

Nora took two mugs from the cabinet — the heavy ceramic ones, dark green, the ones that had been in this kitchen since Tayak was small — and poured without being asked whether Tayak wanted sugar, because she knew, and set both mugs on the table, and sat back down.

She wrapped both hands around her own mug. The arthritis, Tayak thought, or the habit of warmth, or both.

"Drink it before it gets cold," Nora said.

Tayak picked up the mug. The coffee was strong and slightly bitter, the way Nora had always made it. She drank.

The question she had not been able to ask in the Subway parking lot, the question she had been rehearsing for two hours, the question that had ridden with her all the way down Route 301 past the gas stations and the car dealerships and the flagpole in front of the county courthouse — it was still there. It had not gone anywhere. It sat in the kitchen between them like a third person at the table.

Nora had read the permit. She had read the clause. She had said *north edge* the way you said a thing you had always known, not a thing you were guessing at.

Tayak did not ask again. She understood, in the way you understood things in this kitchen, that the question would not be answered today. That Nora had decided something — when to tell her, how much, in what order — and that the decision had been made before Tayak walked through the door, possibly long before, possibly longer than Tayak wanted to calculate.

She looked at the permit. At the quilt, folded on the chair, its pattern hidden now.

She thought: *How long have you known?*

She did not say it out loud.

She drank her coffee. Nora drank hers. The cardinal outside had moved on to a different argument. The afternoon light came through the wavy old glass and lay across the table in a shape that was almost a map — elongated, distorted, the edges uncertain.

When Tayak stood to go, Nora said, without looking up from her mug, "Take the permit with you. You'll need it."

Tayak picked it up. She folded it back into the folder and held it against her chest.

At the door she stopped and turned. Nora was still at the table, both hands around the mug, looking at the window. Not at Tayak. At the glass, or through it, at something in the yard or beyond the yard, in the direction of the white oaks and the road and the two miles of low wooded country between this kitchen and the ridge.

Tayak said, "I'll call you when we're on site."

"I know you will," Nora said.

Outside, the air hit her — June heat, heavy and green, smelling of the oaks and the grass and underneath that the faint salt-rot of the marsh. She walked to the car. She put the permit on the passenger seat. She sat for a moment with her hands on the wheel and looked at the house, at the kitchen window, at the shape of her grandmother still at the table.

She had been asking the wrong question.

Not *does Nora know what's in the mound.*

The question was *how long has she been waiting for me to be ready to find out.*

She started the car. The permit sat on the seat beside her, its Terms and Conditions face-down now, the state seal on the cover page facing up — the outline of Maryland, the Calvert arms, the bureaucratic weight of a document that gave her the right to open the ground.

She pulled out of the gravel strip and onto Billingsley Road, and the white oaks closed behind her, and the house disappeared from the mirror, and she drove toward the site with the feeling that the real excavation had not started yet — and that when it did, what she found would be something her grandmother had been tending, in her own way, for longer than Tayak had been alive.
