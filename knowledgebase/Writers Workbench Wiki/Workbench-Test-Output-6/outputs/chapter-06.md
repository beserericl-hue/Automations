# Chapter 6: The Woman Who Ran [Past — 1693]

*7,049 words · aligned=True · drift=0+0 · craft_passes=0 · cache_read=40716 tokens · persisted to DB (chapter_number 6).*

---

The fire had burned to coals.

Saya lay on her side and watched the orange pulse of them through the smoke hole's faint light, feeling the cold press down on the shelter like a hand. Around her, the others breathed in their sleeping robes — her daughter Lena curled tight as a fist against her back, Okwi on his far side with his face turned to the wall, the two older women near the door who had stopped speaking to each other in October and had not started again. The camp was still. Even the dogs had stopped moving.

She had not slept.

The papers lay folded against her chest, tucked inside her wool overdress where body heat had kept them from going stiff. She could feel their edges when she breathed — a faint pressure, like a hand laid flat. She had written the draft three times. The first two had smeared in the damp, the iron-gall ink running where her fingers had pressed too hard, and she had scraped the quill down to a fresh nib and begun again on the last good sheet. Now the words were there, fixed and dry, and she could not stop touching them through the cloth to make sure.

Three days.

That was what Tomah had said when he came in from the eastern path two nights ago, his leggings dark with mud from the creek crossings, his breath coming in hard pulls. *Survey party. Three days east, maybe less if the ground stays hard.* He had not said more than that. He had not needed to.

She knew what a survey meant. She had watched it happen to the Piscataway band at Zekiah — the men with the long chain walking the wood line, their compass swinging, a boy behind them with a hatchet making blaze marks on the oaks. After the chain came the patent. After the patent came the paper in the courthouse, and after the paper came the men who said *this is ours now, you are here on our sufferance*, and after that came nothing, because there was nothing left to argue.

The mound was two miles north of camp. She had been to it three times in the past year, standing on its low crown in the autumn light, trying to understand what it meant that her people had always known it was there and the colonists had never thought to ask why. It was not large. It rose maybe eight feet from the forest floor, rounded and quiet under the oak leaves, the kind of thing a surveyor's eye might pass over as a natural rise — or might not. If a surveyor recorded it as a landmark, it would anchor a boundary line. The line would be drawn on paper. The paper would go to the courthouse. And then the mound would be on the wrong side of someone else's patent, and no petition in the world would undo it.

She sat up slowly, careful not to wake Lena.

The bead moved at her throat as she rose — she felt it settle, the familiar small weight of it. Her mother had worn it on a twisted cord of sinew that had been replaced twice in Saya's lifetime, the bead itself unchanged: a deep blue glass, the color of river water in late afternoon, traded up from the coast through Lenape hands. Her mother had called it the color of a thing that does not end. Saya had not known what she meant until the morning they laid her mother in the ground and she had felt the bead against her collarbone and understood that this was what remained when everything else was taken.

She would not let the mound be taken.

She pulled her deerskin mantle over the wool dress and moved to the fire pit, crouching to lay two pieces of oak on the coals. The wood caught slowly, the bark hissing where frost had touched it. She held her hands over the small flame and felt the cold in her knuckles begin to ease.

The petition was formal. She had worked hard to make it formal — the language respectful, the grievances laid out in order, the request specific. *We ask the assembly to confirm our right to the land known as —* and here she had used both the English name and the Piscataway name, side by side, because she wanted the men who read it to understand that the land had a name before they arrived and would have one after they left. She had asked Tomah to check the English phrasing, and he had changed three words and looked at her with something she could not quite read. Worry, maybe. Or the particular kind of respect that comes with watching someone do a hard thing well.

The assembly would not meet until spring. She knew that. The petition would have to travel to Annapolis through channels she had spent two years learning to navigate — first to the county court, then through a sympathetic planter who owed Okwi a debt for three canoes, then to the assembly clerk. It would take weeks. It might take longer. And none of that mattered if the survey party reached the mound first and drew their lines and wrote their marks on the trees.

Three days.

She pulled the papers from inside her dress and unfolded them carefully in the firelight. The ink was dark and clean, the letters even. She had a good hand — her father had taught her, a strange gift from a man who had himself been taught by a Jesuit and had spent the rest of his life deciding how he felt about that. She read the petition from the first line to the last, her lips barely moving, checking each sentence the way she checked a canoe seam before a crossing.

It was good. She believed it was good. She believed it was the kind of document that could move a man who was willing to be moved.

That was the fear she could not say aloud, even to Okwi: that the assembly might contain no such man. That the petition might be perfectly written and perfectly argued and perfectly useless. That the chain had already been laid on the ground somewhere east of here and the oak already blazed and the mound already fixed in someone's field notes as a landmark for a line that would take everything.

She folded the papers again and pressed them back against her chest.

Outside, a crow called once in the dark — sharp and close, then gone. The fire steadied. Lena made a small sound in her sleep and drew her knees tighter, and Saya watched her daughter's breathing until it settled again into the long slow rhythm of a child who does not yet know what three days means.

*I will reach the council before midday*, she thought. *I will lay the petition before the elders and I will make them understand.*

She touched the bead at her throat with two fingers — not a prayer exactly, more a habit, the way you touch a sore place to know it is still there — and held the warmth of the fire against her face and waited for the light.

The light came in gray, without ceremony.

Saya was already moving when the first pale strip showed through the trees to the east — her mantle pulled tight, the petition folded twice and tucked inside the wool dress against her ribs, where her body heat would keep the paper from the damp. She had left Lena sleeping, her daughter's small face turned toward the coals, and she had not let herself look back at the doorway.

The forest took her in.

She did not run. Running was noise — cracked branches, labored breath, the slap of feet on frozen ground — and noise in a winter wood carried farther than she liked. Instead she moved at the pace she had learned as a girl following her father through these same trees: weight forward, each foot placed before it was trusted, the body low enough to see under the branches. Faster than a walk. Quieter than a run. The kind of movement that left no story behind it.

The cold had sharpened overnight. The mud of the previous day had locked solid, and the leaf litter underfoot was stiff and pale, each leaf edged with a thin white crust that crunched softly under her moccasins. She could feel the cold moving up through the soles. By the time she reached the first ridge her feet had gone from cold to numb to a dull ache she had learned not to think about.

The petition shifted against her ribs with each stride. She was aware of it the way she was aware of the bead at her throat — a constant small presence, a thing she could not afford to lose.

---

She reached the split oak before the light had fully come.

It stood where it had always stood, at the place where the deer path bent north toward the frozen creek — a white oak that lightning had divided forty years ago or more, the two halves grown wide apart now, each as thick as a man's reach, the gap between them large enough for a child to stand in. She had stood in it herself, once, while her father counted the rings of a fallen tree nearby and told her that the oak had been standing when his grandfather's grandfather was young. She had not understood what that meant then. She understood it now.

She passed through the gap without slowing, her shoulder brushing the rough bark, and felt the cold air funnel between the trunks and push against her face.

*Still here*, she thought. *Still here.*

---

The creek was frozen at the bend.

Not solid — she did not trust creek ice in a winter this irregular, where cold and thaw traded off every few days — but locked at the edges, a shelf of grey-white ice extending two feet out from each bank, the dark water still moving in the narrow channel between. She crossed where she always crossed, at the place where three flat stones broke the surface in a line, the stones themselves rimed with ice now, slick enough that she crossed them sideways, one foot at a time, her arms out for balance. The petition pressed against her ribs. She did not let herself think about falling.

The far bank was a short scramble up frozen clay, and she took it on her hands and knees, her fingers finding the exposed root she had used as a handhold since she was tall enough to reach it. The root was cold and hard as iron. She pulled herself up and stood on the bank and breathed.

Her breath came in white plumes. The wood was very still. Somewhere to the north a woodpecker worked at a dead tree — a dry, hollow knocking that stopped and started and stopped again, the sound carrying cleanly through the cold air.

She pressed the papers against her ribs with one hand to feel them still there, and went on.

---

The ridge was the last landmark before the camp, and it was her father's place more than anywhere else she knew.

He had brought her here on a morning in early winter — she could not have been more than eight — and stood behind her and turned her to face east and said: *watch the birds*. She had watched. A line of geese had come over low, their wings beating in a slow, heavy rhythm, their calls fading south. Her father had said something about the direction they flew and what it meant for the weather, and she had tried to remember the lesson but what she remembered instead was the particular quality of the light that morning, the way it lay flat and pale across the tops of the trees below, and her father's hand on her shoulder, and the feeling that she was being shown something that mattered.

He had died the winter after, of a fever that moved through the camp in three days and took four people with it. She had been nine.

She stood on the ridge now and looked east, the way he had shown her. The sky was the color of old pewter, flat and low. No geese. The light was still thin, the sun not yet over the tree line, but she could see the shape of the land below — the long hollow where the creek ran, the dark mass of the pines on the far side, the pale strip of the frozen marsh beyond. She had walked every part of it. Her father had walked it. His father before him.

The survey party would walk it too, in three days, with their chain and their compass and their field notes, and they would write down what they saw and they would not know what any of it was called.

She turned and went down the far side of the ridge at a near-run, her feet finding the path by memory in the dim light, the cold air burning in her throat.

---

She smelled the camp before she saw it.

Wood smoke first — the particular smell of oak burning low, dense and sweet — and then the sharper smell of something cooking, fat rendered down over coals. Her stomach answered before she could stop it. She had not eaten since the night before.

The camp came into view through the last stand of trees: a cluster of shelters set back from a frozen stream, the bark walls dark with moisture, smoke rising from two fires in thin white columns that bent east in the light wind. A dog lifted its head as she came out of the trees, then settled again. Two women near the larger fire looked up. One of them was Mara, who had been her mother's closest friend and who looked at Saya now with the particular expression she wore when she was deciding how worried to be.

Saya pushed the hood of her mantle back from her face. Her cheeks were burning from the cold. Her feet, she realized, had gone entirely numb below the ankle, and she was walking on them without feeling them, which was not a thing she should continue to do.

She reached the edge of the fire and stopped. The heat hit her face like a hand.

"The survey party," she said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended, scraped by the cold air. "Three days. Maybe less."

Mara looked at the other woman — a quick, private exchange — and then back at Saya.

"Sit down," Mara said. "Your feet are wrong."

"I need the elders —"

"Sit *down*." Mara's voice was not loud. It did not need to be. "The elders will hear you better if you can stand when you speak to them."

Saya sat. The ground was frozen hard and she felt it through her leggings, but the fire's heat was already working at her face and hands. She pulled her moccasins off and held her feet toward the coals and watched the blood come back into them in a slow, painful wave.

The petition was still dry against her ribs. She pressed her hand flat against it through the wool and felt the paper's slight resistance — stiff, intact, the ink unsmeared.

Across the camp, she could see the door of the largest shelter, where the elders met in winter. The hide flap was down. Inside, she knew, they would be doing what they always did in the early morning: talking, or sitting in silence, or mending something that needed mending. They did not yet know what she carried.

She had perhaps an hour before the day's business began.

She needed every minute of it.

The hide flap lifted and old Paka came out first, moving with the careful deliberateness of a man whose knees had decided, some years ago, to become his enemies. Behind him came Etta, who was Paka's younger sister by a decade and still walked as though the ground owed her something, and last came Siwa, who had been the community's memory-keeper since before Saya was born and who said nothing when he emerged, only looked at her the way he always looked at things — measuring, patient, in no hurry to be done.

Saya had the hide already spread on the frozen ground near the smaller fire. It was a piece of scraped deer hide, stiff with cold, and she had weighted the corners with four stones she'd pulled from the fire ring. The petition lay on it in four pages, each sheet smoothed as flat as she could make it, the ink dark against the pale paper.

Paper. She was still not easy with it. The four sheets had cost her two days of trading — dried fish, a small knife, a length of woven cord — and she had written the draft three times before she was satisfied, her quill cutting into the surface with the scratchy resistance of iron-gall ink that wanted to blot in the cold. The final copy was clean. She had checked every word.

Paka lowered himself onto a folded blanket someone had set out for him and looked at the pages without touching them.

"Read it," he said.

Saya crouched at the edge of the hide. The cold came up through the soles of her moccasins. She pressed her palms flat on the first page to hold it against the wind's edge, which was light but persistent, and began.

"*To the Honourable the General Assembly of Maryland, the Petition of the People of the Patuxent and Potomac Watersheds, humbly showing — that your Petitioners have held and occupied the lands lying between the creek called the Mattawoman and the ridge above the Zekiah Swamp from time immemorial, their ancestors having dwelt upon the same from before all memory of any other inhabitant —*"

She kept her voice even. The words sounded strange in her own ears — the English formal and stiff, the sentences built in a way that was nothing like speech. But that was the point. The assembly did not want to hear her speak. They wanted to read a document that looked like a document.

She read the boundary descriptions next, and here she slowed: the creek's north fork, the two large oaks that marked the ridge trail, the marsh edge, the mound. She had included the mound deliberately, as a fixed landmark. It would appear in any survey's field notes. Better to name it first.

Etta was watching her hands, not her face.

Siwa had his eyes closed. She could not tell if he was listening or sleeping. With Siwa, there was rarely a difference.

She turned to the third page — the appeal to precedent, the citations she had spent a week finding in the records the Jesuit mission kept, the two earlier petitions from other communities that had been partially heard. She read more quickly here, because this was the part she was least certain of. The precedents were thin. She knew it. The assembly would know it.

When she finished, she set the last page down and straightened.

Paka was looking at the papers. He reached out and touched the corner of the first page with one finger, not picking it up, just touching it. The paper shifted slightly on the hide.

"You wrote this in English," he said.

"Yes."

"All of it."

"Yes."

He said nothing. His finger was still on the corner of the page. The fire crackled beside them, throwing a pulse of warmth that faded almost immediately in the cold air.

"The governor will read English," Saya said. "The assembly reads English. If I write in our tongue, they will say they cannot —"

"I know why you wrote it in English," Paka said.

The silence that followed was not hostile. It was something worse: the silence of a man who understood the problem completely and had no solution to offer.

Etta spoke without looking up. "The families. Read those again."

Saya found the passage on the second page. "The families of the Mattawoman band, the Zekiah band, the families of Noka's line and Sewa's line and the families of the creek settlements known to the assembly as —"

"Stop," Etta said.

Saya stopped.

Etta looked at her now. Her face was broad and still, the lines around her mouth deep from years of cold and wind and squinting into fire smoke. "You have not named the Wekomi."

The bead at Saya's throat was warm from her skin. She did not reach for it.

"The Wekomi claim runs against the eastern boundary," Saya said. "If I include them, the assembly will say the petition is internally disputed. They will use it to delay. They will say the claimants cannot agree among themselves and therefore —"

"The Wekomi have been on that land since before my mother's mother," Etta said.

"I know."

"And you did not name them."

"I did not name them."

The fire shifted. A log settled with a soft collapse of coals, and a brief bright light moved across Etta's face and was gone.

Siwa opened his eyes. He looked at Saya for a long moment. Then he said, "The Wekomi elder. Does he know?"

"Not yet."

Siwa nodded, very slowly, and closed his eyes again.

Paka lifted his finger from the page. He placed both hands on his knees and looked at the four sheets of paper lying flat on the deer hide in the cold morning light — the careful English script, the names, the boundary lines, the appeal to precedents that might not hold — and his expression was the expression of a man reading something written in a language he understood perfectly and wished he did not.

"Three days," he said.

"Three days."

He nodded. "Then we have work to do."

He did not say: *and the Wekomi will find out*. He did not say: *this will come back to you*. He had been alive long enough to know that some things did not need to be said aloud to be true.

Saya gathered the pages carefully, tapping their edges straight before folding them back into the oilskin. The paper was cold and slightly stiff, and she had to work slowly to keep from creasing the lines of ink. She tucked the packet back inside her mantle, against her ribs.

The bead pressed against her sternum. Her mother had worn it on a cord of twisted sinew, and when Saya had taken it after the burial she had restrung it on a length of deer hide that she had replaced twice since. It was a small thing — glass, the color of deep water, traded up from somewhere south — but her mother had called it a memory and meant that literally: a thing you kept so that what it stood for would not be lost.

She stood. Her feet were still tender from the cold but they held her.

Across the camp, she could see Okwi near the canoe frame he had been shaping since before she left, his hands moving along the wood with the slow, attentive patience that was the one thing about him she had never been able to argue with. He had not come to hear the reading. She had not asked him to. They would talk tonight, when the elders had gone back to their shelter and the children were asleep, and he would listen without interrupting, which was how she knew he was worried, and then he would say something she did not want to hear, which was how she knew he was right.

She did not know yet what he would say about the Wekomi.

She thought she could guess.

The elders left one by one, not together. Paka went first, pulling his mantle close without ceremony. Etta followed, pausing at the edge of the camp to say something to a woman tending a pot over the second fire — something low, not meant for Saya — and then she was gone into the trees. Siwa was last. He rose without speaking, steadied himself with one hand on a birch trunk, and walked away with the careful, unhurried gait of a man who had outlived every argument he had ever lost.

Saya stood where they had left her, the oilskin packet against her ribs, and watched the trees settle back into stillness.

Okwi had not moved.

He sat on a split log at the far edge of the camp, near the canoe frame that lay across two low trestles of forked branch. He was working a peg from a length of white oak, his knife moving in short, even strokes that sent pale curls of wood falling to the frozen ground. He had been at it since before she had read the petition aloud. He would be at it after she was done being angry, or afraid, or whatever she was.

She went to him.

He did not look up.

She stood for a moment watching his hands. The knife was a good one, iron-bladed, with a handle he had wrapped in deer hide so many times the wrapping had built up to the thickness of a thumb. The oak peg was taking shape — tapered, smooth, the wood pale as bone in the flat winter light.

"You heard," she said.

"I heard."

"And?"

He turned the peg, checked its taper with his eye, took one more stroke. "The Wekomi elder will hear by nightfall," he said. "Someone will tell him."

"I know."

"He will be angry."

"I know that too."

Okwi set the peg on his knee and looked at her for the first time. His face was not unkind. That was the thing about Okwi — he was never unkind when he was about to say something she did not want to hear. Cruelty would have been easier.

"The survey teams will not be stopped by paper," he said. "You know this."

"I know it."

"Then what is the paper for?"

She had thought about this. She had thought about it on the long walk through the dark trees, with the oilskin tucked inside her mantle and her feet going numb in the cold, and she had thought about it while the elders sat in silence around her and touched the corners of her pages and said the names of the people she had not written down.

"The paper is not meant to stop them," she said. "The survey teams will come. They will drive their stakes and mark their trees and write their lines in their books, and none of that will stop because I wrote a petition. I know that."

Okwi waited.

"But after they have come," she said, "after the lines are in the books and the patents are recorded and the assembly has forgotten we were here — the paper will still exist. It will say what was here before the lines. It will say our names. It will say the names of the bands and the families and the creek settlements and the boundary we kept." She stopped. "It is not meant to stop the survey. It is meant to exist after the survey has come and gone, so that what was here before them is not forgotten."

The fire behind her snapped. A thread of smoke moved through the camp, carrying the smell of pine resin and something fat-rich from the cooking pot, and then the wind shifted and it was gone.

Okwi looked at her for a long moment. His hands were still on the peg. The knife rested across his knee, blade toward the ground, the way he always held it when he was thinking.

Then he looked down and took another stroke.

The pale curl fell to the frozen ground and lay there, bright against the dark leaf litter.

He did not agree. He did not disagree. He turned the peg in his hands and ran his thumb along the taper, checking for high spots, and his face was the face of a man who had heard something he could not argue with and was not yet sure whether that meant it was true or only that it was beyond argument.

Saya sat down on the log beside him.

Not close enough to touch. Close enough to be there.

The cold came up through the wood and through her leggings and she felt it settle into the bones of her legs the way cold always did when she stopped moving. The bead at her throat was still warm from her skin. She did not reach for it. She let it rest there, against her sternum, the weight of it so familiar she only noticed it when she thought about it, which was only when she needed to remember that some things could be kept.

Across the camp, two children were chasing each other around the second fire, their breath coming in quick white bursts. One of them fell and got up without crying and kept running. The woman at the pot said something sharp and they veered away from the fire and kept on.

Okwi's knife moved.

Stroke. Stroke. The peg grew smooth.

"The Wekomi elder," he said, without looking up. "When will you tell him?"

"Today," Saya said. "Before someone else does."

Okwi nodded, once, and said nothing more.

She sat with him while the fire burned down and the children tired themselves out and the camp settled into its midday quiet, the sound of the knife against the oak the only sound between them — steady, patient, each stroke taking away a little more of what was not needed, leaving only what would hold.

The fire had burned to coals by the time she came back to it.

The camp was quiet. The children had been put to bed — her daughter among them, tucked into the sleeping space at the far end of the shelter where the bark walls were thickest and the cold came slowest. Saya had checked on her before she sat down. The girl slept on her side with one fist pressed under her chin, her breathing even and slow, her hair spread dark across the rolled deerskin she used for a pillow. She was four. She did not know about surveys or petitions or the names of the bands her mother had written down and the one she had left out.

She did not know yet what was coming.

Saya settled onto the ground close to the coals and pulled her mantle around her shoulders. The oilskin bundle lay in her lap. She had been carrying it all day — through the conversation with Okwi, through the walk to the Wekomi elder's fire, through the long silence that had followed her explanation, through the walk back in the dark. It was heavier than it should have been. Paper was not heavy. But the weight of it had been in her arms all day like a child she was carrying and could not set down.

She untied the cord and unrolled the oilskin on her knees.

The pages were intact. The cold had kept them flat — no damp, no warping, the ink lines still sharp where she had pressed the quill hard and still faint where she had run the nib dry before she noticed. She had written the petition in two hands: her own, which was small and ran slightly uphill, and the borrowed hand of the English trader's clerk who had taught her the formal phrases — *humbly petition*, *your humble servants*, *that they may be secured in the enjoyment of*. She had copied those phrases from a letter the clerk had shown her, a petition from a Virginia planter to the governor, and she had studied the rhythm of them the way she had once studied the rhythm of a canoe in fast water — not to become the river, but to move through it without capsizing.

The fire snapped. A coal shifted and settled, and a small tongue of flame came up for a moment and then died.

She touched the bead at her throat.

She did not mean to. Her hand went there the way it always did when she was still — when the day's motion stopped and the night came down and there was nothing left to do but think. Her fingers found the bead without looking, the way you find a word you have said ten thousand times. It was small. Smaller than she remembered it being when she was a child, though it had not changed — she had changed, her hands had grown, the bead had stayed the same. Blue glass, the color of a winter sky at the moment before it went grey, with a faint spiral worked into the surface that you could only feel, not see. A Lenape woman had given it to her mother. Her mother had worn it for twenty years before she died, and on the morning of her death she had taken Saya's hand and pressed the bead into her palm and closed her fingers around it, and Saya had felt the warmth of her mother's skin on the glass and understood that she was being given something that could not be replaced.

She had strung it herself. A cord of twisted sinew, replaced twice since then, the bead threaded on and knotted close to her throat so it sat against her sternum. She had not taken it off. Not in winter, not in summer, not when she worked in the water, not when she slept. It had been against her skin so long that she sometimes forgot it was there, and then she would touch it and the forgetting would end and she would feel the weight of it, which was not the weight of glass but the weight of her mother's hands.

Her mother's hands had been rough. Rougher than Saya's own, which were rough enough. Her mother had worked all her life and her hands showed it — the knuckles wide, the skin cracked at the joints in winter, the palms calloused in specific places that mapped the tools she had used most. When she placed the bead in Saya's hand, the roughness of her palm had been the last thing Saya felt before her fingers closed. She had thought about that roughness many times since. The work in it. The years.

She looked at her own hands now, holding the petition in the firelight.

The quill marks on her right forefinger had not fully faded. Iron-gall ink bit into skin the same way it bit into paper, and she had been writing for three days. The cold had chapped her knuckles. There was a small cut on her left thumb from the oilskin's cord, not deep, already closing. Her hands were not her mother's hands yet. They would be, if she lived long enough.

She thought about her daughter's hands. Small. Soft still, the way a child's hands were soft, the skin not yet mapped by work. She was teaching the girl to read — English letters, the shapes of them, the sounds they made. The girl could write her own name now in a hand that was large and unsteady and unmistakable. Saya had not told her why it mattered. She was four. You did not explain to a four-year-old about surveys and patents and the assembly's habit of forgetting. You taught her the letters. You let the reason come later.

But the reason was coming faster than Saya had planned.

Three days. The survey teams were three days out, maybe less if the ground stayed frozen. She had the petition. She had the names — most of them. She had the formal phrases that moved through the assembly's language like a canoe through fast water, staying upright, going where she needed to go. She had a route to the assembly through a man who owed her family a debt she had not yet collected, and she had Okwi's silence, which was not agreement but was not refusal either, and she had the bead at her throat, which was not a strategy but was the thing that kept her hands steady when the rest of her was not.

What she did not have was the Wekomi elder's name on the petition.

She had gone to him before dark, as she had told Okwi she would. She had walked across the camp and crouched at his fire and told him what she had done and what she had left out, and she had watched his face while she spoke. He was an old man, older than anyone else in the winter camp, his face so lined it was difficult to read — but she had read it. The stillness in it when she said she had written the petition without him. The way the stillness deepened when she said she had not included the Wekomi name. Not anger. Worse than anger. The look of a man who had expected this and was not surprised and was tired of not being surprised.

He had not refused. He had not agreed. He had said he would think on it, and she had come back across the camp in the dark knowing that *I will think on it* from a man who looked like that meant something closer to *you have made this harder than it needed to be*.

She had made it harder.

She had not meant to. She had been moving fast, writing fast, thinking about the survey teams and the assembly's schedule and the debt that could get the petition into the right hands — and she had written the names she knew and the names she was certain of and she had not stopped to ask about the Wekomi name because stopping to ask would have taken time she did not have, and now she was sitting by a dying fire with the petition in her lap and the knowledge that she had moved too fast settling into her chest like the cold settled into her bones.

Her mother would have asked.

Her mother had been slower than Saya. More careful. She had a way of sitting with a problem that Saya had never fully learned — sitting with it, turning it, waiting for the shape of it to become clear before she moved. Saya had always moved first. It had served her. It had also cost her, and she was beginning to understand that the costs were accumulating in ways she could not yet count.

She rolled the petition back into the oilskin. Her hands were steady. She had learned to keep her hands steady even when the rest of her was not — it was a thing you learned when you worked with ink and paper, because shaking hands made bad lines, and bad lines made documents that could be dismissed. She rolled it tight, the way Okwi had shown her, pressing the air out from the center, and she tied the cord in the double knot that would keep the damp out if the weather turned.

The bundle sat in her lap.

She did not know where it would end up. She knew where she wanted it to go — to the assembly, through the man who owed the debt, in time to be read before the survey teams drove their first stake. She knew what she wanted it to do — to put the names in the record, to say what was here before the lines, to exist after the survey had come and gone. She knew she would carry it to the assembly herself if she had to, on foot, through the frozen woods, with her feet going numb and the ink on her fingers and the bead against her sternum.

What she did not know was whether the Wekomi elder would give her his name in the morning. Whether the man who owed the debt would honor it. Whether the assembly would read the petition or set it aside. Whether three days was enough. Whether the name she had left out had already done damage she could not see yet — damage that would move through the petition's life the way a flaw moved through a canoe's hull, invisible until the water found it.

She did not know these things.

She pressed her hand flat against the oilskin bundle and felt the paper inside, the slight give of it, the firmness of the roll. Under her other hand, through the wool of her mantle, through the linen beneath, the bead rested against her sternum. The warmth of it was her own warmth, given back to her. That was all it was, now. Glass that had taken her heat and held it. But the warmth felt like her mother's hands, and she let it.

Across the camp, the shelter was dark and quiet. Her daughter was sleeping. The girl did not know what was coming, and Saya could not protect her from it by not telling her — she could only make sure that when it came, there was something in the record that said they had been here. That they had names. That they had asked.

She had asked. She would keep asking.

The coals dimmed. The cold pressed in from the edges of the firelight, patient and steady, the way cold always was — not in a hurry, not angry, just there, waiting for the heat to fail. She did not move. She sat with the bundle in her lap and the bead at her throat and the dark trees around the camp standing in their winter silence, and she let herself be still for a moment, the way her mother would have been still.

Then she got up and went to bed.
