# Chapter 4: Stratum One: The Glass Bead [Present — 2024]

---

The string lines were taut in the gray light, each corner staked with rebar driven flush to the ground, and the unit looked smaller than Tayak had imagined it would — one meter by one meter, a square of Maryland earth no bigger than a grave.

She stood at the north edge and did not step in.

Behind her, Wren was still setting up the equipment table: camera bag unzipped, scale bars laid out in a row by length, the field notebook open to a fresh page with the site code already written at the top in Wren's small, precise hand. The coffee they'd bought at the Shell station on Route 6 sat in paper cups on the tailgate of the truck, going cold. Neither of them had touched it since they arrived.

The sky over the tree line was the color of old pewter, the sun not yet up but the darkness giving way, the field emerging from it slowly — the grass still wet, the soil at the unit's edge a dark, close brown. Somewhere in the tree line a wood thrush was working through its phrase, the same four notes again and again, unhurried.

Tayak had been here before. She had stood at the edge of units in Georgia, in Virginia, in the low country of South Carolina where the soil was almost black with centuries of occupation. She knew the particular quality of attention that the first cut required — not excitement, exactly, but a narrowing, everything outside the unit going soft and distant while the square of ground in front of her came into sharp focus.

This was different.

This was her family's land. Not legally — the parcel belonged to the county, had belonged to a farm trust before that, and before that to a man named Harrington whose name appeared in the 1890 tax records — but the mound had been here before Harrington, before the county, before the word *county* meant anything on this ground. The permit in her site folder said *Charles County Site 18CH-441*. The mound didn't know that.

She was aware, in a way she hadn't been at any other site, that she was about to do something that could not be undone.

If she found nothing significant in the first stratum — no features, no artifacts with a date that matched the documentary record, nothing that could anchor the recognition petition to this specific ground — then Voss would pull the university's support before the summer was out. He'd been clear about that, in the careful, regretful way he was clear about things he'd already decided. *The committee needs demonstrable yield, Tayak. You understand my position.* She understood it. She also understood that *demonstrable yield* meant a find significant enough to justify the political exposure of backing a recognition claim that the state was already watching with something close to hostility.

And if she found something significant — if the ground gave back what she believed it held — then Slade would move to halt the excavation on preservation grounds, and the fight would get harder and longer and more expensive, and she would need Marcus and the council to hold the line, and she was not entirely sure they would.

Either way, the moment she broke ground, she was committed. The permit was real. The state was watching. There was no version of this where she walked away with her position unchanged.

She picked up the trowel.

It was a Marshalltown, four-inch pointing blade, the handle worn smooth at the grip where she'd been using it since her first field season. The steel was honed to a thin edge along one side — she kept it sharper than most, a habit her dissertation advisor had called obsessive and she had called accurate. She turned it in her hand once, feeling the weight of it, and then she stepped into the unit.

The soil at the surface was soft from the survey team's test probes and the recent rain. She angled the blade almost flat and drew it toward her, a long, shallow stroke that lifted a thin curl of dark earth and left a clean surface behind it. The smell came up immediately — damp and close, with something underneath it that was older than the topsoil, a mineral sharpness that she associated with undisturbed ground.

She worked in silence. The stroke of the trowel had a rhythm to it, a rasping sound against the compact earth that she felt in her wrist as much as heard. She kept her strokes short and even, moving from the north edge toward the center of the unit, turning the loosened soil into the screen beside her. Behind her she heard Wren set down the camera bag and come closer.

"How's the soil?"

"Dense. More clay than the survey suggested." Tayak scraped another thin layer. "I'm getting some rootlet interference in the top five centimeters but it's clearing."

"You want me to start the level bag?"

"Not yet. Let me get below the organic horizon first."

Wren went quiet. Tayak kept working.

The light was coming up now, the pewter sky going pale gold above the tree line, and the unit was filling with enough light to see the trowel marks clearly — the subtle chatter pattern in the soil, the way the clay lenses caught the light differently from the looser sandy pockets. She was eight centimeters down, then ten, then twelve. The topsoil was giving way to a darker band, organically rich, with the first flecks of charcoal appearing in the matrix. She slowed.

Charcoal meant fire. Fire meant occupation. She kept her strokes shorter, lighter, and did not let herself think about what it meant yet.

She was fifteen centimeters down when she hit something.

Not with the trowel — the trowel passed over it. She felt it with her fingertips when she swept the loosened soil aside: a small, hard point of resistance in the matrix, smooth where the surrounding soil was granular. She stopped. She set the trowel down and used her fingers, brushing the soil away from the spot in short, careful strokes.

A glint.

Not large. The size of a pea, maybe smaller. A point of light in the brown earth, sharp and blue, catching the early sun at an angle that made it look almost electrical.

Tayak sat back on her heels and breathed.

"Wren."

Her voice came out flat and controlled, the voice she used when she needed to think before she felt anything.

Wren was beside her in three steps. She looked at the unit floor, at the small blue point in the soil, and said nothing for a moment.

"Is that—"

"Don't touch it." Tayak was already reaching for the camera bag. "Get the scale bar. The five-centimeter one. And the site label for this unit."

Her hands were steady. She was aware that they were steady, aware that she was making them steady, and that somewhere underneath the steadiness was something she wasn't ready to look at yet.

She braced her elbows on the unit edge and shot the bead in situ — the tiny blue point in its matrix of dark earth, the scale bar beside it, the unit label in the corner of the frame. She stopped down to f/16 and took six frames, adjusting the angle twice to catch the glint without blowing out the highlights. The bead was barely exposed — half of it still in the soil, the visible surface no bigger than a thumbnail — but even at this scale, even in the uneven morning light, she could see the quality of it. Opaque. Cobalt. A soft, almost waxy surface that caught the light without scattering it.

She set the camera down and picked up the trowel again, this time using only the tip, working around the bead in a wide margin, lifting the soil away from it in thin slices until it sat free in a small pedestal of undisturbed earth.

It was cylindrical. Perhaps four millimeters long, three across. The cobalt was deep and even, the surface showing the faint asymmetry of a drawn bead — made by pulling molten glass into a tube and cutting it to length, the ends fire-polished but not perfectly smooth, one end very slightly off-square. Under the direct light she could see a tiny air bubble trapped in the glass near one end, a dark pinpoint in the blue.

She knew what it was. She'd seen enough contact-period assemblages to know. But knowing and finding were two different things, and for a moment she simply looked at it.

"FS number," she said.

Wren already had the notebook open. "FS-001."

Of course it was. The first find. The first physical thing this ground had given back.

Tayak photographed it again — free now, the full form visible — and then she lifted it on the tip of her finger and held it up to the light. It was heavier than it looked. Seventeenth century, probably Dutch or Venetian manufacture, the kind of bead that moved along the Potomac trade routes in the 1680s and 1690s, appearing in Indigenous sites from the tidal rivers all the way to the upland piedmont. The kind of bead that ended up in places where people lived, and traded, and buried their dead.

The kind of bead that, in the right context, with the right association, could anchor a recognition petition to a specific piece of ground.

She lowered her hand. The bead sat on her fingertip, blue and small and very old.

"Bag it," she said.

Wren held out the polyethylene bag, the paper tag already written: *18CH-441, Unit N10/E10, Level 2, FS-001, 1 glass trade bead, opaque cobalt, cylindrical, approx. 4mm.* Tayak tipped the bead in and sealed the bag and held it up one more time, the bead visible through the plastic, blue against the morning sky.

Then she set it in the finds tray and picked up the trowel and went back to work, because there was more ground to read and the day was getting on, and she did not trust herself yet to think about what she was going to say when she called Marcus.

---

The morning wore on. Tayak worked the unit in silence, her strokes mechanical now, controlled, the trowel moving in the short flat arcs she'd taught herself in her first field season twenty years ago — shave, sweep, feel, shave again. The dark band held. More charcoal flecks. A fragment of animal bone, too small to identify in the field, bagged as FS-002. A scatter of grit that might be fired clay or might be nothing.

Wren was in the adjacent unit, three meters north — N10/E13 on the grid — doing the same work. The scrape of her trowel was the only sound besides the cicadas and, twice, a truck on the county road.

At eleven-fifteen Tayak sat up straight, pressed her fists into the small of her back, and looked at the finds tray. The bag with FS-001 sat at the near edge, the bead visible through the plastic — a blue point against the white of the paper tag. She looked at it for three seconds, then looked away.

She picked up her phone and walked to the field road, where the signal was better and the ground was flat enough to stand still without thinking about her footing.

Marcus answered on the second ring. "Tell me something good."

"I need you to listen and not say anything until I'm done."

A pause. "That's either very good or very bad."

"Glass trade bead. Opaque cobalt, cylindrical, drawn construction. Four millimeters. Provenience is unit N10/E10, fifteen centimeters down, in a dark organic band with charcoal association." She heard herself reciting it like field notes and made herself stop. "It's contact period, Marcus. 1680s, maybe earlier. Dutch or Venetian manufacture, the kind that moved up the Potomac trade routes. It's exactly the horizon we needed."

The silence on his end lasted long enough that she counted it. Four seconds. Five.

"You're sure."

"I'm looking at it."

Another pause. When he spoke again his voice had changed — quieter, the lawyer in him standing down. "Does Voss know?"

"Not yet."

"Slade?"

"No."

"Okay." She heard him exhale. "Okay. Don't say anything to anyone on site that isn't already your team. I mean anyone — a hiker, a county worker, anyone. Can you get the bead to the lab today?"

"I want to keep working the unit. There may be more."

"Tayak."

"I know. I'll bring it in tonight." She looked back toward the unit — Wren's bent back visible in the northern square, the afternoon light going flat across the grass. "Marcus — this is the anchor. The petition has been arguing occupation in the abstract. This puts us on the ground. This specific ground."

"I know what it means."

"I don't think you do yet. This bead doesn't just document contact. It documents presence. Sustained, domestic presence — it's not a trade scatter, it's not a colonial dump site. It's in a dark organic band with charcoal. Someone lived here. Someone sat by a fire here."

He was quiet again. Then: "How are you doing?"

She hadn't expected the question. She looked out across the field — the string lines of the units, the white tent over the equipment station, Wren's bent back in the northern square, the line of trees at the field's edge standing dark and still in the midday heat.

"I'm fine," she said. "I'm going back in."

She ended the call and sat for a moment with the phone in her hand. The bead in its bag. The open unit. The dark band of soil that was fifteen centimeters of compressed time, two thousand years of rain and root and frost pressing everything downward, holding it.

She put the phone away and picked up the trowel.

---

Wren called out at half past two.

Not loudly — Wren didn't do things loudly — but with a particular flatness in her voice that Tayak had learned to read. She was across the field and crouching at the unit edge in four seconds.

"Show me."

Wren pointed with the tip of her trowel, not touching. In the floor of her unit, in the same dark band Tayak had been working all morning, a second glint. Larger than the first. The exposed surface caught the afternoon sun at a different angle — not the deep, focused blue of FS-001, but something that fractured the light, throwing it back in a small star pattern, white and blue and red in concentric rings.

Tayak's breath stopped.

She knew that pattern. She'd seen it in assemblages from the Hudson valley, from the Delaware sites, from the contact-period material at Piscataway Park. She'd seen photographs of them in the comparative literature since graduate school.

Chevron bead. A rosetta. Multiple layers of glass ground back at the ends to expose the star — blue, white, red — like a small eye looking up from the earth.

"Don't touch it," she said, though Wren's hands were already in her lap.

"I know."

Tayak straightened up and went back for the camera. Her hands were not steady this time. She noticed this without trying to correct it, and she braced her elbows on the unit wall and breathed until the viewfinder held still.

The bead was larger than the first — perhaps eight millimeters across, the exposed face showing three distinct color layers, the innermost a dark cobalt, then white, then a red-orange that had faded to something closer to brick. The grinding at the end was deliberate, skilled. Someone in Venice or Amsterdam, three hundred and fifty years ago, had made this object with care. Someone had carried it across the Atlantic in the hold of a ship. Someone had traded it up the Potomac. And someone had worn it, or kept it, or lost it here in this dark soil above the mound.

She took the photographs. She worked the pedestal. She assigned FS-003 — FS-002 was the bone fragment — and wrote the tag in Wren's notebook because her own handwriting was unreliable at the moment and she knew it: *18CH-441, Unit N10/E13, Level 2, FS-003, 1 glass trade bead, chevron construction, multilayer cobalt/white/red-orange, approx. 8mm.*

When the bead was free she did not hold it up to the light. She set it directly in the bag, sealed it, and placed it in the finds tray beside FS-001.

Two beads. Same dark band. Same organic matrix. Same charcoal association.

Not a scatter. Not a coincidence.

She sat back on her heels and looked at both bags in the tray — the small cobalt cylinder and the larger chevron, its star pattern just visible through the plastic. Two objects that had moved through the same networks, ended up in the same hands, and been pressed into the same ground. The same fire.

"Wren," she said.

"Yes."

"Write this up tonight. Full field notes, both finds, complete provenience. I want the chain of custody documented from the moment you hit FS-003. Photographs, measurements, everything."

"Already planned."

Tayak looked at her — at Wren's careful face, the field dust on her forearms, the notebook held open against her knee. "You did good work today."

Wren looked briefly uncomfortable, the way she always did when praised directly. "The ground did the work."

"Don't be modest. You kept your strokes clean. You didn't disturb the context."

Wren nodded and wrote something in the notebook, and Tayak suspected it was not field data.

She stood up and looked at the sky. The light was going amber, the shadows of the tree line stretching across the field. Four o'clock, maybe four-thirty. She had paperwork ahead of her, and after that she had to go to La Plata.

She had to go to Nora.

She picked up the finds tray and carried it to the equipment tent, and she thought about what she was going to say. She had been turning it over since eleven this morning, since the first bead, and she still didn't have the words. Not for Nora. You didn't walk into that house with an artifact and a theory. Nora had known this land for eighty-two years. Nora had been to the tribal council meetings when Tayak was in diapers. Nora had made quilts in patterns she said came from her grandmother's grandmother, and she had never once explained where those patterns came from or what they meant, and Tayak had never pushed, because some silences in that house felt load-bearing.

She set the tray down and logged both bags into the site register, signing her initials beside each entry and noting the time.

The bead — FS-001, the small cobalt cylinder — sat in its bag under the fluorescent strip of the tent light, and the color of it was the same as it had been in the ground: deep, even, almost waxy. The kind of blue that didn't fade. The kind that lasted in soil for three hundred and fifty years and came out still looking like itself.

*This is it*, she had said to Wren at eleven this morning, and she had not said anything else, because she hadn't trusted what would come next.

Now, alone in the tent with the register open and the light going outside, she let herself think it through.

The recognition petition had been built on documentary evidence — colonial records, census fragments, the 1697 land dispute Nora had found in the county archive, the genealogical chain Marcus's legal team had assembled over four years. All of it was real. All of it was solid. And all of it could be argued around, because documents could be misread, genealogies could be contested, and the state had a long practice of finding the gap in the chain and pulling.

But a glass trade bead in a dark organic band fifteen centimeters down, in a stratified deposit above a burial mound on the exact parcel the petition claimed — that was not a document. That was the ground itself.

She initialed the last entry, closed the register, and picked up her keys.

Outside, the field was cooling, the cicadas louder now in the dusk. She could smell the river somewhere behind the tree line — tidal mud and brackish water, the specific smell of the Potomac lowlands in summer, the smell she had grown up with and left and come back to without ever quite deciding to. Wren was still in the northern unit, packing her tools. Tayak watched her for a moment — the careful way she wrapped the trowel, the way she checked the string lines before she left the square, the small deliberate gestures of someone who understood that how you left a site mattered as much as how you worked it.

"Lock up when you're done," Tayak said. "I'll see you tomorrow."

"Where are you going?"

"My grandmother's."

Wren looked up. There was something in her expression — not a question exactly, more a held breath, the recognition that something was about to happen that she was not part of and should not ask about. "Okay," she said. "Drive safe."

Tayak walked to the truck. She put the finds tray in the locked box in the bed, checked the hasp twice, and got in. The engine turned over with the familiar diesel shudder, and she sat for a moment with her hands on the wheel before she put it in gear.

She drove south on Route 6 with the window down, the warm evening air carrying cut grass and hot asphalt and, once, a brief thread of woodsmoke from somewhere she couldn't see. The road ran past a new housing development — raw earth and concrete foundations and a sign reading *HERITAGE PINES — FROM THE $400S* — and then past an older farmstead with a stone wall at the property edge, fieldstone stacked without mortar, the kind of wall that had been there since before anyone currently living had been born.

She did not look at the sign. She looked at the wall.

Nora's house was on Billingsley Road, a white wood-frame house with a covered porch and a kitchen garden that had been producing the same varieties of tomatoes and pole beans for as long as Tayak could remember. The porch light was on. It was always on after dark; Nora had kept it on for thirty years, first for Tayak's parents and then, after, for Tayak herself.

She sat in the truck for a moment after she turned off the engine. Through the kitchen window she could see Nora at the table — the shape of her, the white of her hair, the lamp on the table beside her casting a warm circle of light across whatever she was working on. Probably the quilt. The new one, the one she'd been piecing since spring, the pattern she said she'd never tried before and wouldn't explain further.

Tayak got out of the truck and went up the porch steps and knocked twice — she always knocked, even now, even after thirty years of this being the house she came home to — and heard Nora's voice: "It's open."

The kitchen smelled of coffee and something savory that had been on the stove earlier, the ghost of it still warm in the air — onion and the iron-dark smell of the cast skillet Nora had used for forty years. Nora was at the table with the quilt spread across her lap, her reading glasses pushed up on her forehead, her hands still in the way they went still when she was waiting for something.

"Sit down," Nora said, before Tayak had spoken.

"I haven't said anything yet."

"You don't need to. Sit down."

Tayak sat. The kitchen was the same as it had always been — narrow plank flooring worn pale in the paths between the stove and the sink and the table, the double-hung windows dark now against the evening, the radiator under the east window that clanked in winter and was silent in summer. On the table beside the quilt: a coffee mug, a pair of reading glasses on a chain, a small pile of fabric squares in deep blues and reds.

Tayak put her phone on the table and pulled up the photograph. The bead in situ — the small cobalt point in the dark earth, the scale bar, the unit label.

She turned the phone to face Nora.

Nora looked at it for a long time. She did not pick up the phone. She looked at the photograph the way she looked at things she already knew — not discovering, but confirming.

"Where in the unit," she said.

"Fifteen centimeters. Dark organic band with charcoal. And there was a second one, larger — a chevron bead, multilayered, the kind that moved up the Potomac trade routes in the 1680s."

Nora was quiet.

"Nora. Do you know what this means for the petition? With proper provenience, with the stratigraphic context, this anchors the occupation claim. This is physical evidence of presence on this specific—"

"I know what it means." Nora's voice was not sharp, but it stopped her. "I know what a bead means."

Tayak waited.

Nora set the quilt aside. She took her reading glasses off her forehead and folded them and set them on the table, a gesture Tayak recognized — the gesture she made when she was deciding how much to say and in what order.

"That blue," Nora said. "The cobalt one. Small, cylindrical?"

"Four millimeters. Opaque cobalt. Drawn construction."

Nora nodded once, slowly, the way she nodded when something confirmed a thing she had long believed but not said aloud.

"I know whose that was," she said.

The kitchen was very quiet. Outside, the tree frogs had started.

"Sit down properly," Nora said. "Not on the edge of the chair. All the way back." She reached for her coffee mug, and her hands — Tayak noticed now — were not entirely steady. "I need to tell you something about your great-great-grandmother's grandmother. And I need you to listen the way I taught you to listen, which is without interrupting and without deciding what it means until I'm finished."

Tayak sat back in the chair. The phone was still on the table between them, the photograph still on the screen — the bead in its dark earth, blue and small and very old.

"All right," she said.

Nora looked at the photograph one more time. Then she looked at Tayak, and her expression was something Tayak had no name for — grief and relief together, the face of someone who has been carrying a thing for a long time and has finally found the right hands to put it in.

"Her name was Saya," Nora said. "And that bead was the only thing she kept from her mother."

The kitchen went very quiet.

Tayak heard the tree frogs. She heard the refrigerator. She heard her own breath.

"Nora." Her voice came out lower than she intended. "How do you know that?"

Nora set her coffee cup down. She did not answer immediately, and the not-answering was itself an answer of a kind — the deliberate pause of a woman who has thought about this moment, who has rehearsed it, who knows exactly what she is about to do.

"How long have you known what was in that mound?" Tayak said.

Still nothing. Nora's hands were folded around the mug, the bent finger curved inward, and she was looking at the photograph on the phone screen between them. Not at Tayak.

"Nora."

"I'm thinking about how to start."

"Start with how you knew." Tayak heard the edge in her own voice and tried to soften it. "Start with that."

Nora looked up then. Her eyes were dark and entirely steady, and what Tayak saw in them was not guilt and not apology. It was patience — the deep, structural patience of a woman who had been waiting for a question for a very long time and had decided, years ago, that she would not ask to be asked.

"Ida told me," Nora said.

Tayak knew the name. Nora's grandmother. Schoolteacher. Dead sixty years.

"Ida told you there was a bead in that mound."

"Ida told me a story about Saya. About what Saya left behind, and where, and why." Nora's thumb moved once across the side of the mug. "She told me in 1962. I was twenty years old. She sat me down at a table not much different from this one and she said: *I need to tell you something before I lose the words for it.* And she told me."

The refrigerator hummed. Tayak did not move.

"And you never told me," she said.

"I told you when you were ready to hear it."

"I'm thirty-eight years old, Nora."

"I know how old you are." Not sharp — just level. "You weren't ready until you had a trowel in your hand and a permit in your pocket and something real to anchor it to. A story without an anchor is just a story. You needed the bead first."

Tayak looked at the phone on the table. The bead in its photograph — cobalt blue, the star-points catching the morning light. Small and hard and patient, the way old things are patient — not because they wait, but because they simply endure while everything around them moves.

She had spent six years building the case for this excavation. She had written the permit application in three drafts. She had argued with Voss, negotiated with Marcus, driven out to this field in the dark to check the survey stakes before the crew arrived. She had done all of it believing she was the one who had found this.

"You could have told me ten years ago," she said. "I would have—"

"What?" Nora said. "Excavated without a permit? Gone out there with a shovel? Given Slade exactly what he needed to shut you down before you started?" She shook her head, a single small movement. "The story needed the archaeology. The archaeology needed the permit. The permit needed you to be who you are now, not who you were at twenty-eight."

Tayak opened her mouth and closed it.

Outside, a car passed on the road, its headlights sweeping briefly across the kitchen window and gone. The tree frogs did not stop.

"Tell me about Saya," Tayak said finally.

Nora nodded. She looked at the photograph on the phone screen for a moment with an expression Tayak could not name — recognition, maybe, or something older than recognition, something that did not have a word in English.

"Her mother was from the north," Nora said. "Lenape, or near enough. She came south along the Potomac with a trading party when Saya was very small, and she stayed. The bead came with her — a trader's gift, or a diplomat's gift, something given in that register where trade and ceremony are the same thing. Her mother wore it. When her mother died, Saya kept it. That was all she kept."

Tayak was watching her grandmother's face. Nora's voice had shifted — not into performance, not into the cadence of a formal telling, but into something quieter and more precise, the voice of a woman reciting something she has gone over so many times in her own mind that it has worn smooth.

"How did the bead end up in the ground?" Tayak said.

"Saya put it there." Nora's hands tightened slightly around the mug. "She was making a claim. An argument for the land — she had put it in writing, Ida said, a petition of some kind. When the petition failed, she buried it. She put the bead with it." A pause. "Ida understood it as a kind of statement. *We were here. This is proof. Someone will find it.*"

Tayak felt the word *petition* land in her chest. She did not ask the next question immediately. She held it for a moment, feeling its weight.

"And the bead survived," she said. "Three hundred and fifty years."

"Glass keeps," Nora said simply.

Tayak looked at her grandmother's face — the deep lines around her eyes, the white hair, the bent finger curved around the mug. Sixty-two years of carrying this. Sixty-two years of watching the land, attending the council meetings, making the quilts, waiting.

"How did Ida know?" she said. "How did the story get to her?"

"The same way it got to me." Nora looked at her steadily. "Someone sat someone down at a table and said: *I need to tell you something before I lose the words for it.* Generation after generation. All the way back to Saya herself." She paused. "Ida was the one before me. Before Ida, her own grandmother. Before that—" She shook her head. "I don't know all the names in the chain. But the story is the same story. It hasn't changed."

The overhead light buzzed faintly. Tayak had not noticed it before.

She thought about Nora at twenty years old, sitting at a table like this one, listening to an old woman set down something heavy she had been carrying upright for years. She thought about all the women before Nora — the chain of them, each one holding the story in her hands, each one waiting for the right moment, the right person, the right anchor. She thought about Saya burying a bead in the dark and understanding, somehow, that the proof had to outlast the moment. That someone would come.

That someone was her.

The feeling was not dizziness exactly. It was more like the feeling of stepping back from a painting and suddenly seeing the whole composition — the thing you had been standing too close to understand.

She had thought she was excavating a site. She had been excavating her own family's ground. She had thought she was building a case. The case had been building toward her.

"How long?" she said. Her voice was very quiet. "How long have you known what was in that mound?"

Nora looked at her with those steady dark eyes.

"Sixty-two years," she said. "I've been waiting sixty-two years for someone to go and look."

The tree frogs pulsed outside. The phone lay between them on the table, the photograph still on the screen — the bead in its dark earth, blue and small and very old, carrying in its layered glass the whole weight of what Nora had just said.

And underneath that weight, pressing up through it like the mineral sharpness Tayak had smelled when the trowel first broke ground, a new question she could not yet bring herself to ask: Saya had buried a petition. The ground had kept the bead. Whether it had kept anything else — whether there was more down there, deeper, waiting — that was a question for another layer. Another day. Another cut of the trowel.

She was not ready for it yet.

But she would be.
