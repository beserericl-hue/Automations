# Chapter 9: Voss Calls [Present — 2024]

*5,353 words · aligned=True · drift=0+0 · craft_passes=0 · cache_read=39216 tokens · persisted to DB (chapter_number 9).*

---

The trench smelled of wet clay and something older underneath it — a dark, mineral cold that the morning sun hadn't reached yet. Tayak was on her knees in the southeast quadrant, brush in hand, working loose a patch of compacted fill that had resisted yesterday's trowel work, when her phone buzzed against her thigh.

She sat back on her heels and pulled it out. The screen was filmed with dust. She wiped it on her jeans, which were already beyond help, and read the name.

*Voss, E.*

Three seconds. She counted them without meaning to.

Marcus had called him. She'd known he would — had known it the moment she saw Marcus's face in the council room shift from pride into that particular look he got when he was already calculating two moves ahead. He'd called Voss last night, or this morning, or sometime in the window between the meeting and now. What she didn't know was what he'd said. Whether he'd led with the bead, or the stratigraphy, or whether he'd used the word *significant* — because *significant* was the word that made Elliot Voss pick up the phone.

She hoped he was calling to say *well done, keep digging, send me the context sheets when you're ready.*

She was afraid he was calling to say *I've been thinking.*

She answered.

"Tayak." His voice was warm, the way it got when he was pleased with something and wanted her to know it before she could be displeased with him. "I hear you've had quite a week."

"It's Tuesday, Elliot."

"Marcus tells me the stratigraphy is exceptional. Intact layers?"

She looked down at the trench wall in front of her — four visible bands of soil, each a slightly different color, the lowest one almost black. A record no one had touched in centuries. "It's looking that way."

"And a glass trade bead in the first stratum. That's a solid colonial-contact marker. That's real."

"Yes."

"You must be excited."

She was. She'd been awake until two in the morning with it, lying in her truck's back seat with her field notebook on her chest, too wound up to drive home. But she didn't say that. "I'm focused."

"Of course." A pause, the kind he used when he was arranging what came next. She heard, faintly, the sound of his office — the old building's radiator knock, the creak of his chair. "I've been thinking about the reporting structure. Given what Marcus described."

There it was.

"The reporting structure is in the permit, Elliot."

"Naturally. But a find of this potential — and I'm not getting ahead of ourselves, I know you hate that — a find of this potential warrants thinking about methodological rigor. Peer eyes on the process from the start. It's protective, really. For you."

Tayak pressed her free hand flat against the trench wall. The clay was cold and faintly damp, and it held the shape of her palm when she pulled away. "What are you suggesting?"

"A second team. Not to direct anything — purely consultative. Ceramics specialist, perhaps a physical anthropologist on standby given the burial context. People who can verify the record as it's built. It would strengthen the eventual report considerably."

She stared at the handprint she'd left in the clay. "I have Wren."

"Wren is excellent. She's also your student, Tayak. Reviewers will note that."

"Reviewers will note that I ran a clean excavation with full documentation and a complete contextual record."

"They will. And they'll also ask who else was watching." His voice stayed warm, which was worse than if it had hardened. "I'm thinking of the publication. I'd like to be involved — co-author, obviously, given the departmental resources behind this project. We can talk about the order."

There it was again. The real thing, dressed in the first thing.

Tayak stood up. Her knees ached from the clay. She climbed out of the trench in two steps and walked away from the site, far enough that Wren, twenty meters off at the finds table, wouldn't hear.

"The departmental resources behind this project," she said, "are one graduate student, a shared GPS unit that took me three weeks to borrow, and a permit I wrote myself."

"And the university's institutional affiliation, which is on every document you've filed." He said it gently, which was how he always said the things that were meant to land. "I'm not trying to complicate your work, Tayak. I'm trying to protect it. A find like this, on a site with tribal recognition implications — it will be scrutinized. Hard. You want the strongest possible team behind the record."

"I want the record to be mine."

A short silence. "Of course it will be yours."

She looked back at the trench. Wren was bent over the finds table, making notes in her field register, her handwriting so small it was nearly invisible from this distance. The morning light caught the dust on the tarp above her and turned it gold.

"I'll think about it," Tayak said.

"Good. That's all I'm asking."

She ended the call and stood in the field for a moment, the phone warm in her hand, the sun already hard on the back of her neck. A red-winged blackbird called once from the tree line and went quiet.

*I'll think about it.* She hadn't meant it. She'd said it because she needed to get off the phone before she said something she couldn't take back, and because she needed time to understand exactly what had just happened and what it was going to cost her to stop it.

She walked back toward the trench.

The first thing she did was pick up her field notebook from the folding table and open it to a clean page. She wrote the date and time at the top. Then she wrote: *Voss call. Co-authorship. Second team. "Methodological oversight." No authorization yet — his words.*

She underlined *no authorization yet* twice.

Wren looked up from the finds register. "Everything okay?"

"Fine." Tayak capped her pen. "I need you to do something for me."

"Sure."

"I need you to document everything twice from here on. Every context sheet gets a duplicate. Every photo goes to the university server and to my personal drive. Every unit measurement gets logged in your notebook and mine."

Wren set down her pencil. She had the kind of stillness that came from being very good at reading rooms. "How much trouble are we in?"

"None yet." Tayak pulled on her work gloves — the right one had a split along the thumb seam she kept meaning to replace. "I'd like to keep it that way."

"Who called?"

"Voss."

Wren said nothing. She picked up her pencil and opened a fresh page in her register and began copying the morning's unit measurements from memory, her handwriting precise and extremely small, each numeral the same height as the one before it. She didn't ask anything else.

That was why Tayak had asked for her specifically, three years ago, when Wren was still a first-year and half the faculty thought she was too quiet to manage fieldwork. Wren understood that some questions were better answered by getting back to work.

Tayak climbed down into the trench.

The walls held the morning cool for another hour, maybe two, before the sun cleared the tree line and turned the clay into something that radiated. She had that time. She used it. She took her trowel and worked the lowest visible stratum — the dark one, almost black, with the faint organic smell that meant undisturbed deposit — and she thought about what Voss had said and what it meant and what she was going to do about it.

*Co-author, obviously, given the departmental resources.*

She scraped a thin layer of soil from the trench face and examined it. Nothing. She scraped again.

The thing about Voss was that he wasn't wrong, exactly. A find of this kind — stratified, intact, on land with active tribal recognition implications — would be scrutinized. She knew that. She had known it before she filed the permit, before she drove out to the field the first morning, before she put the trowel in the ground. She had planned for it. That was why the context sheets were meticulous, why she'd made Wren photograph every unit from three angles before disturbing it, why she'd kept a running GPS log of every find's exact position.

She had planned for scrutiny. She had not planned for Voss to get there first.

*A second team. Not to direct anything — purely consultative.*

She knew what consultative meant. It meant their names on the report. It meant their interpretive framework in the discussion section. It meant that when the report was cited in a recognition hearing — and it would be, if the stratigraphy held — the citation would include Voss and two of his senior colleagues, and the story the report told would be a story they had all agreed on together, and the story she had been building in the ground, layer by layer, with her own hands, would become a chapter in someone else's book.

She scraped the trench face again and stopped.

A color change. Faint — a thin line of reddish-brown cutting across the dark deposit at a slight angle, no more than four centimeters wide. She leaned closer. Not a root trace. The edges were too clean, the color too consistent.

She set down the trowel and picked up a brush.

Behind her, she heard Wren's pencil moving steadily across the register page. The blackbird called again from the tree line, closer this time. Somewhere in the field, a grasshopper started up and stopped.

Tayak brushed the soil away from the line, millimeter by millimeter, and thought: *Whatever this is, it's mine. I found it. I'm recording it. No one else was here.*

She photographed it with her phone — three angles, scale bar in each frame — and entered it in her notebook: *possible intrusion feature, reddish-brown, angular edge, depth 38 cm, Unit 4 North.*

Then she climbed out of the trench and walked to the finds table and stood next to Wren and said, very quietly, "When you send the photo backup tonight, use the personal drive first. Then the university server."

Wren looked up. "Personal drive first."

"Yes."

Wren wrote it in the margin of her register, circled it, and went back to her measurements.

Tayak looked out across the field. The grass beyond the site boundary was high and going yellow at the tips, and the heat was beginning to build at the edges of the shade shelter, and somewhere under two thousand years of soil the ground was keeping everything it had ever been given.

She thought about what Marcus had told Voss. She thought about what Voss was going to do next, now that she'd said *I'll think about it* and he'd taken that as a yes.

She thought about the reddish-brown line in the trench wall.

She picked up her phone and called Marcus.

The phone rang four times. Five. She was about to hang up when he answered.

"Tayak."

"You called Voss."

A pause. Not long — Marcus wasn't a man who needed long pauses to gather himself. But long enough.

"I let him know the find was significant. That's my job."

"Your job." She kept her voice level. "You called my department chair before you called me."

"I called you first. You didn't pick up."

That was probably true. She'd had her phone in her pocket all morning and the trench had her full attention and she couldn't remember every time it had buzzed. "What did you tell him?"

"That the stratigraphy looked intact. That the glass bead was documented. That it had recognition implications." Another pause. "That the council was watching the timeline."

She pressed the heel of her hand against her forehead. The shade shelter was doing nothing against the heat now — the sun had cleared the tree line while she was in the trench and the air under the canopy had gone thick and still. Thirty meters away, the trench waited. The reddish-brown line waited.

"He wants to co-author the report," she said.

"I know."

"You know."

"He called me back," Marcus said. "After he talked to you."

She lowered her hand. "He called you back."

"Tayak—"

"He called you back and told you he'd already spoken to me, and you're telling me this now."

"I'm telling you because I think you should know." His voice had the careful quality it got when he was coaching — patient, deliberate, not quite an apology. "And because I think you need to decide how to play this before he decides for you."

She looked at Wren, who was bent over the register with her pencil and her precise small handwriting and her complete apparent deafness to everything happening six feet away. "I already told him I'd review the site protocols. That I'd be in touch."

"He told me that too." A beat. "He said it was a promising start."

She said nothing.

"I know how that sounds," Marcus said.

"Do you."

"He has resources you don't. He has two senior colleagues who've testified at recognition hearings before. That's not nothing, Tayak."

"It's not nothing," she agreed. "It's also not mine." She watched a horsefly land on the edge of the finds table and walk the length of a sample bag in a slow, proprietary way, as if it owned the site. "If his name goes on this report, the interpretation goes with it. You know that. You went to Howard. You know exactly what that means."

"I know what it means," Marcus said, and his voice had shifted — quieter, something underneath it she couldn't quite read. "I also know what it means if the state pulls your permit in thirty days and we have nothing to show a hearing."

She let that sit.

"I'm not saying give him the report," Marcus said. "I'm saying don't make him an enemy before you know what you have."

"I know what I have."

"You have one glass bead and a color change in the trench wall."

"I have two thousand years of intact deposit."

"You have a hypothesis." His voice was still careful, still patient, but there was something firmer underneath it now — the part of Marcus that had sat across tables from state officials for twenty years and learned when to press and when to wait. "A good one. Maybe a great one. But it's still a hypothesis until you have more."

She thought about the reddish-brown line. The clean edges. The depth.

"Marcus." She kept her voice very quiet. "Did you tell Voss about the feature in Unit Four?"

A pause. Longer this time.

"What feature?"

She exhaled. "Never mind."

"Tayak—"

"I'll call you when I have more." She hung up.

She stood at the finds table and looked at her phone for a moment — the cracked corner of the screen, the smear of clay on the case from where she'd set it down in the trench — and then she put it in her pocket and pulled on her work gloves, the split along the right thumb seam catching on her knuckle the way it always did.

Wren said, without looking up, "Personal drive first."

"Yes."

"I'll do it tonight. And I'll run a second photo log — separate file, separate naming convention. So if anyone asks for the university records later, the university records are complete, and ours are also complete."

Tayak looked at her. Wren was still writing, her handwriting still precise and even, each numeral the same height as the one before it.

"That's good thinking," Tayak said.

"I've been thinking it since you came out of the trench." Wren turned the page. "What's in Unit Four?"

"I don't know yet."

"But you think it's something."

"I think the ground doesn't make clean edges by accident."

Wren wrote something in the margin of the register and circled it. Then she set down her pencil and looked up for the first time, and her face had the expression it got when she was about to say something she'd been working up to. "If Voss brings in a second team," she said, "they'll want access to the unit logs."

"Yes."

"All of them. Including whatever we find in Unit Four before we tell anyone about it."

"Yes."

"So we need to find it first." She picked up her pencil again. "And record it first. And understand it first."

"Yes," Tayak said. "We do."

She walked back to the trench. The walls were warm now — the morning cool was gone, the clay radiating the sun's heat back at her the moment she dropped below ground level. She picked up the brush and crouched in front of the reddish-brown line and began to work.

The line ran at a slight angle, cutting through the dark deposit, and as she cleared the soil from around it she could see it was not a single line but two — parallel, maybe eight centimeters apart, the space between them a slightly different color from either the surrounding deposit or the lines themselves. She photographed it again. Entered it in her notebook. *Two parallel edges, reddish-brown, spacing 8 cm, possible structural feature — post mold? Prepared surface? Depth 38–41 cm, Unit 4 North. Requires further exposure.*

She kept working. The brush moved in small strokes, clearing millimeters at a time, and the field was quiet except for the insects and the distant sound of a truck on the county road and the faint scrape of Wren's pencil above her.

Whatever this was, it was old. Older than the glass bead. Older than the stratum she'd been working. She could feel it in the way the deposit had settled around it — the compaction, the color, the way the soil had organized itself over centuries around something that had once been solid and was now only a trace.

She thought about Voss's voice when they'd hung up. The warmth that had come into it at the end — careful, recalibrated, the voice he used when he thought he'd made progress. *We'll talk soon, Tayak. I'm very excited about what you're building out there.*

What she was building. As if she were a student still, building something under his supervision, and he was the one who would decide when it was done.

She set the brush aside and took the trowel and worked the edge of the feature with the flat of the blade, reading the soil the way she'd been taught — not by Voss, not by any of the Harvard-trained men who'd run the programs she'd moved through, but by a woman named Dr. Cecile Marant at Howard who'd told her once: *The ground will tell you everything if you listen with your hands. The trouble is, so will everyone else. And only one of them is actually there.*

She listened with her hands.

The feature extended further than she'd thought — past the edge of the unit, into the unexcavated baulk. She would need to extend the unit to follow it. That meant a new context sheet, a new photo log, a new GPS point. It meant another day, maybe two, before she understood what she was looking at.

It meant Voss would call again before she knew.

She climbed out of the trench and walked to the finds table and wrote *Unit 4 extension — priority* at the top of the next blank page in her notebook, and underlined it twice, and then she wrote below it: *Do not discuss with Voss until fully exposed and documented.*

Wren read it upside down without comment and opened a fresh context sheet and wrote the unit number at the top in her precise small hand.

The horsefly was gone. The sample bags sat in their labeled row. The heat pressed down on the shade shelter and the field beyond it shimmered at the edges where the yellow grass met the tree line.

Tayak's phone buzzed in her pocket. She looked at the screen: *Voss — Elliot.* She let it ring. It stopped. A moment later, a text: *Great talking earlier. Let's find time this week — I have some thoughts on the sampling strategy I think you'll find useful.*

She put the phone face-down on the finds table and picked up her trowel.

*Sampling strategy.* He was already inside the methodology. Already treating the conversation they hadn't finished as a conversation they had. She had said *not yet* and he had heard *yes, soon* and now he was sending texts about sampling strategy as if he'd already written his name on the permit.

She thought about the reddish-brown lines in the trench wall. The clean edges. The eight centimeters of space between them, holding their shape across however many centuries had passed since someone pressed something into the ground and walked away.

She needed to know what it was before Voss did.

She needed to know what it was before anyone did.

She climbed back into the trench.

She called Marcus at four-fifteen, when the light had shifted and the shadows in the trench had grown long enough to make fine work impossible. She was sitting on the tailgate of the truck, boots still caked with clay, her field notebook open on her knee to the page she hadn't written anything on yet.

He answered on the first ring.

She'd known he would. The speed of it told her everything — he'd been holding the phone, or close to it, waiting for the number to come up.

"Tayak."

"When did you call him."

A pause. Not the pause of a man who didn't know what she was asking. The pause of a man deciding how much of the truth to lead with.

"Yesterday evening," Marcus said. "After the meeting."

The truck's metal was hot through her jeans. She looked out at the field — the shade shelter, the labeled crates, the unit flags standing in their small row, Unit 4's orange marker catching the late sun.

"What did you tell him."

"That you'd found something significant. That the site warranted institutional attention before the state could establish a competing claim." He paused again. "I told him the Piscataway Nation had a vested interest in how this was reported and that the university needed to be aligned with us, not neutral."

"And what did he say."

"He said he agreed completely. He said he'd been meaning to reach out to you."

"He called me this morning," Tayak said. "He wants to co-author the final report. He wants to bring in a second team for methodological rigor."

Silence.

Not the silence of a man calculating. The silence of a man who had just understood what he'd done.

"I didn't know it would go that way," Marcus said.

"I know."

"Tayak—"

"I know you didn't." She kept her voice level. "That's not the point."

"The point is I was trying to protect the site. Slade has contacts at the state office. If he moves before we have institutional weight behind us—"

"Voss isn't weight behind us." She closed the notebook. "Voss is weight. That's different. He doesn't stand behind people. He stands in front of them and calls it support."

Another silence. Longer this time.

"He has the monograph," Marcus said. "The Chesapeake trade network work. His name on a report—"

"His name on a report means his name on the report." She said it slowly, the way she'd say it to a first-year student who'd handed her something with a structural problem buried in the methodology. "It means his interpretation of what we find. It means his framing of what this site is and who it belongs to. It means that when the commission reads the final document, the first credential they see is Harvard, 1989, and everything I've built here becomes a footnote in his work."

She heard Marcus breathe out — a long, controlled breath, the kind he used when he was absorbing something he didn't want to absorb.

"What do you want me to do," he said.

"Nothing." She looked at the unit flags again. The orange marker. "Nothing, Marcus. That's what I want you to do. I want you to do nothing until I tell you what needs doing. I want you to make no calls, send no emails, have no conversations about this site with anyone at the university or the state office or anyone else without talking to me first."

"That's going to be difficult when—"

"I know it's going to be difficult." She stood up, moved away from the truck, her boots pressing into the dry grass. "I know you have relationships to maintain and calls to return and I know Slade makes you nervous and I know you think moving first is the same thing as moving smart. But this is my site. My permit. My name on the field director line." She stopped. "Next time, I make the calls."

The silence this time was different. Softer. The silence of a man who was not going to argue.

"All right," Marcus said.

"I mean it."

"I know you do."

She stood in the grass with the phone at her ear and the late sun at a low angle across the field and the smell of warm clay rising from her own clothes. She believed him — that he hadn't known Voss would move that fast, that the call had been meant as a shield and had become a door left open. She believed it completely and it didn't change anything.

"I'll talk to you when I have something to show you," she said. "Not before."

She ended the call and stood there a moment, watching the shadow of the shade shelter stretch east across the grass.

Wren was at the finds table, still writing, her pencil moving in the small precise strokes she used for final context entries. She didn't look up when Tayak walked back.

"Unit Four extension," Tayak said. "First thing tomorrow. I want the full north wall exposed before anyone else comes to this site."

Wren turned the page. "I'll have a fresh context sheet ready."

"And Wren." She waited until the younger woman looked at her. "If anyone calls the department asking about our progress — Voss, anyone from his office, anyone from the state — you haven't seen anything that surprised you."

Wren held her gaze for a moment. Then she looked back down at the context sheet and wrote the unit number at the top in her careful hand.

"I haven't seen anything that surprised me," she said.

She waited until Wren had looked back down at the sheet before she pulled out her phone again.

The call connected on the second ring.

"Wren."

A pause — the kind that meant Wren was already looking at her from twenty feet away, watching Tayak stand at the edge of the field with the phone at her ear. Tayak didn't turn around.

"I can see you," Wren said.

"I know. I need to tell you something I don't want to say loud enough for the field to hear."

Another pause. The sound of a pencil being set down.

"From here on," Tayak said, "every artifact gets photographed twice. Independent angles, separate memory cards. Every context sheet gets a timestamp and a backup copy uploaded off-site before end of day. Not end of week. End of day."

Silence.

"That's — yes, I can do that. That's not a small amount of extra work."

"I know what it is."

"Tayak." Wren's voice had shifted — the careful, slightly formal register she used when she was asking something she wasn't sure she had standing to ask. "Why."

Tayak looked out at the trench. The afternoon had turned the exposed north wall a deep ochre, the soil layers reading clear as lines in a ledger — dark loam, then pale clay, then the darker band where the stratum changed. Two thousand years of ground, sitting open to the sky because she had cut it open. Because she had the permit and the training and the right.

"Because Voss wants to bring in a second team," she said. "For methodological rigor."

She heard Wren understand it — not the words, but the shape of what the words meant. The way a second team's records would become the authoritative record. The way parallel documentation, arriving later, would absorb hers. The way her field notes, her context sheets, her photographs would become source material for someone else's interpretation, cited in a footnote, credited in an acknowledgments section, thanked for their cooperation.

"If they arrive," Tayak said, "I want a record that already exists. Timestamped. Backed up somewhere they don't control. A record that cannot be disputed because it was made before anyone thought to dispute it."

The line was quiet. Not the silence of hesitation. The silence of someone making a decision that had consequences they were already calculating.

"I'll start tonight," Wren said.

"The glass bead photographs first. Then the context sheets from units one through four. Then the GPS logs."

"The GPS logs are already backed up to my personal drive."

"Back them up again. Somewhere else."

"All right."

Tayak turned the phone over in her hand after she ended the call. The screen was warm from the sun, and there was a thin line of dried clay along the edge of the case where she'd set it down in the trench that morning without thinking. She rubbed it with her thumb and it didn't come off.

Wren was already moving. Tayak could see her from the truck — pulling the external drive from the finds bag, connecting it to the tablet, her movements quick and precise in the way they got when she had a task with clear edges. The late sun caught the silver of the drive and threw a brief hard light across the table.

Tayak walked back to the trench.

She stood at the edge and looked down into it. Unit 4's north wall, the one they'd extend tomorrow. The soil was dry on the surface and darker below — she could see the moisture gradient even from here, the way the ground held its own history in layers, each one a century compressed, a life compressed, a decision made by someone who had stood on this ground and done something that mattered and then died and become part of the record.

Her people's ground.

She had a permit for it. A university-issued, state-approved, time-limited permit that authorized her to excavate and document and interpret and report. A permit that Voss had signed off on, that Slade's office had reviewed, that Marcus had pushed through the tribal council with more effort than he'd told her and less authority than he'd implied. A permit that said, in the language of institutions, that she had the right to be here.

She didn't need the permit to know she had the right to be here.

But the permit was what the institutions understood. And now the institutions were interested, which meant the permit was the only thing standing between her and the very people who had issued it.

She crouched at the edge of the trench and picked up a small clod of soil that had been displaced from the north wall. It was dense, slightly damp on the inside when she broke it apart with her thumb — the kind of soil that held its shape, that compressed under weight and kept the impression. Good soil for keeping things. Good soil for burying what you needed to last.

Someone had known that. Someone had chosen this ground.

She set the clod down on the spoil heap and stood up.

Behind her, she heard the quiet mechanical click of the tablet camera — Wren, already working, already building the record that would have to be enough.
