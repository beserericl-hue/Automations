# Chapter 3: The Field [Present — 2024]

*7,317 words · aligned=True · drift=0+0 · craft_passes=0 · cache_read=41120 tokens · persisted to DB (chapter_number 3).*

---

The access road was a pair of ruts through knee-high fescue, and Tayak's Subaru found every rock in both of them.

She'd turned off Route 6 twenty minutes ago, following the GPS until it gave up and left her with a blue dot and a lot of green nothing. The county road had narrowed to a farm lane, the farm lane had narrowed to this, and now the muffler was scraping something and she didn't care. She dropped to first gear and kept going.

*You know what's in that ground, Tayak. You know and you're not ready.*

Nora's voice in the kitchen. The smell of chicory coffee and the sound of the radio playing low — a gospel station Nora kept on all morning the way other people kept on the heat. Tayak had kissed her grandmother's cheek and said she'd call that evening, and Nora had held her wrist for a moment, just a moment, with a grip that still surprised her.

The ruts ended at a rusted gate, padlocked. She parked on the verge, cut the engine, and sat.

The field beyond the gate was maybe twelve acres of rough pasture, the grass still holding the silver of morning dew. A line of tulip poplars ran along the eastern edge, their new leaves the yellow-green of something just born. The sky was low and white, the kind of sky that couldn't decide.

She got out.

The air smelled of wet clay and something underneath it — not rot, not quite. More like the inside of a root cellar: old and shut and waiting. She'd smelled it on other sites, always in the first minute, before her nose adjusted. She breathed it in deliberately.

The permit was in her jacket pocket. The field log was in her bag. The GPR printout was in a cardboard tube in the backseat, already soft at the edges from her handling it too many times in the past two weeks. She'd looked at it until she could close her eyes and see the subsurface anomaly: a roughly oval mass, twelve meters on the long axis, rising perhaps a meter and a half above the surrounding grade. Modest. Consistent with a small late-woodland period earthwork. Consistent with a dozen other sites in the county.

She climbed the gate.

On the other side, the grass was longer, undisturbed by any recent grazing, and it soaked her boots to the ankle in the first ten steps. She didn't stop. She walked toward the center of the field, following the slight upward cant of the ground, and she watched the horizon line shift as she moved — the treeline dropping behind her, the sky opening.

She saw it before she expected to.

Not dramatic. That was the first thing. No crown, no clear summit. Just a long, low swelling in the pasture, the way ground swells when something is buried deep and the earth has had centuries to settle over it. The grass on the rise was shorter than the surrounding field — she noted that — and a different shade, the blades thinner, the color more grey-green than the lush fescue around it. Shallower soil over harder fill. She'd seen that before too.

But the size was wrong.

She stopped walking and looked at it straight on, then turned and looked at the treeline, then back. She counted her paces from the gate: sixty-two. She turned to face the mound again and tried to hold the GPR image in her mind alongside what her eyes were giving her now.

The printout had shown twelve meters on the long axis.

What she was looking at was not twelve meters.

She walked the perimeter. She didn't rush it. She kept her stride even, counting, watching the slope breaks where the mound's edge met the surrounding grade. The slope was wrong too — not the gentle convex curve of a natural rise, but something flatter, something that had been worked. The eastern edge especially: a slight shelf in the profile, a few centimeters of almost-level ground before the slope resumed, the kind of feature that happened when a rounded form was deliberately pressed down, the soil compacted and regraded by someone who understood what they were hiding.

She stopped at the eastern shelf and crouched. She pressed her palm flat against the ground. The grass here was dense and fine, the root mat tight, and beneath it the soil gave less than it should — packed harder than the surrounding field, harder than frost or simple age would account for.

She stayed crouched for a long moment.

The GPR had been run in dry conditions, late October, the operator from a consulting firm out of Annapolis who'd done good work on three other sites she knew. The attenuation could be moisture, could be the clay content of the subsoil, could be a dozen things. She had told herself that. She had told Wren that. She had written it in the preliminary notes.

But GPR didn't miss a mound by this much. Not without help.

She stood and walked the rest of the perimeter. The long axis, paced out: closer to eighteen meters. The short axis: fourteen. The rise above the surrounding grade, estimated by eye and confirmed by the way her calves felt on the ascent: not a meter and a half. Closer to two and a half, before whatever flattening had been done. She was guessing at the original profile from the shelf and the compaction, but she'd been doing this for fifteen years and her guesses had a way of holding up.

Someone had been here. Not a farmer with a plow, not road graders, not erosion. Someone who knew that a mound with a lower profile was a mound that didn't make it onto survey lists, didn't attract attention, didn't generate the kind of report that ended up on a state archaeologist's desk.

She pulled out her field log — a hardback Rite in the Rain notebook, its cover already dirty from her jacket pocket — and wrote the date, the time, the weather. Then she wrote: *Profile significantly larger than GPR data indicated. Long axis approx. 18m, short axis approx. 14m, estimated rise 2.2–2.5m. Eastern edge shows possible shelf feature consistent with deliberate compaction. Mound fill harder than surrounding matrix. Note: altered profile cannot be attributed to natural processes alone.*

She stopped. Read it back. Crossed out *cannot be attributed to natural processes alone* and wrote: *requires further investigation.*

It was the same thing. She knew it was the same thing. But she was not ready to write the first version in a document that would be filed with the university and the state.

Not yet. Not until she knew who had done it and how long ago.

She looked up from the notebook. The field was quiet. No sound except the flagging tape she hadn't hung yet, because she was alone, because Wren and the undergraduates weren't due for another forty minutes, because she had wanted this first sight for herself.

*You know what's in that ground.*

The low white sky sat on the treeline. A red-winged blackbird called once from the poplars and went silent.

She turned to face the mound again and felt, for the first time since she'd read the permit approval, something that was not hope and not fear but the particular alertness that came when a site started talking before you'd asked it anything.

Someone had tried to make this place invisible.

They hadn't tried hard enough.

The sound came before the vehicles: gravel popping under tires on the access road, then the low growl of Wren's Subaru, then the higher pitch of a second engine behind it. Tayak was still standing at the mound's eastern edge when the cars pulled through the gate.

She closed her notebook and put it in her jacket pocket — the personal one, not the field bag — and walked back across the pasture.

Wren got out first. She was wearing her green canvas field jacket, the one with the torn left pocket she'd been meaning to sew for two years, and she had her hair pulled back with a pencil already threaded through the knot. She looked at the mound before she looked at Tayak, which was the right instinct, and her face did the thing it did when she was filing information away: a slight stillness, eyes moving in a pattern, lips pressed together.

"Bigger than the GPR," she said.

"Yes."

"How much bigger?"

"We'll run the tape. Get your total station set up first."

Wren nodded and went to the back of her car.

The second vehicle was a rusted white pickup that belonged to one of the undergraduates — Marcus Birch, who went by Birch to distinguish himself from the other Marcus in the department, and who had arrived wearing a flannel shirt that was not going to be warm enough. Behind him, from the passenger side, came Delia Okafor, who was wearing a proper field jacket and carrying two water bottles, which meant she had done this before or read ahead, and Tayak made a note to give her the more precise grid work.

"Dr. Moyaone." Birch raised a hand. He had the slightly startled look of someone who had not entirely believed, until this moment, that the site was real.

"Birch. Delia." Tayak looked them over once. "Boots are good. Birch, you'll want a second layer. There's a fleece in the supply crate if you need it."

"I'm fine."

"You'll say that for about an hour."

Delia hid a smile. Tayak turned back to the vehicles.

The supply crate was in her own truck bed: a grey plastic tote, latched, containing the measuring tapes, pin flags, sample bags, the total station case, a spare GPS unit, notebooks, pencils, gloves, and the waterproof survey forms she'd printed at the department office at six this morning while the building was still empty. She'd also packed a thermos of coffee and a second thermos of hot water, because she had learned, on her first field season, that people made better observations when their hands were warm.

"Grid layout," she said, and they gathered around the truck's tailgate. She unfolded the base map — a printout of the site plan she'd drafted from the GPR data and the county parcel records, showing the mound's footprint as the radar had given it, with the property boundary and the access road marked in. She put her finger on the mound's center. "We're setting up a two-meter grid across the full surface and a ten-meter buffer zone around the perimeter. No excavation today. Surface survey only. You're flagging visible anomalies, photographing, bagging any surface finds, and logging GPS positions. Everything gets a bag number and a field form. Nothing goes in your pocket. Nothing gets moved without a photograph first."

Birch looked at the map. "The mound's bigger than this shows."

"I know."

He waited for more. She didn't give it.

"Wren's setting up the total station at the northwest corner — that's your datum point for today. Delia, you're on the east transect. Birch, west. I'll walk the perimeter with you both before you start your lines."

She handed out the pin flags — the orange plastic kind, on wire stakes, the kind that looked like something a utility company would use and that she had bought at a hardware store in La Plata two days ago. She handed out the field forms and the pencils. She handed Delia the spare GPS unit and showed her how to log a waypoint.

"Questions."

Birch had one. "What are we looking for?"

"Anything that doesn't belong. Color change in the soil. Surface finds — worked stone, ceramic, bone, shell, anything that's not grass and dirt. Changes in vegetation. Depressions, humps, anything that looks like a previous cut or backfill. You're not interpreting. You're describing. Write down what you see, not what you think it means."

"And if we think it means something?"

"Write that in the margin. Separately. In brackets."

She left them to it and walked toward Wren.

Wren had the total station case open on the ground and was lifting the instrument out with both hands, the way you handled something that cost more than a used car. She set it on the tripod, leveled it with the thumb screws, checked the bubble, adjusted, checked again. Her movements were unhurried and exact. Tayak had worked with three graduate students before Wren and had spent more time on two of those field seasons correcting setup errors than doing actual survey work. She watched Wren for a moment without saying anything.

"Northwest corner," Wren said, not looking up. "I know."

"I want the datum pegged before we run tape."

"I'm pegging it now." Wren tightened the last screw and looked through the eyepiece. "The mound's profile is wrong."

"I said we'd run the tape."

"I'm not asking about the tape." Wren straightened and looked at her. "The slope break on the eastern edge. That's not erosion."

Tayak said nothing.

"I'm not putting it in the field log yet," Wren said. "I'm just saying I see it."

"Log what you can measure. We'll interpret when we have the full surface record."

Wren held her gaze for a moment, then turned back to the total station. "Understood."

It was not entirely understood, Tayak thought. But it was accepted, which was enough for now.

She walked the perimeter with Birch and Delia, pointing out the slope breaks, the shelf on the eastern edge, the change in grass color on the crown. She kept her voice level and instructive, the way she used it in the lab when she was teaching students to read a soil profile. She said: *note the vegetation change here, note the compaction here, note the slope break here.* She did not say: someone did this deliberately. She did not say: this mound is twice the size the state thinks it is, and I need to know who else has been here before I tell anyone that.

They started their transects. The sound of the field changed: boot-steps in wet grass, the snap of a pin flag being pushed into soil, the scratch of pencils on waterproof paper. Birch had taken the fleece.

Tayak walked the eastern perimeter alone.

She was looking at the ground the way she always looked at it on a first day — not searching, exactly, but open, her eyes moving in the loose, unfocused way that caught anomalies before the analytical mind could explain them away. The grass was heavy with last night's dew, soaking through the canvas of her boots. The sky was still flat and white, the light directionless, which was actually good for reading surface texture — no shadow to hide the subtle variations, no glare to flatten them.

She found the tire track near the eastern edge, twelve meters south of the shelf feature, in a narrow gap where the pasture fence made a slight jog inward and left a strip of bare, churned mud between the wire and the grass. The track was clear: a single pass, two tires, the tread a wide aggressive pattern — a truck, not a car, something with a working tire rather than a passenger all-season. The mud had dried to a crust on the surface but was still soft underneath when she pressed it with her thumbnail, which put the pass at least twenty-four hours ago, no more than seventy-two. Not from today. Not from her own vehicle, which she'd parked at the gate, forty meters north.

She stood very still.

Then she took out her phone and photographed it. Six shots: wide, medium, close, with her trowel laid beside the track for scale, the way she'd been trained. She photographed the fence post nearest to it, the treeline behind it, the mound's eastern edge visible in the background of the wide shot.

She put her phone away.

She took out her personal notebook — not the field log, the small black Moleskine she kept in the inside pocket of her jacket, the one she used for observations she wasn't ready to formalize — and wrote the time, the position relative to the datum peg, a rough sketch of the tread pattern, the estimated width between the tire centers.

She did not open the official field log.

She told herself it was because she didn't have enough information yet. She told herself it was standard practice to confirm before logging. Both of these things were true, and neither of them was the whole reason.

The whole reason was that she wanted to know whose truck it was before she created a record that anyone with a FOIA request could read.

She closed the Moleskine and put it back in her inside pocket.

"Dr. Moyaone." Delia's voice, from the transect line, twenty meters away. "I've got something on the east side."

Tayak walked toward her. Delia was crouching in the grass, her flag pushed in beside a small dark object, her field form on her knee.

"Ceramic," Delia said. "Rim sherd, I think. Very small."

Tayak crouched beside her. The sherd was thumb-sized, the color of old ash, its outer surface cord-marked in a pattern she recognized immediately — a tight, diagonal wrap, the kind made by pressing twisted cord into wet clay before firing. It was sitting on the surface, not buried, which meant it had worked its way up through frost cycles or animal disturbance, which meant there was more below.

"Good eye," Tayak said. "Photograph it in place, then bag and tag. Form number, GPS waypoint, depth noted as surface."

"Surface find," Delia said, writing.

"Surface find," Tayak confirmed.

She stood and looked at the mound. From this angle, with the light flat and the grass wet, she could see the shelf feature clearly — a slight step in the profile, barely visible, the kind of thing you'd miss from a car window or from the air, the kind of thing that only showed when you were standing at ground level, close enough to read the slope with your body.

Someone had stood here. Had read this slope. Had done something about it.

She looked at the tire track, still visible in the mud by the fence, and then back at the mound.

Forty meters away, Wren was sighting through the total station, her eye to the eyepiece, one hand resting on the instrument's housing. She had not looked up. But Tayak had worked with her long enough to know that Wren's attention was never as narrow as it appeared, and that whatever Wren had seen this morning, she was keeping in her own private column — the one she didn't share until she was sure.

Tayak wondered, for the first time, what Wren's private column already held.

She walked the northern face last.

The transects were still running behind her — she could hear Birch calling distances to Delia, a flat recitation, "six, seven, eight" — and she had the measuring rod in her right hand and the GPR printout folded in her jacket pocket, the paper worn soft at the creases from how many times she'd unfolded and refolded it since Voss had sent it over three weeks ago.

She stopped at the northern base and unfolded it now.

The printout showed a clean profile: a mound roughly eight meters across at the base, rising to a rounded crown approximately one point four meters above the surrounding ground surface. Modest. The kind of profile consistent with a late Woodland period burial feature, the kind that wouldn't raise flags at a state review, the kind that a SHPO officer could look at and call low-impact without losing sleep.

She looked up at the mound.

Then she looked down at the printout.

Then she looked up again.

The base was not eight meters. She had paced it twice already, east to west, and she had paced it again north to south, and the number she kept arriving at was closer to fourteen. She set the rod against the slope now, its foot in the soil at the base, and sighted along it. The angle was wrong. Not dramatically wrong — not the kind of wrong that shouted at you — but wrong in the quiet, persistent way of something that had been carefully made to look right. The slope was too gradual. The crown was too flat. A natural mound of this age would have settled into a softer, rounder profile, the crown dropping toward the center as the fill compacted over centuries. This crown was almost level. Not eroded level. Not plowed level. Level the way a surface is level when someone has moved material from the center outward and tamped it down.

She crouched.

The soil at the base of the northern face was darker than the surrounding pasture — a grayish-brown against the reddish clay of the field — and where the grass thinned near the slope's foot, she could see a faint line, barely a centimeter of exposed soil, where two different fills met. She set the rod down and pressed her fingers into the ground just above the line.

The upper layer gave slightly. The lower layer did not.

She pressed harder. The upper layer was loose — not freshly disturbed, but not consolidated either, not the way soil consolidated after decades of root penetration and freeze-thaw cycling. She pulled her fingers back and looked at the smear of soil on her glove. Darker than it should be. Damper than the surrounding ground, even accounting for last night's rain, because loose fill held moisture differently than settled matrix — it pulled water in and kept it, the way a sponge kept water, rather than channeling it away.

She took off the glove and pressed her bare fingers in.

The smell hit her before the texture did: damp earth and something deeper underneath, something ferrous and faintly organic, the smell of soil that had been turned and not yet settled back into itself. She had smelled it on excavations before, in the first moments after a spade cut, before the air dried the face of a new cut and took the smell away. She had never smelled it on a surface that was supposed to be undisturbed.

She sat back on her heels and looked at the line in the soil.

Someone had redistributed the upper fill. Not a plow — a plow left parallel scoring and a consistent depth, and she could see neither. Not erosion — erosion took material away, it didn't move it laterally and redeposit it in a smooth, unbroken layer. Not an animal — no animal made a deposit this even, this deliberate, across a surface this large.

Someone had stood here with tools and knowledge and reshaped this mound's profile. Had taken the crown down and spread it outward. Had made a fourteen-meter mound look like an eight-meter mound to a ground-penetrating radar survey conducted from the surface, because GPR read depth and density, and if you spread the upper fill thin enough across a wider base, you changed what the instrument saw.

She thought about the tire track on the eastern edge.

She thought about Nora's voice that morning: *They don't want it to be what it is.*

She pressed her fingers into the soil one more time, feeling the give of it, the wrongness of it, the deliberateness of it.

Then she stood, picked up the measuring rod, and pulled her glove back on.

She did not look toward Wren. She did not look toward the volunteers. She walked back to her kit bag, which was sitting against the datum peg, and she opened the official field log — the bound, waterproof, date-stamped log that was the legal record of this excavation — and she wrote, in her clearest handwriting:

*Northern face: apparent discrepancy between GPR-predicted base diameter (approx. 8m) and measured base diameter (approx. 14m). Crown profile atypically flat. Upper fill layer at northern base appears unconsolidated relative to lower matrix; possible altered profile. Soil moisture anomaly consistent with recent disturbance of fill. Note: further investigation required before interpretation.*

She read it back.

*Altered profile.* Two words. Neutral, technical, exactly the kind of language that described what she could see without stating what she knew.

She closed the log.

Wren was walking toward her from the total station, her field notebook open, a pencil tucked behind her ear. She had the careful, unhurried walk of someone who had already decided what she was going to say and was taking her time getting there.

"I've been checking my measurements against the GPR overlay," Wren said. She stopped a meter away and held out her notebook. Her handwriting was very small and very even, the numbers in a column down the left margin. "The base diameter doesn't match. On any face."

Tayak looked at the numbers. They matched her own pacing almost exactly.

"I see that," she said.

"It's not instrument error." Wren said it quietly, not as a challenge. "I ran it twice."

"I know."

Wren looked at the mound. Then she looked back at Tayak. She did not ask the obvious question — the question Tayak could see sitting just behind her eyes — because Wren had been in the field long enough to know that the obvious question, spoken aloud on day one, before you had the evidence to carry it, was the kind of question that could end a project.

"I'll note the discrepancy in my field notes," Wren said.

"Note the measurements," Tayak said. "Just the measurements."

Wren wrote something. Tayak did not read it over her shoulder.

"The cord-marked sherd Delia found," Wren said, without looking up. "I've seen that diagonal wrap pattern before. In the Accokeek assemblage. The one in the university collection."

Tayak went still.

The Accokeek assemblage was pre-contact. The diagnostic cord-wrapping pattern Wren was describing had been dated to the late Woodland period, no later than 900 CE, possibly earlier. If the sherd on the eastern face was typologically consistent with that assemblage, it meant the surface scatter was older than the permit application had projected. It meant the mound's occupation sequence was longer than the GPR survey had suggested. It meant the altered profile was not just a physical anomaly — it was a physical anomaly on top of a site that was already more significant than anyone had told the state it was.

She looked at the tire track, still visible from here if you knew where to look, a dark line in the mud by the eastern fence.

Someone had known.

"Bag the sherd for the lab," she said. "Don't put a period estimate on the form. Just the typological description."

Wren looked up. "You want me to leave it open."

"I want the lab to tell us what it is."

It was not a lie. It was also not the whole truth. The whole truth was that she needed time — time to understand what she was standing on before she filed anything that would trigger a mandatory review, a review that would go to Slade's office, a review that Slade could use to freeze the permit while he decided what to do with a site that was twice the size the state had been told it was.

Wren closed her notebook. She looked at the mound for a long moment, the way she looked at things when she was memorizing them.

"Dr. Moyaone," she said. "The profile."

"Yes."

"Someone did that on purpose."

Tayak picked up her field log. She looked at the two words she had written — *altered profile* — and thought about how much weight those two words were being asked to carry.

"Note the measurements," she said again. "Just the measurements."

Wren nodded once. She turned and walked back toward the total station.

Tayak stood at the northern face of the mound and looked at the flat crown against the white sky. Fourteen meters. Unconsolidated fill. A cord-marked sherd consistent with a pre-contact assemblage. A tire track twenty-four to seventy-two hours old.

She thought: *Someone reshaped this mound. And someone came back to check their work.*

She did not write that down.

Wren came back forty minutes later.

She had the elevation rod under one arm and her notebook open in her other hand, and she walked the way she always walked when she had numbers she wanted to talk about — with her chin down, like she was reading the ground. The two undergraduates, Delia and Marcus-not-Marcus — she had already started calling him by his last name, Osei, to avoid confusion — were still on the eastern transect, their orange pin flags bright against the pale grass.

"Crown elevation," Wren said, and held out the notebook.

Tayak took it. The numbers were arranged in a table, four readings at the cardinal points and one at the center, all tied to the datum peg. Wren had drawn a small cross-section sketch in the margin, barely two centimeters wide, the mound's profile rendered in a few pencil strokes. It was flat on top in the sketch, the way a hill looks after someone has taken a blade to it.

The predicted crown elevation from the GPR survey was 1.8 meters above the surrounding field. Voss's team had used that figure in the permit application. Tayak had used it herself, because it was the number they had.

The number in Wren's table was 1.2 meters.

Tayak stood with the notebook and looked at the figure. She looked at it for long enough that she became aware of looking at it for too long, and she looked up.

The sky had gone flat and white while they worked, the kind of Charles County overcast that pressed down on the fields like a lid. The treeline to the south was still. A crow crossed above the mound and was gone.

"Does this match what Voss's team projected?" Wren asked.

Her voice was even. She was not accusing anyone of anything. She was asking a question that any field assistant would ask, that any careful person standing in front of a discrepancy would ask, and the question was entirely reasonable and Tayak could not answer it honestly without saying things she was not ready to say in front of an open field notebook on the first day of excavation.

"We'll need more data before I can say," Tayak said. She handed the notebook back. "Let's get Delia and Osei onto the western transect before the light goes. I want full coverage today."

Wren took the notebook. She did not write anything in it. She held it at her side and looked at Tayak the way she had looked at the mound earlier — that memorizing look, patient and very still.

"Grid assignments," Tayak said. She moved toward her kit bag and pulled out the site plan, a laminated sheet she had marked up the night before, grid squares labeled A through F along the north-south axis. She spread it against her knee and pointed. "Western face, C and D squares. Osei takes C, Delia takes D. You take the southern approach, E square. I want pin flags at every soil change and every surface find. Nothing gets bagged without a GPS tag."

"All right," Wren said.

She turned and walked toward the eastern transect to collect the volunteers, her boots leaving shallow prints in the damp turf. At the edge of the mound's slope she paused, just briefly, and looked back at the crown — at the flat line of it against the white sky — and then she kept walking.

Tayak watched her go.

She folded the site plan and put it back in her kit bag. Her fingers found the field log at the bottom of the bag, the bound waterproof cover soft with use. She did not take it out. She had already written what she could write. The rest was not ready to be written.

Sixty meters away, Wren reached the volunteers and said something Tayak couldn't hear. Osei laughed. Delia crouched to pick up a pin flag that had blown loose and pressed it back into the turf. Ordinary field sounds: tape measure clicking, boots on dry grass, the soft percussion of a notebook cover opening.

Tayak looked at the mound.

Fourteen meters at the base. Crown at 1.2 meters, not 1.8. The difference was not erosion — erosion softened slopes, it didn't truncate crowns. The difference was not plowing — the field around the mound had not been tilled in living memory, the old furrow lines long since smoothed over. The difference was sixty centimeters of deliberate removal, and whoever had done it had known enough to take from the crown and leave the base vegetation undisturbed, so that from the county road, from a car, from a satellite image, the mound still looked like a low natural rise in a field.

She thought about Nora's voice that morning, the particular flatness of it when she said: *You'll find what's there to be found.*

Not *I hope you find something.* Not *I wonder what's there.* 

*You'll find what's there to be found.*

She put that thought away.

Across the field, Wren had settled at the southern approach, kneeling in the grass at the edge of E square, her notebook open on her knee. She wrote something. Then she stopped and looked at the mound's crown again, and Tayak saw her write something else — a single line, from the way her hand moved. Short. Deliberate.

Tayak could not read it from here.

She did not need to.

The light was going by the time they finished the western transect. Not dark yet — a slow Charles County dimming, the white sky deepening to grey at the edges, the treeline to the south thickening into silhouette. Osei had a headlamp around his neck and kept touching it the way people touch a key they're not sure they locked the door with.

"That's the coverage," Tayak said. "Good work today."

Delia was already bagging the last of the pin flags, rolling them in bundles of ten the way Tayak had shown her that morning. Osei coiled the measuring tape with the mechanical precision of someone who had been corrected about it once and intended never to be corrected again. Wren stood at the equipment table — a folding card table they'd set up at the site margin, its legs sinking slightly into the soft ground — and was labeling sample bags in her small, even hand, each bag turned to catch the last of the light.

"I'll do the final GPS log," Wren said, without looking up. "Then we can load out."

"Go ahead."

Tayak carried her kit bag to the truck and set it on the tailgate. The field had gone very quiet. A wood thrush called once from the tree line, the sound dropping clean through the still air, and then nothing. The smell of the turned soil from the test cuts lingered — damp iron, something older underneath it, the particular sourness of earth that has been sealed for a long time and has not yet adjusted to being open. She had smelled it at other sites. She had never smelled it and thought about it the way she was thinking about it now.

She listened to the three of them loading out. The card table folding, its legs knocking together. The soft thud of equipment bags in the truck bed. Delia saying something to Osei in a low voice; Osei's short laugh. Wren's boots on the gravel at the site margin, steady and unhurried.

"We're loaded," Wren said.

Tayak turned. Wren was standing at the rear of the truck, both hands in her jacket pockets, her notebook tucked under one arm. Behind her, Delia and Osei were already in the cab, their voices muffled through the glass.

"You two take the truck," Tayak said. "I'll walk out. I want to do a final check of the datum peg."

Wren looked at her. The datum peg was thirty meters away and in no danger.

"All right," Wren said.

She did not say anything else. She climbed into the passenger seat and pulled the door shut, and Tayak stood and watched the truck move down the access track toward the county road, its headlights coming on as it reached the gravel, sweeping once across the field before it turned south and was gone.

---

The field was hers now.

She walked back to the mound. The grass was wet with evening dew, soaking through the knees of her trousers where she'd been kneeling, and her boots left dark prints in the pale turf. The mound rose ahead of her — low, flat-crowned, ordinary-looking in the way that only a very careful reshaping could achieve. A natural rise would have a gentler crown, a more varied profile. This one was too even. She had been thinking about it all afternoon in the part of her mind she kept running below the work, and she had not been able to stop.

She climbed to the crown. It was a short climb — 1.2 meters, Wren's figure, not 1.8 — and at the top she turned and looked out across the field. The county road was a grey line to the north, the treeline a dark mass to the south. A fence post caught the last light at the field's eastern edge. She could see the tire track from here, the shallow parallel lines she had found that morning on the northern slope, their edges already softening in the evening damp. From this height, at this angle, they were unmistakable. From the road, from a car, they would be invisible.

She stood there for a moment. The thrush called again, farther off this time.

Then she sat down on the crown of the mound and opened her field log.

The waterproof cover was stiff with dried mud from the season's first test trench at a different site, three weeks ago. She found today's date, turned to the next clean line, and wrote in the careful, level hand she used for official records — the hand that assumed a court might read it.

*Day one surface survey complete. Full transect coverage achieved, all six grid squares. 34 surface finds flagged and GPS-tagged, predominantly lithic scatter and historic-period ceramic fragments. Topographic measurements recorded at cardinal points and datum. Minor variance noted between projected crown elevation (1.8m, per GPR survey, permit application) and measured crown elevation (1.2m). Further investigation required to determine cause. No subsurface work undertaken. Weather: overcast, dry, light wind. Team: Moyaone, Calvert, Osei Asante, Delia Marsh.*

She read it back. Every word was true. Every word was also insufficient, and she knew it, and she closed the log and set it on the ground beside her.

She took the personal notebook from her jacket pocket. It was a cheap thing — black cover, narrow rule, the kind sold in drugstores — and she had been keeping one like it since her first field season at twenty-two, a habit her methods professor had called unprofessional and that she had never stopped. She found the current page, uncapped her pen, and wrote without stopping.

*The mound has been touched. Not eroded, not plowed, not settled — touched. The crown has been taken down by approximately 60cm, removed in a way that left the base vegetation intact and the slope profile undisturbed below the 0.8m contour. Whoever did this knew that the base reads as natural from distance. They knew where to cut and where to stop. The GPR survey was done in 2019 — Voss's team, pre-permit. If the reshaping happened after 2019, the GPR data is accurate and the mound has been modified in the last five years. If it happened before 2019, the GPR missed it or the data was misread. Either way the permit application used a figure that doesn't match the ground.*

She stopped. Looked at what she had written.

*The tire track on the north slope is 2–3 seasons old. Possibly less. Consistent with a small utility vehicle, or a quad. No tool marks visible on the crown surface — the removal was done with something broad and smooth, not a blade edge. Possibly a plate compactor. Possibly a small loader with a bucket. Someone who knew enough to work slowly.*

She thought about the permit. She thought about Voss's name on the permit application, and her own name below his, and the figure 1.8 meters that she had not questioned because it was the number they had. She thought about what it would mean to file an amended survey report noting deliberate disturbance before she had evidence of who had done the disturbing. She thought about Garrett Slade's office in Annapolis, the clean desk, the framed photographs of his marathons on the wall.

She wrote: *I cannot file this without evidence. If I file without evidence and I am wrong, the permit is in question and the dig stops. If I file without evidence and I am right, the dig still stops while the disturbance is investigated. Either way the dig stops. I need to know who did this before I say anything official.*

She read that back too.

Then she wrote, below it, smaller: *Nora knew the mound was here. She has known for years. She said: you'll find what's there to be found. She did not say: I wonder what you'll find.*

She capped the pen. She did not write what that implied. She did not need to write it. It was already in the notebook in the only form that mattered — not as an accusation, not as a conclusion, but as a question she was now carrying.

She closed the notebook and put it back in her jacket pocket.

The field was fully dark now at the edges, the sky still holding a thin grey light at the center. She picked up the field log and stood, her knees aching from the damp, and looked out one more time across the mound's flat crown. The tire track was invisible in the dark. The fence post was invisible. The treeline was a black mass against the grey sky, and the county road was nothing, just a memory of a line.

She climbed down from the crown and walked back across the field toward her truck. The dew had deepened; her boots came up wet with each step, the turf giving slightly under her weight, the way turf does over fill that has been disturbed and has not yet fully settled. She noticed that. She noticed it and did not stop walking.

At the truck she stowed both notebooks in the glove box, locked it, and sat for a moment with her hands on the wheel. Through the windshield the mound was a low dark shape against the field, already looking like nothing. Already looking like a natural rise. Already looking like something a person could drive past on the county road and not see.

She started the engine and turned south.

Behind her, in the glove box, the field log said *minor variance noted* and the personal notebook said *someone knew enough to work slowly*, and both of those things were true, and they were not the same truth, and she was the only person who knew both of them, and she had chosen — for now, for tonight, for as long as she could hold it — not to say which one was the one that mattered.

The question she could not answer: whether that choice was caution or something she would have to answer for later.
