## Chapter 44 — Stratum Five: The Copper Pendant — present-day discovery
*[Present — 2024] · POV Tayak — 5 sub-chapters*

| # | Title | POV | Beat |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The Ring in the Earth | Tayak | Tayak's trowel strikes the copper pendant with a faint metallic note. She freezes, brush in hand, afraid to damage it — and afraid of what it might be. She clears the soil millimeter by millimeter, alone in the trench in the late afternoon light, until the crescent shape emerges: Hopewell-influenced, green-black with age, a drilled hole at its apex. Her professional training catalogs it instantly. The rest of her goes very still. |
| 2 | The Vision: Firelight and a Child | Tayak | The vision takes her without warning — longer and more vivid than any before. A woman kneels in firelight: the same face Tayak saw in the shell gorget vision, older now, or perhaps the same age but worn differently. She holds the pendant above a small child's head. The child is perhaps two years old. The woman's hands are steady. She lowers the pendant around the child's neck and speaks a name. In the vision's strange acoustics — sound arriving as if through water — the name is unmistakably, impossibly, *Tayak*. |
| 3 | The Field Log | Tayak | The vision ends. Tayak is sitting in the trench. She does not know how long she has been there. The pendant is in the evidence tray. Her hands are shaking in a way she cannot make stop. She picks up the field log — the one she has filled with precise, defensible, professionally calibrated language for every prior find. She opens it to the next blank line. She holds the pen above the page for a long time. She closes the log without writing anything. This is the first time in her career she has done this. |
| 4 | The Call | Tayak | Tayak climbs out of the trench, walks to the site perimeter — past Wren's equipment cases, past the flagged grid markers, to the edge of the field where the tree line begins — and calls Nora. Nora picks up before the second ring. Before Tayak can say a single word, Nora says: 'I know. I know what you found.' Tayak stands at the tree line with the phone against her ear and says nothing for a long moment. Then she asks: how. |
| 5 | Before You Were Born | Tayak | Nora says: 'Because I've been waiting for you to find it since before you were born.' She does not explain further — not yet. She asks if Tayak is alone. Tayak says yes. Nora says: 'Good. Don't write it down yet. Come see me first.' The call ends. Tayak stands at the tree line with the phone in her hand, the site behind her, the pendant in its tray, the field log unopened. She understands now that her grandmother has known the contents of this mound for decades — and has said nothing. The question of what else Nora knows, and why she has kept it, is suddenly more urgent than any artifact. |

## Chapter 46 — The Pendant Passes — the life of the pendant's owner
*[Past — circa 200–300 CE] · POV Waya — 5 sub-chapters*

| # | Title | POV | Beat |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The Weight of It | Waya | Waya sits with her mother Lena in the longhouse at dusk, watching her mother's hands tremble as she lifts the copper pendant from around her own neck. Waya's hope: that the pendant will come to her as a sign of trust and continuity. Her fear: that her mother is giving it away because she is dying and knows it. |
| 2 | This Came from Far Away | Waya | Lena places the pendant around Waya's neck and speaks the words — 'This came from far away. It means we are not alone.' She tells Waya the chain: four generations back, a trader from the north passed through once and left it as a gift, and no one has seen his people since. The pendant is proof that the world is larger than their suffering. Lena dies three days later. |
| 3 | Thirty Years | Waya | A compressed passage: Waya wears the pendant through drought, through the births and deaths of children, through the years after Sokan's death when she becomes the one who holds the old knowledge. She touches it at every ceremony. Her daughter Suli grows up watching her mother's hand rise to it in moments of fear or decision — and learns what the gesture means before she learns the words. |
| 4 | The Same Words | Waya | Waya, now old, calls Suli to her and lifts the pendant from her own neck. She speaks the words exactly as her mother spoke them. Suli asks where it truly came from — who made it, what people, what hands shaped the copper. Waya admits she does not know. That not-knowing is part of what it means. It came from somewhere beyond the edge of what they can see. That is the point. |
| 5 | Not Lost, But Kept | Waya | Suli wears the pendant for thirty years. On her deathbed she tells Waya's granddaughter that it must not be buried with her alone — it must go to the mound, with the other objects, where it will be kept rather than lost. She uses a word that means both 'remembered' and 'held.' The granddaughter does not fully understand but promises. The pendant is placed with the mound's objects. The chain of transmission ends — and becomes something else. |

## Chapter 61 — Yanu's World — the founding ancestor, deepest stratum
*[Past — circa 100 BC] · POV Yanu — 5 sub-chapters*

| # | Title | POV | Beat |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The Birds Know First | Yanu | Yanu stands at the river's edge at dawn, watching a murmuration of starlings shift direction three times in quick succession — a sign she reads as a storm coming from the northwest within two days. She calls out to her oldest surviving daughter, Sika, who is gathering reeds nearby, and begins teaching her the specific pattern: not the flock's shape but the hesitation between turns. Yanu's hope entering the scene: that this ordinary morning, this teaching, this life, is enough. Her fear: the copper pendant is warm against her collarbone and she cannot stop touching it. |
| 2 | The Weight of the Pendant | Yanu | Yanu returns to the community's cluster of structures and passes the place where Taya died — a specific spot near the fire ring she cannot stop marking with her eyes. Her husband Ranu is already awake, telling a story to two of the younger children about a beaver who outwitted a river spirit; the children laugh. Yanu watches him and feels the distance between his grief and hers: he has made Taya into a story already, and she has not yet made Taya into anything. She touches the pendant — the Hopewell trader's copper work, made for a child who wore it for six weeks — and does not join the circle. |
| 3 | The Full Life | Yanu | The community's morning work begins: Yanu joins the women processing dried fish, and the scene dramatizes the texture of her world — the specific jokes exchanged, the hierarchy of who sits where, the small argument between two men about a canoe repair, the smell of the river and the smoke. Yanu is respected here; people ask her about the weather, defer to her reading of the birds. Her three living children move through the scene. This is a full life. The story turn: Yanu's neighbor Pika asks, gently, whether Yanu has yet decided what to do with the pendant — whether she will keep wearing it or give it to the earth with Taya's memory. Yanu says she does not know. [ESCALATION SEED — starts here: Yanu has not resolved what to do with the pendant; this unresolved grief will force the burial decision in ch. 63 and pay off when Tayak's trowel finds it in ch. 73.] |
| 4 | Ranu's Story | Yanu | That evening, Ranu tells the community a story about Taya — a story in which Taya appears as a small spirit who taught the birds to change direction, which is why the flocks move the way they do. The community receives it warmly. Children ask questions. Yanu sits at the edge of the firelight and listens, and the scene dramatizes the specific agony of watching someone you love grieve in a way that is entirely foreign to you: Ranu's story is beautiful and it makes Taya smaller, not larger. Yanu does not speak. She holds the pendant in her fist under her robe. |
| 5 | What She Does Not Yet Know | Yanu | After the fire dies, Yanu lies awake beside Ranu and her children and looks at the pendant in the dark — she can feel its shape without seeing it. She does not know what to do with the grief. She does not know that in two years she will find the answer. She does not know that the answer will be the mound. She does not know that the pendant will outlast everything. The scene ends on a specific, concrete image: Yanu hears the birds shift in the trees above the camp — a small sound, a night adjustment — and she reads it correctly: the storm will come tomorrow, not in two days. She was wrong by a day. She notes this to herself, corrects her reading, and closes her eyes. The new question raised: what will it take for Yanu to find the answer she does not yet have? |
