- **Regional peoples and names**  
  Late Woodland–contact peoples around the Chesapeake included groups later known as the Powhatan, Piscataway, Nanticoke, and other Algonquian-speaking chiefdoms, as well as Iroquoian-speaking groups like the Susquehannocks upriver.[3]  
  The tidal Potomac, Rappahannock, York, and James rivers formed the core of this **“Tidewater”** world, with villages often placed on the first or second terrace above the floodplain.[3]

- **River geography and salinity zones**  
  Chesapeake tributaries are **drowned river valleys**, broad and slow near their mouths, narrowing and freshening upstream; salinity creates distinct ecological zones (salt marsh, brackish marsh, freshwater tidal swamp).  
  Late Woodland villages often sat in brackish reaches where people could exploit both **anadromous fish** (shad, herring, sturgeon) and estuarine species (oysters, blue crabs).[3]

- **Seasonal fish runs (“the river’s gift”)**  
  Spring runs of **American shad, river herring, and striped bass** crowded the rivers, making fish a major seasonal bounty.[3]  
  People trapped fish with **weirs** of woven brush or stakes set in a V-shape and with nets and basket traps; the sound and sight would be of water thrashing with silver bodies and the smell of fish oils and wet wood.

- **Oyster bars and shell middens**  
  Tidal creeks and river mouths held dense **oyster reefs**, harvested at low tide by hand or with wooden poles.[3]  
  Long-term shell dumping created **midden ridges** of crushed white shell, gleaming in the sun, smelling faintly of brine and decay after fresh dumping; these often underlay or bordered village areas.

- **Bow and arrow technology**  
  By the Late Woodland period, hunters relied on the **bow and arrow** with small, triangular stone points, replacing most spear use.[3][1]  
  Bows were wooden, often as tall as the archer, with sinew or plant-fiber strings; arrows were reed or wooden shafts fletched with feathers, producing a soft hiss in flight.

- **Triangular projectile points**  
  The diagnostic artifact of the period in Virginia is the **small triangular arrowhead**, often of local quartzite, chert, or rhyolite.[3]  
  Edges were finely pressure-flaked; a novelist could note the sharp, glassy feel, the flake-scar ripples, and the way unfinished blanks littered a knapper’s work area like glittering chips.

- **Maize–bean–squash agriculture**  
  New varieties of **maize, beans, and squash** became central to the diet, supplementing native seeds and roots.[1][3]  
  Maize was grown in small hills with beans climbing the stalks and squash spreading between, in gardens close to villages; the smell would be of warm earth, crushed green leaves, and drying husks in late summer.

- **Other plant foods and foraging**  
  People continued to gather **nut crops** (acorns, hickory, walnuts) and native seeds such as chenopodium and maygrass, as well as roots and tubers from wetlands.[1][3]  
  Nut processing involved pounding kernels in wooden mortars, leaching acorns in water to remove bitterness, and roasting; the air would carry the aromas of smoke, warm nuts, and damp tannin.

- **Settlement pattern and village layout**  
  Late Woodland settlement in the Chesapeake shows **many small sites** rather than a few huge ones, indicating population growth and dispersed hamlets.[1][3]  
  Villages were often palisaded, with houses oriented around central open spaces for work and gatherings; shell, bone, and pottery sherds underfoot gave the ground a gritty, crunching feel.

- **House construction (yehakins/longhouses)**  
  Dwellings were **bent-sapling frame houses** covered with bark or woven mats, sometimes elongated “longhouses” in larger villages.[3]  
  Interiors were dim and smoky, with central hearths and storage pits; light filtered through smoke-holes and mat seams, and smells mingled: woodsmoke, drying corn, hides, and human bodies.

- **Pottery forms and decoration**  
  Late Woodland pottery in Virginia became more **standardized and regionally distinctive**, generally coil-built, smooth or cord-marked, and tempered with sand or crushed rock.[3][4]  
  Vessels were globular or jar-shaped with rounded bases, suitable for setting in earth beside fires; cord-marked exteriors felt rough under the hand, and rims sometimes bore simple incised motifs.

- **Clay sourcing and local color**  
  Potters collected clay from **riverbanks and creek cuts**, often mixing clays from different local sources, which modern geochemical studies can distinguish.[4]  
  Clay was kneaded with temper, then coils were laid and paddled; a novelist can use the tactile stickiness, the squeak of wet clay, and the sight of red, brown, or gray clays staining hands.

- **Lithic raw materials and trade**  
  Stone for tools and points came from **local gravels** and more distant quarries in the Appalachians and Piedmont.[4]  
  Some types—like high-quality rhyolite or jasper—indicate regional exchange networks; their colors (deep greenish-gray rhyolite, yellow-brown jasper) would stand out against common local cobbles.

- **Burial mounds and their decline**  
  Earlier Woodland peoples built **burial mounds**, but by the Late Woodland in much of the East, mound construction had sharply declined, and other forms of burial became common.[1]  
  Where older mounds existed, they could appear as low, tree-covered rises overlooking rivers, perhaps partly eroded, with old trade items (copper, exotic stone) buried within from earlier times.

- **Food storage and pits**  
  Surplus maize and nuts were stored in **subterranean pits** or bark-lined caches, sometimes within house floors or just outside.[3]  
  Opened pits smelled of cool, damp earth and dried grain; if storage failed, a sour, moldy odor and clumped kernels signaled spoilage and loss.

- **Cooking methods and flavors**  
  Common methods included **stone boiling** (dropping heated stones into water-filled pots), roasting on wooden spits, and baking in earth ovens.  
  Fish and meat were often smoked over low fires, hanging from racks; the taste profile would be lightly salted from brine, heavy with woodsmoke, and sometimes sweetened with squash or nut-meal.

- **Textiles and cordage**  
  People made cordage from **plant fibers** (milkweed, dogbane, nettle, inner bark) and used it for nets, fish lines, and lashing.  
  Nets for river fishing were knotted from fine, slightly rough cord; when wet, they darkened and smelled of mud, fish slime, and vegetal fiber.

- **Transportation and canoes**  
  Rivers were highways traveled in **dugout canoes**, hollowed from large logs (e.g., tulip poplar, oak) by controlled burning and adzing.  
  A canoe’s interior was charred and scraped smooth; paddling produced a muffled slap of wood on water, the hull smelling of smoke, resin, and river.

- **Climate variability and drought episodes**  
  Late Woodland communities experienced climate fluctuations, including **multi-year droughts** that could stress maize production and alter settlement or conflict patterns.[6]  
  During dry years, rivers ran low, exposing more sandbars and shallows, and fish runs could be weaker, increasing reliance on nuts, wild plants, and stored food.

- **Hunting and game along the river**  
  Forest edges and river bottoms supported **white-tailed deer, black bear, turkey, waterfowl, and small mammals**.  
  Hunters moved silently through moist leaf litter and undergrowth of laurel and young hardwoods, the river nearby audible as a steady hiss or lapping, with occasional calls of herons and geese.

- **Fire use and landscape management**  
  People used **controlled burning** to maintain open fields and park-like woods, encouraging deer and certain plant foods.  
  A burned-over area after a few seasons would show lush new growth, berry thickets, and easier travel; the scent of old charcoal and ash lingered in the soil when disturbed.

- **Social and political organization**  
  By late pre-contact, the lower Chesapeake showed a **complex geopolitical landscape** of ranked chiefdoms and smaller polities centered on key rivers.[4]  
  Authority often focused in paramount chiefs controlling tribute of maize, furs, and shell beads, with alliances and rivalries mapped along water routes rather than abstract borders.

## Full text

She had not slept.

Tayak sat in her truck at the edge of the access road, engine off, watching the sky above the tree line shift from black to the color of a bruise. The heater had quit twenty minutes ago. She didn't move to restart it. The cold kept her sharp, kept yesterday at arm's length—or tried to.

She had driven home from the site, eaten half a bowl of soup she couldn't taste, and then sat at her kitchen table for four hours with her field notebook open to a blank page. The pen had stayed dry. There was no academic language for what had happened when she touched those copper fragments. No stratigraphic notation, no catalog number, no peer-reviewed framework that could hold it.

Red Fire Woman's hands. The hammer stroke falling. The smell of something burning that had no name in any language Tayak knew.

She got out of the truck.

The path to the mound ran between stands of tulip poplar, their bare February trunks pale as bone in the beam of her headlamp. The ground was frozen hard enough to ring under her boots, and the cold had a smell—iron and old leaves and the faint brine edge that came off the Potomac even this far from the bank, the river's breath reaching through a quarter mile of bottomland. A great blue heron lifted from somewhere in the dark ahead of her, its wingbeats slow and enormous, and was gone.

The site appeared as a low rise in the lamplight, tree-covered and unremarkable to anyone who didn't know what lay beneath. Tayak had measured it on her first survey: eleven meters across, less than two meters of vertical relief above the surrounding ground. The kind of mound that erosion and two thousand years of root growth had pressed nearly flat. She had driven past it a dozen times before she'd stopped.

She ducked under the orange mesh fencing and set her kit down at the edge of Unit 4, the deepest trench, the one she'd been working for three weeks. The tarp she'd staked over it last night had held against the frost. She folded it back carefully, clipped her headlamp to the adjustable arm of the portable LED stand, and switched it on.

The unit glowed white and clean. The artifacts from yesterday were gone—bagged, labeled, locked in the transport case in her truck. But the earth where they had rested still showed the impression of them: a slight hollow in the reddish-brown clay, the soil around it undisturbed in a way that made the absence feel like a held breath.

She lowered herself into the trench, knees and palms, and began.

The trowel moved in short, deliberate strokes. She had learned to read this soil the way she'd learned to read a page—not word by word but in phrases, in the rhythm of color change and texture shift. The upper levels were mixed, disturbed by tree roots and the slow churn of freeze-thaw cycles over centuries. But down here, at sixty centimeters below datum, the stratigraphy went still. The clay was dense and cold and had the faint iron-red color of a soil that had never been plowed, never been turned. It held its secrets the way a closed fist holds a stone.

She was working east of the copper pendant's position, extending the exposure by four centimeters, when the trowel met something that didn't give.

Not root. Not rock. Something smooth.

She set the trowel down and picked up the horsehair brush—the one she kept for this level, worn down to a soft whisper of bristles from three weeks of careful work. She swept in one direction, then the other.

Dark gray emerged from the red-brown clay. A curve. Then a second curve meeting the first.

A stone. River-smoothed, the way only sustained water could make stone—not the rough tumbling of a creek bed but the long, patient polishing of a river running over the same surface for a thousand years. The Potomac had made this. She knew it the way she knew the feel of her own tools: the Potomac ran a quarter mile east, and its gravel bars threw up stones exactly like this, worn to an almost waxy finish, the kind of surface that caught light and held it.

She brushed again. The stone was dark gray with a faint greenish undertone—argillite, she thought, or possibly a dense greenstone, the kind of rock that didn't occur naturally in this clay-and-sand formation. Someone had carried it here.

She photographed it before she touched it. She always did. She shot it from directly above, then from the north, south, east, and west, her camera's macro lens pulling the texture into focus—the fine scratches of river gravel still visible on its surface beneath the polish, the way one edge had a slight flattening that might have been deliberate or might have been the accident of how it had come to rest. She measured its position with the folding rule: eleven centimeters north-northeast of the copper pendant's center point.

She wrote it in her field notebook in the careful block letters she used for documentation:

*Unit 4, Level 6. River-smoothed stone, argillite or greenstone, approx. 9 cm long, 6 cm wide, 3 cm thick. Surface polish consistent with long-term fluvial abrasion. Position: 11 cm NNE of copper pendant locus. Orientation: long axis running roughly N-S. Depth: 61 cm below datum. No obvious working marks. Placement relative to pendant appears non-accidental.*

She underlined the last two words. Then she looked at them for a moment and added: *Intentional burial pairing strongly suggested.*

The sky above the trench had gone from bruise-purple to a thin, cold gray. She could hear the Potomac now—not the close rushing sound of a fast river but the low, steady voice of a tidal river moving with the tide's permission, broad and slow and patient. In spring, this stretch would fill with shad running upstream, thousands of silver bodies crowding the water so thick the surface boiled white. She had read about the old weirs—stakes driven in a V-shape, woven brush between them—that had made the shad runs a harvest rather than a spectacle. Her ancestors had fed their families from this river for two thousand years.

She put the notebook down and reached for the stone.

It fit her palm as though it had been made for it. That was the first thing—the way it settled, its weight distributed across the pad of her hand, the smooth surface neither warm nor cold for one instant, balanced exactly at the temperature of her skin. Then, as she held it, she became aware that it was warmer than the surrounding clay had been. Not dramatically. Not the way the copper pendant had been warm yesterday, that impossible warmth. Just—not cold. The way a stone holds heat from an afternoon sun even after the sun has gone.

Except there had been no sun. This stone had been buried under sixty centimeters of frozen clay since before the first European ship had entered the Chesapeake.

She turned it in her hands. The surface caught the LED light and threw it back in a long, faint streak—almost a reflection. She could see the distorted shape of her own fingers in it, curved and dark, like a face seen in a river.

She looked at the hollow where the copper pendant had rested yesterday, then at the stone in her hand, then at the measurement in her notebook. Eleven centimeters. The distance wasn't random. In a burial context with this level of intentional deposition—the pendant placed with care, the stone placed with equal care at a specific remove from it—you didn't get eleven centimeters by accident. You got eleven centimeters because someone had chosen eleven centimeters.

She photographed the stone in her hand. She photographed it against the scale bar. She photographed it against the soil where it had rested, so the shape of its absence was recorded. She was thorough. She was always thorough. It was the only reliable thing she knew how to be.

But she did not put it in the collection bag yet.

She held it a moment longer, her thumb moving across the polished surface in a slow arc, and listened to the Potomac moving through the bottomland in the February dark. The sound of it was old. Older than anything she could date with a carbon sample or a typological chart. The river had been making that sound since before the mound existed, and it would be making that sound long after every artifact she'd ever cataloged had turned to dust.

Eleven centimeters from the copper pendant. A stone that the river had made perfect, carried here by hands she couldn't name yet.

She wrote one more line in her notebook, below the formal documentation, in her own handwriting rather than block letters:

*It was waiting.*

Then she bagged it, labeled it, and climbed out of the trench to watch the sun come up over the Potomac.

---

She ate breakfast in the truck—a granola bar and cold coffee from the thermos she'd filled at five in the morning—and watched the light change over the river. The Potomac was running gray-green in the February dawn, its surface flat and opaque, the far bank a dark smear of leafless trees. A great blue heron stood motionless at the water's edge, one leg lifted, patient as stone.

She should drive back to the lab. The stone and the pendant needed proper storage conditions, controlled humidity, acid-free tissue, the whole protocol. She had a faculty meeting at eleven. She had a graduate student's chapter draft waiting in her inbox, flagged urgent three days ago.

She poured more coffee and stayed where she was.

The collection bag sat on the passenger seat. She could see the stone through the clear plastic, its dark surface catching the early light. She had labeled it correctly—*Unit 4, Level 6, Specimen 4-6-02, argillite/greenstone, river-smoothed*—and sealed the bag with the red evidence tape she used when she didn't want to second-guess herself later about whether she'd opened it. She had not opened it.

She finished the granola bar and drove back to the site.

---

The trench was exactly as she'd left it. She pulled the blue sheeting back and stood at the trench edge for a moment, looking down at Unit 4.

The hollow where the stone had rested was still visible—a shallow depression in the clay, its edges crisp, the soil around it slightly darker from where her brush had disturbed the surface. Beside it, the larger hollow where the copper pendant had lain. Two absences, eleven centimeters apart, reading the burial like a text she was only beginning to understand.

She climbed down and got to work.

The field notebook was in her jacket pocket. She opened it to the last page of yesterday's notes and uncapped her pen.

*Unit 4, Level 6. Continued examination of burial context. Copper pendant (4-6-01) removed to collection previous session. River stone (4-6-02) removed to collection previous session. Both specimens in transit to lab.*

She wrote that much and stopped.

The Potomac was louder this morning. She told herself it was the tide—the tidal reach extended this far upriver, the water level rising and falling a foot or more twice a day, and a rising tide would push the current's voice ahead of it. She wrote it in the margin of her notebook as a reminder: *check tide table*.

She did not check the tide table. She kept writing.

*Spatial relationship between specimens suggests intentional pairing. The 11 cm separation is too precise for random deposition. Comparable burial contexts in the Chesapeake literature—*

She stopped.

She had been going to cite Curry's 1999 analysis of paired grave goods at the Accokeek Creek site, the way objects in Late Woodland burials were sometimes arranged in deliberate geometric relationships, the living placing things with the dead according to a logic that the living understood and the dead would recognize. She knew the literature. She had taught it.

Instead she wrote: *as if the stone was meant to be found with the pendant, not after it.*

She looked at that sentence. It had no place in an archaeological field record. It was not a datum. It was not an observation. It was an interpretation dressed up as a feeling, which was the worst kind of bad science—the kind that felt like insight.

She crossed it out with three firm strokes.

Then she picked up the collection bag and held the stone through the plastic, feeling its shape through the barrier.

Even through the bag, it was not cold.

She set it down and wrote: *Stone retains anomalous warmth relative to ambient soil temperature. Possible explanation: thermal mass of dense greenstone retains heat longer than surrounding clay matrix. Requires measurement.*

She had a small infrared thermometer in the equipment case. She measured the soil at the base of the trench: 4.2 degrees Celsius. She measured the air: 3.8 degrees. She pointed the thermometer at the stone through the plastic.

The reading was 8.1 degrees.

She wrote it down. She did not write what she thought about it.

The morning passed in the way field mornings passed when the work was going well—time moving differently inside the trench than outside it, the world narrowing to the square meter in front of her, her brush and trowel making their small careful sounds against the clay. She was working the soil immediately around the two hollows now, looking for anything else that might have been buried in deliberate relationship to the pendant and the stone. The clay was dense and undisturbed, and she worked slowly, removing thin horizontal slices and screening each one through the quarter-inch mesh.

She found a scatter of charcoal flecks at 63 centimeters below datum—not a hearth, too diffuse for that, more like the residue of something burned nearby and carried here in the fill. She bagged a sample for radiocarbon dating. She found a fragment of cord-marked pottery at 65 centimeters, its exterior rough under her fingertips, the cord impressions still crisp after two thousand years, the clay itself a reddish-brown that told her it had been fired in an open fire rather than a kiln. She measured it, photographed it, bagged it.

She found nothing else that seemed to belong to the burial.

She sat back on her heels and looked at the two hollows.

A pendant. A stone. Placed together, intentionally, at the deepest level of the mound. In the Chesapeake burial literature, paired objects usually meant something about relationship—between the living and the dead, between the person buried and the person who buried them, between this world and the next. The pendant she understood, or thought she did: copper from the Great Lakes, worked into a shape meant to be worn, meant to be seen, meant to carry something of the person who made it. But the stone was harder. River stones appeared in burial contexts throughout the Woodland period, sometimes as tools, sometimes as nothing identifiable—just a stone someone had picked up and carried and placed with the dead for reasons that hadn't survived.

Except this stone had been carried from the river. The Potomac was three hundred meters away. Someone had walked to the water, chosen this particular stone from among all the stones the river had made, and brought it back to this mound and placed it eleven centimeters from the copper pendant.

The river was still loud. She had stopped noticing it for a while, the way you stopped noticing a sound that didn't change, but now she noticed it again.

She opened her notebook to a fresh page and began to write her morning observations in order—artifacts first, then context, then interpretive notes clearly labeled as such. She wrote about the charcoal scatter and the pottery fragment. She wrote about the soil matrix and its color and texture. She wrote about the absence of any other objects in the immediate burial context.

Then she wrote: *The Potomac seems louder this morning.*

She stared at that line for a moment, then crossed it out.

She wrote it again on the next line.

She left it there and kept writing.

*The stone fits the hand in a way that is difficult to account for by fluvial process alone. River abrasion rounds and smooths, but it does not shape for grip. The slight flattening on the lower face—* she paused, reached for the collection bag, held the stone through the plastic, feeling the flat place with her thumb. *—sits naturally against the thenar eminence. The weight distribution across the palm appears to be unusually even. This is consistent with selection rather than random deposition. Someone chose this stone because of how it felt in the hand.*

She read that back. Selection of river stones for hand-feel was documented in the archaeological literature—ground stone tools, gaming pieces, objects of uncertain function that showed evidence of prolonged handling. She wasn't making anything up. She was describing what she observed.

She wrote: *as if shaped for human hands.*

She did not cross it out.

---

By midmorning the light had shifted, the sun angling low over the trench's south wall and throwing a long shadow across the burial context. She was photographing the hollows again—raking light revealing surface textures that direct overhead light flattened out—when she heard the river change.

Not louder, exactly. More present. The way a voice in the next room becomes comprehensible when you stop talking yourself and simply listen.

She lowered the camera.

The sound was the same sound it had been all morning. Water moving over a soft bottom, the slight chop of wind on an open surface, the distant call of something—a goose, she thought, or a heron—from the far bank. Nothing had changed. The river was doing what the river did.

She was the one who had changed.

She looked at the collection bag on the trench floor beside her. The stone inside it was dark and still and patient.

She picked up her notebook and wrote quickly, the way she wrote when she was trying to get something down before she could stop herself: *The stone was not lost here. It was left here. The distinction matters. Things that are lost have no intention. Things that are left—*

She stopped. Read it back. Crossed out everything after *left here.*

Wrote it again.

She capped her pen. She looked at the notebook page—the careful documentation at the top, the crossed-out lines below it, the lines written again, the crossed-out lines below those. It looked like a palimpsest. It looked like a record of someone arguing with herself and losing.

She took a photograph of the page. Evidence of process, she told herself. Methodological transparency.

Then she closed the notebook, picked up the collection bag, and climbed out of the trench. She stood in the thin February sun and looked south toward the river she couldn't see through the trees. The heron was still there—or another one, she couldn't tell—a gray shape at the water's edge, not moving.

The stone was 8.1 degrees in a 4.2-degree world.

She had a number. She had a measurement. She had a spatial relationship documented to the centimeter and a pottery fragment bagged and labeled and a charcoal sample that would give her a date within a generation or two. She had everything she needed to write a solid site report.

She also had a page of field notes that kept writing itself against her will, that kept insisting on words like *waiting* and *chosen*, words that had no business in a scientific record, words that felt less like her own thoughts than like something the stone was pressing into her palm through the plastic.

She put the collection bag in the equipment case and latched it.

The river sounded like it was trying to tell her something she already knew.

She should have left the stone in the equipment case.

She knew that. She had latched it herself, walked back to the trench, spent forty minutes photographing the burial context at raking angles, eaten half a granola bar, drunk cold coffee from her thermos. She had been professional and methodical and entirely in control of herself.

Then she had opened the case again.

She told herself it was to check the bag's seal. The temperature was dropping and condensation was a legitimate concern with organic material in the same case. She told herself this while her hand bypassed the pottery bag and the charcoal sample and went directly to the smooth dark stone.

She pulled it out. Still sealed in its evidence bag. She held it in her left hand, the flat place settling against the base of her thumb the way it always did, the weight distributing itself across her palm with that unsettling evenness she had already documented and could not fully explain.

With her right hand, she reached for the pendant.

She had not planned to do this. She was certain she had not planned it. The copper fragments were in a separate bag, in a separate compartment, and she had to unfasten two clasps to get to it. She did all of this one-handed, the stone held against her chest while she worked the clasps, and she did not ask herself why.

The pendant bag was heavier than the stone's. The copper had oxidized to a deep verdigris over two thousand years, the green of old bronze, the green of river water in shadow. Through the plastic she could feel the irregular edges of the largest fragment, the slight ridge where the original surface had been worked smooth by a stone hammer, the ghost of a curve that had once been the pendant's lower arc.

She held both bags, one in each hand.

Nothing happened.

She stood in the February trench with cold mud on her knees and the sun at a low angle and the river pushing its voice through the trees, and nothing happened, and she felt the particular embarrassment of a scientist who has allowed herself to expect something she cannot justify expecting.

Then the river got louder.

Not gradually. Between one breath and the next, the sound of the Potomac went from background to foreground, from ambient to immediate, the way a word you've been trying to remember suddenly arrives whole and fully formed. She heard it moving. Not the polite February murmur of a cold river at low water, but something fuller, warmer, a sound with weight and direction and urgency.

She looked down at her hands.

The bags were still there. The stone and the copper fragments, sealed in their labeled evidence bags, cool and ordinary in the February air. But the air itself had changed. The light had changed. The shadows in the trench were wrong—too long, angled from the east rather than the south, as if the sun had moved backward.

She tried to set the bags down.

Her hands would not open.

---

The cold left first.

Not warmth replacing it—more like the concept of February dissolving, the body's memory of cold becoming irrelevant. The trench walls were gone. The equipment case was gone. The granola bar wrapper she had folded and put in her jacket pocket was gone. She was standing—still standing, she was sure of that, she could feel the ground under her feet—but the ground was different. Softer. The particular give of riverbank mud in a season that was not February, mud that had been rained on recently and was now drying in actual warmth.

She smelled the river before she saw it.

Not the February Potomac, cold and mineral and faintly industrial even out here in Charles County. This was something else. Green and heavy and alive, the smell of water that had never been anything but water, carrying the oils of fish and the tannins of fallen leaves and the mud of an undisturbed bank. The smell of a river that was also a food source, a road, a boundary, a god.

She saw it through the trees.

The trees themselves were wrong—right species, wrong scale. The oaks were enormous, their trunks wider than she could have reached around, their bark deeply furrowed and gray, their canopy bare now in what she was beginning to understand was early spring. Hickory and tulip poplar between them, and below them a forest floor of last year's leaves, uninterrupted, no trail, no cleared ground, no sign of anything that had been managed by the kind of management she knew.

The river was wide and clear and cold-looking in the way that clear rivers look cold even in warmth, and it was full of sound. Not just water. The sound of something moving through water in numbers, a thrashing, silver and rhythmic, the kind of sound that meant the spring run had come.

Shad. She knew it without knowing how she knew it. American shad crowding upriver, their silver sides catching the light, the river thick with them for the three weeks of the run that made this the most important three weeks of the year. The sound was of ten thousand fish and the water displaced by ten thousand fish and the birds that had come for the fish—herons working the shallows, ospreys hanging overhead and then dropping, the distant percussion of a kingfisher working the far bank.

She was not in the trench. She was not in 2019. She was not anywhere she could name.

She was standing in the forest above the Potomac in a spring that was two thousand years old, and she was not alone.

---

The young woman was waist-deep in the current twenty meters downstream, and Tayak knew immediately that she had been there for a long time.

She stood with her arms raised, palms up, face tilted toward the sky, and her stillness was the stillness of someone who has moved past exhaustion into something beyond it—not rest, not collapse, but a kind of terrible clarity that the body achieves only when it has run out of everything else. Her hair was loose and flowing with the current, black and long, spreading on the surface of the water around her like a second river moving inside the first. Her face was painted: two lines of red ochre from her cheekbones to her chin, a third across her forehead, the color of dried blood or old clay, the color of the earth opened up.

She was young. Not a girl, but young—and in the third day of something.

Tayak felt it.

That was the wrong word. She did not feel it the way she felt cold or warmth or the weight of the stone in her hand. She felt it the way you feel a memory that is not yours—a knowledge that arrives already shaped, already true, bypassing the process of learning. Three days. No food. The water was cold enough to be painful but the young woman had stopped feeling it as pain some hours ago. What she felt now was the river as a presence, not a temperature. The current pressing against her thighs was not water pushing against flesh. It was something speaking against flesh, something that had been trying to tell her something for three days and was finally getting through.

Tayak felt the hunger.

It hit her like a fist—not her own hunger, she had eaten half a granola bar forty minutes ago, she was fine—but a hunger that was two thousand years old and immediate and physical, a hollow that went from throat to belly and made the edges of her vision go slightly bright. She swayed. Caught herself. The river sound was very loud.

The young woman's arms did not lower.

*Speaks with Rivers.* The name arrived the same way the hunger had, already true, requiring no introduction. This was the woman who would find the pendant. This was the woman who had been sent to the river by something she could not have named in words Tayak would recognize, sent here to fast and stand and listen until the river gave her what she had come for.

Tayak watched her and felt her exhaustion and felt her hunger and felt, underneath both of those, the thing that kept her arms raised when every muscle in her body was screaming to lower them: a certainty. Not faith in the sense of belief without evidence. Evidence was everywhere. The river was full of evidence. The shad run was evidence. The osprey dropping from thirty feet and hitting the water and rising with a fish was evidence. The forest behind her, managed and productive and known down to the last hickory tree, was evidence. Everything that kept her people alive was evidence that the world was in conversation with them, that attention was reciprocated, that the river gave what the river was asked to give if you came to it correctly and waited long enough and were willing to pay the price of the waiting.

She had been paying it for three days.

Tayak felt the cold water against her own thighs. She looked down. Her jeans were there, her field boots—but the riverbank was two thousand years old and the water that was cold against her thighs was the same water that was cold against Speaks with Rivers' thighs, the same river, the same cold, the same spring, separated by two millennia and no distance at all.

She took a step toward the water.

The mud of the bank was soft and dark and smelled of tannin and decay and new growth all at once—the smell of a riverbank that had been flooding and receding for ten thousand years, building itself up from the silt of the watershed, giving itself back every spring. Her boot sank to the ankle. She pulled it free with a sucking sound and the mud closed behind it as if she had never been there.

She stopped at the water's edge.

Downstream, Speaks with Rivers had not moved. Her arms were still raised. Her face was still tilted to the sky. But something had changed in the quality of her stillness—a listening that had shifted from waiting to receiving, as if the thing she had come for had finally begun to arrive.

The shad moved around her. Hundreds of them, silver and urgent, crowding upriver, parting around her legs and rejoining behind her. They had been running this river since before there were humans to stand in it. They would run it long after. The river did not belong to anyone who stood in it. The river belonged to itself and lent itself to those who understood the terms of the loan.

Speaks with Rivers understood the terms.

Tayak felt that understanding the way she felt the hunger—as a knowledge that arrived already shaped. She felt the three days of cold and fasting not as suffering but as payment, and she felt the thing that the payment was buying: a permeability. The membrane between herself and the river, between herself and the forest, between herself and the accumulated knowledge of everyone who had stood in this water before her, had been wearing thin for three days and was now almost gone.

Her foot struck something on the riverbed.

Tayak felt it in her own foot.

Hard. Irregular. Not a stone—she knew the feel of stones, smooth and passive and indifferent. This was something that had been worked by human hands, something that had a shape that was not the shape of the river's making. Her toes found the edge of it, the curve of it, and her body knew what it was before her mind did, and the knowledge arrived like the hunger had, like the name had, like everything in this place arrived: already true, requiring nothing but recognition.

Speaks with Rivers lowered her arms.

She looked down at the water. She could not see through it—the shad had churned the bottom, the silt was up—but she did not need to see. She reached down with both hands, plunging them into the cold current, and her fingers found the object on the riverbed and closed around it.

Tayak felt the copper against her own palms.

The verdigris rough under the fingers, the shape of it—a pendant, curved, the size of a palm, with a hole at the top where a cord had once run. The weight of it. The particular weight of copper, heavier than stone of the same size, heavier than it had any right to be, the weight of something that had been made with intention and buried with intention and lost and now found.

Speaks with Rivers straightened. She held the pendant above the water. The sun caught it—greenish, corroded, but still copper underneath the patina, still warm-toned in the spring light, still the color of something that had come from a long way away and carried the distance with it.

She spoke. Tayak heard the words without understanding them, the syllables of a language she did not speak but that her body recognized the rhythm of, the way you recognize a song in a language you don't know because the music is older than the words.

The river ran around her.

The shad ran around her.

The osprey circled overhead.

Speaks with Rivers held the pendant and the pendant held the prayers of the woman who had made it, and those prayers were now in Speaks with Rivers' hands, and Tayak felt them in her own hands, the copper warm against her palms through the evidence bag, warm in a way that copper sealed in plastic and held in a February trench had no business being warm.

She was crying.

She did not know when she had started. She was standing at the edge of the two-thousand-year-old Potomac with tears running down her face and the sound of the shad run all around her and the smell of the river—green, heavy, alive—filling her lungs with every breath, and she could not have said whether the tears were hers or Speaks with Rivers' or whether that distinction meant anything at all in a place where the membrane had worn this thin.

Speaks with Rivers turned.

For a moment—just a moment—she looked directly at Tayak.

Her eyes were dark and steady and very tired and very awake, and she looked at Tayak the way you look at someone you have been expecting for a long time, someone whose arrival you have been waiting for through three days of cold water and hunger and the slow dissolution of everything that separates one person from another.

She did not look surprised.

She looked like someone who had found what she came for.

---

She turned toward the village.

No ceremony in the turning, no pause to gather herself. She simply turned, the pendant in her fist, the river at her back, and began to walk.

The bank was steep here, slick with clay the color of old blood, and she climbed it on her hands and knees because three days of fasting had taken the strength from her legs. The clay squeaked under her palms and left red-brown stains on her fingers that the river had not washed away. She noticed this without caring about it. The pendant was in her right hand and she did not put it down, not even to climb, so she climbed with one fist and her knees and the toes of her feet finding purchase in the clay, and when she reached the top she stood and looked at the forest and began to walk through it.

Tayak walked with her. Or rather Tayak was her, or near enough that the distinction had stopped mattering. She felt the clay drying on her palms. She felt the cold of the river still in her legs, the muscles stiff and reluctant, and she felt the hunger—three days of it, a hollow that had moved past pain into something quieter and more permanent, the way a wound stops hurting when it goes deep enough. She felt the pendant's weight in her fist. She felt, underneath all of it, the thing that the pendant had given her when her fingers closed around it in the riverbed.

The flood of it had lasted only a moment—a cascade of faces and firelight and hands working copper, hands working clay, hands braiding cord from dogbane fiber, hands holding infants, hands releasing the dead into the earth. A chain of women stretching back behind her into a darkness she could not see the end of, and forward in front of her into a brightness she could not see the end of either, and she was somewhere in the middle of it, one link among many, and the chain was not broken. That was the thing the pendant had told her. The chain was not broken.

She had not known she was afraid it was broken until the pendant told her it wasn't.

The forest was loud with birds. Spring had filled every branch, every thicket of young laurel, every stand of river birch along the bank, and the sound was layered and constant and indifferent to her—wrens and thrushes and the liquid pouring call of a wood thrush somewhere deep in the oaks, and underneath it all the river, receding behind her, its voice dropping as she moved inland. The ground was soft with leaf mold and last year's mast. Her feet sank slightly with each step and the smell rose around her: damp earth and old leaves and the faint sweetness of something rotting at the base of a tulip poplar that had come down in winter. Life doing what it always did. Eating itself to make more of itself.

She walked for a long time. The village announced itself before she could see it—the smell of woodsmoke, the sound of a wooden mortar working, a child's voice cut off by an adult's, the crunching grit of shell underfoot where the middens had been worked into the ground over generations. White shell fragments caught the afternoon light between the trees, crushed oyster and mussel, the accumulated meals of her people laid down year by year until the earth here was different from the earth anywhere else, mixed with lime and salt and the slow mineral record of everything they had eaten and survived.

She walked into the village with clay on her hands and river water still in her hair and the copper pendant held at her side, not displayed, not hidden. Just carried.

The first person to see her was an old woman sitting outside a bark-covered house, working a length of dogbane cord between her palms—the inner fiber stripped and twisted against her thigh, the half-finished cord coiling in her lap. The woman looked up. She looked at Speaks with Rivers' face and then at the pendant and then back at her face, and she set the cordage down and did not pick it up again.

Word moved the way word always moved in a small village—faster than a person could walk, arriving at the center before the person did. By the time Speaks with Rivers reached the open ground in the middle of the houses, three elders were already there, standing together in the slant of afternoon light, and more people were coming from the houses around the edge, drawn by the particular quality of the silence that had preceded her.

The eldest of the three was a woman whose hair had gone entirely white, worn loose across her shoulders in the way that marked her standing, and whose face was a map of every winter she had survived. She looked at the pendant.

"Where," she said.

"The sacred pool." Speaks with Rivers' voice came out rough from three days of silence and cold. "Below the confluence. My foot found it in the silt."

The old woman was quiet for a moment. Around them the village had gone still—the mortar had stopped working, the child's voice had stopped, even the dogs had settled. Only the fire at the center of the open ground continued its business, sending a thin column of smoke straight up into the windless afternoon.

"Show me."

Speaks with Rivers opened her hand.

The pendant lay on her palm, green with age, the copper beneath the patina still warm-toned where the light caught the edges. The cord hole at the top was empty. The shape of it—curved, the size of a palm, worked with a deliberateness visible even under two generations of river silt—was not the shape of anything made recently. It was older than anyone here. Older than anyone's grandmother's grandmother.

The old woman did not touch it. She looked at it for a long time.

"I know this," she said finally. "My grandmother's grandmother spoke of it. A pendant carried in the time of the mound builders, lost in a crossing." She raised her eyes to Speaks with Rivers' face. "You were fasting."

"Three days."

"And the river gave it to you."

"My foot found it. The river put it where my foot would find it."

The old woman considered this. She had the stillness of someone who had learned long ago that the space between a question and its answer was not empty. Around them, the people waited. The fire sent its smoke upward. A heron called from somewhere near the water, its voice harsh and carrying.

"What did it show you?" the old woman said.

Speaks with Rivers had been thinking about how to say this since she climbed the bank. She had not found a way that did not sound like too much.

"The mothers," she said. "All of them. Back to the beginning. Forward to—" She stopped. "Forward a long way."

"Did you see their faces?"

"Some. Others I only felt. Like knowing someone is in a house even when you can't see them."

The old woman nodded slowly. "And you understood what it is asking of you."

It was not a question. Speaks with Rivers answered it anyway.

"I am to keep the stories. Every mother in the chain. Every name, every ceremony, every thing that must not be forgotten. I am to hold them until there is someone to give them to."

"And if no one comes?"

"Someone will come." She said it without hesitation, and the certainty of it surprised her even as she heard herself say it. It was the same kind of certainty as the hunger—not argued into, just present. "The pendant was in the river for fifty years and it waited. The stories will wait longer if they have to."

The old woman looked at her for a long moment. Then she turned to the two elders beside her and spoke to them in a low voice, and they spoke back, and Speaks with Rivers stood with the pendant in her open hand and waited, because this was not hers to hurry.

When the old woman turned back, her face had settled into something formal.

"You are named," she said. "Memory keeper. The stories come to you now and they go from you only when you find the one who is ready to receive them. This is not an honor." She said it plainly, without apology. "It is a weight. Do you take it?"

Speaks with Rivers looked down at the pendant. The copper caught the light—greenish, ancient, the color of something that had come a long way and survived the distance. She thought of the faces she had seen. The chain of them. She thought of the ones she had not seen yet, the ones forward in the brightness she couldn't look directly at.

"I take it," she said.

---

She spent what remained of the afternoon eating—carefully, a little at a time, the way you eat after a fast, small amounts of corn porridge that one of the women brought her, warm and slightly sweet with dried squash stirred in. She ate sitting outside because the smoke inside the house made her eyes water after three days of clean air, and while she ate she thought about the river stone.

She had carried it in the pouch at her hip since the first morning of the fast, when she had found it on the bank—smooth, dark, the size of a fist, worn to silk by the river over more years than she could count. She had known when she picked it up that it mattered, though she had not known how. Now she knew.

The stone had brought her to the pool. Not by any logic she could have explained, but by the same logic that had brought the shad upriver and the osprey to its perch and her foot to the exact spot on the riverbed where the pendant lay. The stone was part of it. It belonged with the pendant the way a path belongs with the place it leads to.

She took both objects out when the light was going. She held them together in her palms—the stone cool and smooth, the pendant rough with patina, heavier than the stone though smaller. Together they filled her two hands exactly.

She had been given a medicine bundle to hold them in—bark-sewn, lined with deer hide, the stitching done by the old woman herself, which meant something. She set the stone in first, then the pendant on top of it, and then she sat for a while with the bundle open in her lap, looking at what was inside, because she understood that once she closed it the arrangement would be permanent. The stone and the pendant would be together from now until whoever came to find them opened the bundle again, and she did not know how long that would be, and she wanted to look at them while she still could.

The fire had burned low. The village had quieted around her. Somewhere a dog was moving through the shell-crusted ground at the edge of the houses, its paws crunching softly, and the river was audible again now that everything else had stilled, its voice low and constant from the direction of the trees.

She closed the bundle.

Then she said the words. She said them quietly, not performing them for anyone, because there was no one to perform them for—the people had gone inside, the elders had gone inside, this moment was between her and whoever was going to receive it.

"I do not know your name," she said. "I do not know your face. I do not know how many seasons will pass between my hands and yours, or what the world will look like when you come. I know that you will come. I know that the chain is not broken. I know that when you find this"—she pressed her palms flat against the closed bundle—"you will already know what it is, the way I knew what it was when my foot found it. You will not need me to explain it. You will only need to receive it."

She stopped. The river ran. The fire settled with a soft collapse of ash.

"I am Speaks with Rivers," she said. "I am the one who found it and kept it for you. Remember me when you open this. That is all I ask."

---

The mound was a low rise above the river, tree-covered, older than anyone in the village by a long way. The earth of it had a different quality from the earth around it—denser, darker, the accumulated intention of the people who had built it and buried their dead in it and continued to tend it even after the building had stopped. You could feel the difference underfoot when you walked onto it, the way you can feel the difference between a room where nothing has ever happened and a room where everything has.

She dug with a pointed stick and her hands. The earth came up in dark clumps, smelling of roots and old rain and the mineral cold of depth. She dug until the hole was deep enough that a frost would not reach it, deep enough that a flood would not wash it out, deep enough that it would take intention to find it rather than accident.

She placed the bundle in the hole.

She covered it.

She smoothed the earth over it with her palms until the surface was even, and then she pressed both hands flat against it and held them there for a moment, feeling the cold come up through the soil, feeling the weight of what was below.

Then she stood and walked away without looking back, because looking back was not part of it.

---

Tayak came back to herself the way you surface from deep water—gradually, the light changing, the sounds of the present world reassembling themselves around her. The scrape of her trowel against the trench wall. The February cold in her fingers. The smell of turned earth and the distant, barely-there scent of the river carried on a wind she couldn't feel down in the trench.

She was kneeling in the dirt with both hands pressed flat against the earth at the bottom of the excavation, exactly the way Speaks with Rivers had pressed her hands against the mound.

She did not move for a long time.

The stone was in its evidence bag beside her knee. The pendant was in its bag beside that. Together they took up less space than a paperback book. Together they had been here for two thousand years, waiting with the patience of things that understand that the right person will eventually arrive, and that arrival is not something you can hurry.

She thought about the words she had heard—*I do not know your name. I do not know your face.*

She thought about Speaks with Rivers' eyes when she had turned in the river and looked directly at her. The look of someone who had found what she came for.

She picked up the evidence bags, one in each hand, and held them until the cold of the February air had taken whatever warmth they carried.

Then she set them in the transport case and closed the lid, and she understood, with the particular clarity of something that has been true for a long time and has only just been said aloud, that she had not chosen this excavation. It had chosen her. The stone and the pendant had been waiting in the earth since Speaks with Rivers placed them there, and they had been waiting specifically—not for any archaeologist, not for any scholar, but for the one who would come already knowing what they were.

She had known. From the first morning, before she understood why, she had known.

She latched the case. Above her, the February sky was the color of old pewter, and the bare trees along the ridge stood in it like the ribs of something enormous and patient, and somewhere below the ridge the Potomac ran toward the bay, carrying the spring thaw in its current, indifferent and eternal and lending itself to those who understood the terms.

She understood the terms now.

She picked up the case and climbed out of the trench, and as she walked to her truck she was already thinking about the testimony she would give—not just the carbon dates, not just the stratigraphic analysis, not just the academic argument for continuous habitation—but the other thing, the thing Speaks with Rivers had given her: the knowledge that the chain was not broken, had never been broken, and that she was standing at the end of it, holding it in both hands, and it was her turn now to decide what she did with what she carried.

---

She didn't go straight to the truck.

The river was below the ridge, maybe two hundred yards through bare February scrub—hawthorn and young tulip poplar, their trunks pale as bone in the afternoon light. She had walked that path a dozen times since the excavation began, always with a purpose: to check the flood gauge she'd set on the bank, to eat lunch away from the site, to think. She walked it now with the transport case in one hand and no purpose she could name, only the pull of the water, which had been there since morning and which she was done resisting.

The bank was a narrow shelf of frozen mud above the current. The Potomac ran low and dark, the February drawdown exposing a strip of pale sand below the shelf's edge, and the far shore was a smear of gray tree-line against a sky that had been deciding whether to snow all day and had not yet made up its mind. The river smelled of cold iron and wet clay and something faintly vegetable—the upstream thaw sending its first signals ahead of itself, the way a season announces its intention before it arrives.

She set the transport case on a flat stone above the waterline and stood for a moment, looking at the current. The water moved with the particular indifference of things that have been doing this long before anyone was watching and will continue long after. It did not care that she was standing here. It did not care about the testimony she would give, or the committee, or the carbon dates, or the two-thousand-year-old stone in the case behind her. It ran south toward the bay, carrying what it carried, and that was all.

She crouched at the edge and put her right hand in.

The cold was immediate and total, the kind that skips past discomfort and goes straight to fact: *this is cold, this is real, this is the present tense.* The current pushed against her palm. She could feel the river bottom through the water, sand and gravel shifting in the pull, and she thought of Speaks with Rivers standing waist-deep in this same current, her foot striking copper in the dark, the shock of it traveling up through her body like something struck on an anvil.

She held her hand in the water until she could not feel her fingers. Then she pulled it out and pressed it wet against her knee and looked at the far shore.

"I'm Tayak Moyaone," she said.

Her voice came out steadier than she expected. The river took no notice.

"I'm a Piscataway woman and I'm an archaeologist, and I know those two things are supposed to create a problem, but I don't think they do anymore." She paused. A great blue heron lifted from the shallows forty yards downstream, its wings making no sound she could hear over the current, its long neck folded into the gray air. "I found what you left. Both of you. I understand what you were doing with it."

The heron was gone. The river ran.

"I won't own it," she said. "I know I can't own it. I know what it is." She looked down at the case on the flat stone. "I'll carry it. That's what I'm saying. I'll carry it the way you carried it, and when it's time to put it back in the ground, I'll know."

She was quiet for a moment, listening to the current, to the bare branches clicking together somewhere above the bank. The Potomac in February was not the Potomac of Speaks with Rivers' vision—not the clear, fish-crowded water of the old runs, the spring shad crowding the shallows, the brush weirs straining silver with the catch. This water was darker, slower, carrying the weight of everything that had happened to the watershed in the centuries between. But it was the same river. The same channel, the same pull toward the same bay. The bones of it were unchanged.

"I'll remember you," she said. "Both of you. All of you."

She stood, her knee joints cracking in the cold, and picked up the case. She held it with both hands against her chest for a moment, the way you hold something you are about to give back, and then she settled the strap over her shoulder and turned toward the path.

---

The walk back up through the scrub took five minutes. The light was going faster now, the pewter sky darkening toward the tree-line in the west, and she moved quickly, her boots breaking through the thin skin of ice that had formed over the mud since midday. The hawthorn caught at her sleeve once, a brief, dry grip, and she pulled free without stopping.

Her field notebook was on the tailgate where she'd left it that morning, held open against the wind by the weight of her hand lens. She picked up the lens and let the notebook fall shut—and then stopped.

It hadn't fallen shut. The pages had riffled in the wind and settled open again, to a page near the back that she had not reached yet in the day's work.

She looked at it for a long moment without touching it.

The page was in her own handwriting. She recognized the particular slant of her capital letters, the way she formed her sevens with a European crossbar from the semester she'd spent in Edinburgh. She recognized the pressure of the pen—she pressed harder when she was writing fast, when she was trying to keep up with a thought before it moved on. This had been written fast.

At the top of the page, underlined twice:

*Speaks with Rivers*

Below it, a date she did not remember writing: *50 BC (approx.)*

Below that, three lines of field notes describing the stone's position relative to the pendant, the intentional pairing, the evidence of deliberate burial—notes she had no memory of writing, but which were accurate, which matched exactly what she had documented in her conscious notes that morning.

And below those lines, separated from them by a gap, a single question in the same fast hand:

*Who buried the stone the second time?*

She read it twice. She read it a third time.

The stone had been buried with the pendant. Speaks with Rivers had buried it herself, in the mound, in the bundle—she had watched her do it, had felt the cold of the earth coming up through the ancestor's palms as she smoothed the surface flat. The burial was deliberate. The pairing was deliberate. Speaks with Rivers had placed both objects in the ground and walked away without looking back.

But the mound was old. Older than Speaks with Rivers. Red Fire Woman had built it, or helped build it, or been placed in it—the stratigraphy was clear on that, the copper pendant at the deepest level, the stone in the same layer.

She stood very still.

The stone was in the same layer as the pendant. Below the level where Speaks with Rivers had dug her hole and placed the bundle. Which meant Speaks with Rivers had placed the bundle *above* the original deposition—she had buried the pendant again, yes, but she had not buried it at the original depth. The stone at sixty-one centimeters below datum, the pendant at sixty centimeters: they were together now, they had been together when she found them, but they had not always been together.

Someone had placed the stone in the mound before Red Fire Woman. Before the pendant. Before any of it.

Someone had been here first, and had left the stone, and had left it at a depth that the pendant would later be placed above—as if whoever came after would know to look for it, would know to set the copper down in that exact relationship to what was already there.

Eleven centimeters. The distance wasn't random. It had never been random.

She closed the notebook carefully and set it on the seat. She put the transport case beside it. She got in and sat for a moment with her hands on the wheel, not starting the engine, looking at the ridge line above the excavation site where the bare trees stood in the last light.

The river was still down there, running south. She could not hear it from here, but she knew it was there, the way you know certain things—not because you can verify them in this moment, but because they have been true long enough to stop requiring proof.

She started the engine and pulled out onto the county road, heading north, the transport case on the seat beside her, the notebook on top of it, open to the question she had written herself and could not yet answer.
