# 11 — Chapter 46: The Pendant Passes — the life of the pendant's owner

---

The fire had burned low by the time Ahanu reached for the cord.

Waya saw it happen the way you see a thing you have been dreading — not as a surprise, but as a confirmation. Ahanu's fingers, which had once stripped a deer hide in the time it took Waya to sharpen a bone awl, now moved with the careful deliberateness of someone who could not afford to drop what she was holding. She worked the knot at the back of her neck for a long moment. The braided plant-fiber cord was dark with years of body oil and smoke, nearly black where it had lain against her skin.

Waya did not help her. She sat with her knees drawn up on the sleeping mat and watched the shadows move on the wattle-and-daub wall and told herself: *she is only showing it to me. She does this sometimes. She will put it back.*

The longhouse held the smell of the evening meal — goosefoot porridge thickened with hickory paste, still warm in the ceramic pot near the coals. Outside, the last light was going out of the sky above the tidal river, and Waya could hear her children's voices somewhere beyond the door-hide, her daughter's high and insistent, her son's lower, beginning to change. She had told herself she would go to them when the meal was done. She was still here.

Ahanu lifted the pendant free.

It came away from her chest and caught the firelight — a crescent of cold-hammered copper, no wider than a woman's palm, its surface polished to a reddish-gold that made it look, in this light, as though it were lit from inside. The edges had gone slightly greenish in the creases where sweat and smoke had worked at it over decades, but the face of it still shone. Waya had known this object her entire life. She had never once held it.

"Come here," Ahanu said.

Waya's throat tightened. She did not move.

"Waya."

She crossed the three steps between them and sat. Up close, she could see what the firelight had been merciful enough to hide from across the room: the tremor in Ahanu's hands was not the cold. The skin across her knuckles had gone thin and papery, the kind of thin that happened when a body began to use itself up from inside. Ahanu's eyes were clear — they had always been clear, sharp as a hawk's — but the rest of her face had a hollowness to it that had not been there at the last new moon. Or perhaps it had, and Waya had not let herself see it.

Ahanu held the pendant out.

"I'm not ready," Waya said.

"I know." Ahanu's voice was dry, unhurried. "Take it anyway."

Waya took it. The copper was cool against her palm, heavier than she expected, though she had watched it hang at Ahanu's throat for years. She turned it over. The back was rougher than the front, the hammer-marks still visible where some craftsperson — someone who had lived and died long before anyone in this village was born — had worked the metal flat with patient, repeated blows. There was a small perforation near the top, smooth-edged with long use, where the cord threaded through.

"It came from far away," Ahanu said. She was watching Waya's face the way she used to watch the river in flood season — reading it for information. "You know this."

"I know this."

"A trader gave it to the first woman who held it — a man who came through once and was never seen again. He came from the west. He had other things with him." Ahanu paused. "Mica, cut into shapes. A stone the color of night. He gave her the copper because she gave him shelter for three days and fed his people."

Waya had heard this story. She had heard it the way you hear a story told to you in childhood — present in the memory but worn smooth, the edges gone. Hearing it now, with the pendant in her hand, was different. The weight of it was different.

"She wore it at every ceremony," Ahanu said. "Her daughter wore it. I wore it." She looked at Waya steadily. "You will wear it."

"You can still wear it."

Ahanu made a small sound that was not quite a laugh. "I can still walk to the river," she said. "That does not mean I should carry the water jug."

The fire shifted. A knot of oak wood broke apart and sent a brief flare of light across the room, and for a moment the pendant in Waya's hand blazed — a sharp reddish-gold, brighter than any bone or shell ornament she owned, brighter than anything she could have made herself. She understood, not for the first time but more completely than before, why the trader had carried it so far and why the first woman had kept it. There was nothing else like it. It was the color of the sky at the moment before dawn, when the light was still more promise than fact.

"What did she call it?" Waya asked. "The first woman. Did she have a name for it?"

"She called it the thing from far away." Ahanu's mouth curved. "She was not a poetic woman."

Waya looked at the pendant. Then she looked at Ahanu's hands, resting now in her lap, still.

"Tell me the truth," she said.

Ahanu was quiet for a moment. The porridge pot ticked as it cooled.

"I am not dying tonight," she said at last. "Or tomorrow. But I have been watching my hands for two seasons now, and I know what they are telling me." She met Waya's eyes. "I want you to have it while I can still give it to you. While I can still say the words."

Waya pressed her lips together. She would not cry here, in front of Ahanu, who had never cried in front of her — or not that she had seen, which was not the same thing, but felt like it.

"Say them, then," she said.

Ahanu reached out and closed Waya's fingers around the pendant.

"This came from far away," she said. "It means we are not alone."

The words were plain. They were the same words, Waya knew, that had been spoken over this same object before — a chain of voices going back to a time before the oldest person in the village had been born, back to a trader moving west to east along the river routes, back to wherever the copper had come from before that, somewhere far to the north and west where the great lakes lay. The words were plain and they were very old and they landed in Waya's chest like a stone dropped in still water.

She held the pendant and said nothing.

Outside, her daughter called her name.

---

She did not answer her daughter right away.

The fire had settled back to coals, the brief flare gone, and the pendant in her closed fist was warming now, losing the cool it had held from Ahanu's keeping. Waya could feel the hammer-marks against her palm — the ridged surface where some craftsperson, generations dead, had beaten the raw copper flat. Patient work. Repeated blows. She thought about the hands that had done it and could not picture them.

Her daughter called again. *Nahi.* Mother.

"Go," Ahanu said.

"I'm not finished."

"You are, for tonight." Ahanu's voice had the quality it always had at the end of long conversations — not cold, but closed. The thing she had needed to say had been said. "Go to her."

Waya stood. Her knees ached from sitting on the packed-earth floor, and she was aware, as she had not been an hour ago, of every year in her own body. She was thirty-four. Ahanu was — she tried to count and found she did not know exactly, which seemed impossible and was not. Old enough. Old enough for the hands to tell their story.

She threaded the pendant onto its cord — sinew, dark with smoke and body oil, supple from long use — and looped it over her head. The copper settled against her sternum, cool again where it left her hand and met the air.

She looked at Ahanu.

Ahanu was watching her with the hawk's-eye clarity that had not yet dimmed, would not dim, Waya suspected, until the very end. She was sitting straight despite everything. Her spine had always been her stubbornness made visible.

"It looks right on you," Ahanu said.

Waya did not trust herself to answer. She ducked through the low doorway and out into the evening.

---

The village was settling toward dark. Cook-fires sent thin columns of smoke up through the trees — oak and hickory, their canopy thick enough that the smoke spread sideways before it rose, so that the whole clearing sat in a low haze that smelled of woodsmoke and the fish-stew someone three houses down was finishing for the night. The river was audible but not visible from here, a low constant sound beneath the insects and the children's voices.

Her daughter, Suki, was standing at the edge of the clearing with two other girls, all three of them watching something in the grass. When she heard Waya's step she looked up.

"There's a frog," she said. "A big one."

"There are many frogs."

"This one is very big."

Waya came and stood beside her. The frog was indeed large — a bullfrog, fat and still, its throat pulsing. It regarded them with ancient, indifferent eyes.

Suki looked at the pendant. She had seen it before, of course — had seen it at Ahanu's throat her whole life — but she had never seen it here, hanging at her mother's neck. Her face did something complicated that Waya recognized as the effort not to ask a question that felt too large.

"Ahanu gave it to you," she said.

"Yes."

"Is she—"

"She is resting." Waya kept her voice level. "She is well enough."

Suki looked back at the frog. She was nine years old, and she had her father's jaw and Waya's eyes, and she had been learning since she was six how to read the sky for weather and the river for fish-runs. She was quick and serious and she asked more questions than any child Waya had known. Right now she was asking none of them, which meant she understood more than Waya had said.

The frog shifted, gathered itself, and leaped into the grass. The three girls watched it go.

"Come in before full dark," Waya said. "Both of you." She nodded at the other girls.

She went back inside.

---

That night she lay awake with her husband sleeping beside her and the pendant still at her throat. She had not taken it off. She was not sure she could.

She thought about the chain Ahanu had described. A trader who had come from the west, from somewhere along the great river routes, carrying mica cut into shapes and a stone the color of night and this copper pendant, reddish-gold and heavier than it looked. He had stayed three days. He had eaten their food and slept under their roof and left this behind as payment, or gift, or something that had no single word. And then he had gone west again, or north, or wherever he had come from, and no one had seen his people since.

*He came from far away,* Ahanu had said. *It means we are not alone.*

She understood the logic of it. The pendant was proof that the world extended beyond what she could see — beyond the river, beyond the tree-line, beyond the edge of everything she knew. Somewhere to the north and west, people had pulled this copper from the earth and hammered it into this shape and set it moving through the world. They had their own fires, their own children calling from the edge of the clearing. They had their own dead.

She pressed her palm against the pendant. The metal had gone fully warm now, body-warm, so that she could only feel it by its weight and its edges.

Three days later, Ahanu died.

---

It was not a hard death, which was the only mercy. She had been eating less for a week, and then she had stopped eating, and on the third morning after she stopped she did not wake. Waya's husband found her just after dawn and came to get Waya before he told anyone else, and Waya sat with Ahanu's body for a long time before she let him go.

The face was peaceful. That was the word people used, and it was true, and it was not enough.

Waya held the pendant in both hands — she had taken it off, she did not know when, sometime in the night — and looked at Ahanu's hands folded on her chest. The thin skin. The knuckles. The hands that had made decisions and led ceremonies and held her grandchildren and, years ago, threaded this same cord through this same perforation and set this same weight against her own sternum.

*This came from far away,* Waya thought. *It means we are not alone.*

She was alone. She knew she would not always be alone. But she was alone now, in this moment, with the pendant in her hands and Ahanu's face going still and cool as copper, and the words were plain and old and true and not enough, and she said them anyway, quietly, to no one, to the room, to the pendant itself.

Then she put it back on.

Then she went to find her daughter.

---

The years did not pass the way water passes, all in one direction. They pooled and eddied. They froze and broke open. Some of them she could not have told apart if she had tried.

But the pendant was always there.

She wore it through the next spring's fish-run, when the river below the village turned silver for three days with herring so thick a child could scoop them in her hands. She wore it through the summer that followed, planting goosefoot and squash in the cleared ground behind the houses, her fingers black with soil, the pendant swinging forward when she bent and tapping against the earth as if testing it. She wore it at the ceremony that fall when old Keshu died and his family brought his body to the mound and laid him in the prepared ground — the clay floor swept clean, the offerings arranged — and covered him over, and sang until the stars moved. She touched the pendant then without thinking. Her hand went to it the way her tongue went to a sore place in her mouth: not to fix anything, only to confirm it was still there.

Her husband noticed. He did not say anything for a long time, which was his way — he was a man who let understanding accumulate before he spoke, like water in a clay pot, filling slowly, no splash. One evening in the second year after Ahanu died, he came and sat beside her at the outdoor hearth where she was grinding dried corn against the stone, the flat scraping sound steady as breathing, and he watched her work for a while. The pendant hung at her throat, dull in the firelight, the copper gone dark except where it caught the flame and briefly flared.

"You touch it when you're afraid," he said.

She kept grinding. "I know."

"You touched it this morning when Suki's cough came back."

"It's nothing. A cold-weather cough."

"I know." He was quiet. "I'm not saying it means something bad, the touching. I'm saying I see it."

Waya lifted the grinding stone and set it down. The corn flour was fine enough. She looked at the pendant. In the low light it was almost black, only a thread of reddish-gold at its highest curve.

"Ahanu did the same," she said. "The one before her. I don't know if it's habit or if it does something."

"Does it matter?"

She thought about it. "No," she said. "I don't think it does."

---

Suki's cough cleared. She grew. She was twelve, then fifteen, then a young woman with her father's jaw set firm and her mother's eyes reading everything they landed on. She had learned to hunt by then — Waya had taught her, as she had taught her to read the sky — and she moved through the forest with a quietness that still surprised Waya, who had watched her learn it step by step and yet could not entirely account for the result. The girl who had stood at the edge of the clearing asking about a frog had become someone else, or the same person extended further into herself.

She watched the pendant. Waya saw her watching.

It was not the watching of a child who wants a thing. It was the watching of someone learning a language, noting where the stress falls, how the mouth shapes the sounds. Every time Waya's hand went to the copper in a moment of decision — at the council fire when the men argued about the winter stores, at the mound when they laid in another body, at the edge of the river when the water ran too high and she was deciding whether to pull the fish-weirs — Suki's eyes would track the gesture. She never commented. She was learning something that had no name yet.

---

Then the drought came.

Not the first drought Waya had known — she had been a child in a dry summer once, remembered the cracked mud at the river's edge and the way the adults' voices changed, tighter, more careful — but this was different. This was three years, one after another, each one worse. The goosefoot withered in the ground. The fish-runs thinned. The deer moved north or west or simply became scarce, and the hunters came back with less, and then with nothing, and then stopped going out in large groups because a large group needed too much food to sustain itself on the trail.

Her youngest child, a boy named Ren, died in the second year. He was four years old. He had been sick before the drought with the kind of sickness that comes and goes, and the drought found him already weakened and did not let go.

Waya did not speak of it to anyone for a long time. She knew the shape of this silence. She knew how to move inside it without letting it show on her face.

But her hand went to the pendant constantly in those months. At the fire, in the night, in the middle of grinding corn or scraping a hide or doing any of the hundred ordinary tasks that continued even when a child was dead and the river was low and the sky gave nothing. Her palm against the copper, which was cool in the morning and warm by midday and body-warm by evening, which was always the same weight, which had been worn by others before her and had outlasted all of them.

*This came from far away.*

She said it to herself on the worst nights. Not as comfort exactly. As fact. The pendant had traveled from somewhere far to the north and west, along the river routes, through hands she would never know, to arrive here at her throat. The people who had hammered this copper from the earth had their own droughts. Their own dead children. Their own nights when the fire burned low and the dark pressed in and the world seemed to be contracting to a single point of grief.

They were not alone. She was not alone.

It was not enough. It was something.

---

Sokan died in the third year of the drought.

He had been failing for two winters — she had watched it, the way his step shortened, the way he lowered himself to sit and then could not always rise without help, the way his voice in ceremony remained full and certain even as his body went quiet around it. Her father. She had known this was coming and had not let herself know it, which was a thing she had learned from watching others and had apparently learned too well.

He died in early spring, before the fish-run, before the worst of the hunger had broken. His body was brought to the mound and laid in with care, with the things that had been his. The carved antler figure — the one he had made for Ahanu as a gesture of reconciliation after the drought's worst year, and that Ahanu had eventually pressed back into his hands — went with him into the earth. So did the small stone pipe he had used at every ceremony Waya could remember. She watched the earth go over him and felt something shift in her chest — not grief exactly, or not only grief, but a change in weight. A redistribution. He had been the one who held the old knowledge, the ceremonies, the names of the names of the names going back past memory. Now she was one of the ones who held it.

She had not asked for this. She had been learning it her whole life, absorbing it the way the pendant absorbed warmth, and she had not understood until this moment that she had been absorbing it on purpose, or that purpose and habit were sometimes the same thing.

Her hand went to the copper.

Suki, standing beside her, saw it.

---

After the drought broke — a wet spring, the river running high and brown and full, the goosefoot coming up thick and unasked-for in the cleared ground — Waya began to take Suki to the ceremonies deliberately. Not just to watch. To stand beside her and learn the words, the order, the reasons behind the order.

"Why do we say that part twice?" Suki asked once, after a midsummer ceremony, walking back through the oak-and-hickory forest in the long evening light, the trees enormous and indifferent above them.

"Because once is for the living and once is for the dead."

"Which is first?"

Waya thought about it. "The dead," she said. "They were here before."

Suki was quiet for a moment, stepping over a root. "The pendant," she said. "When you touch it. What are you doing?"

Waya stopped walking. The forest was loud with insects, the same sound it had always been, the same sound it would be long after them. She looked at her daughter, who was twenty-three now and had her father's jaw and her mother's eyes and something else that was entirely her own — a particular quality of attention, a stillness that was not passivity but readiness.

"I'm remembering," Waya said. "That the world is larger than what I can see."

Suki looked at the pendant. The copper had gone dark over the years, the bright reddish-gold of Waya's first days wearing it now deepened to a brownish-red with green gathering in the crevices, the cord replaced twice but the perforation original, the same hole that had been threaded by hands before Waya's own.

"Ahanu gave it to you," Suki said.

"Yes."

"And before that?"

"A trader," Waya said. "He came from the west. From somewhere along the great river routes, very far. He stayed three days and left this behind and no one saw his people again." She paused. "He came from far away. That's what Ahanu said. It means we are not alone."

Suki looked at her steadily. She was not asking for the pendant. She was asking for the words.

"Say it again," she said.

Waya said it again.

They stood in the forest until the light changed, and then they walked home together through the oak-mast and the fallen hickory leaves, the pendant warm at Waya's throat, the insects loud and steady all around them, the river audible somewhere below, always the river, running toward something neither of them would ever see.

---

She had been putting it off for three days.

Not because she did not know the time had come — she knew. She had known since the last ceremony, watching Suki move through the forms with that particular quality of attention that was not imitation but comprehension, the way a river comprehends its bank, working with it and against it at once. She had known since the morning Suki had corrected her own daughter's grip on the pottery coil without being asked, the same correction Ahanu had made to Waya's hands years ago. She had known, and she had put it off, because once the pendant left her neck it would not come back, and she had worn it for twenty-six years, and she was not ready.

She woke on the fourth morning and understood that not-ready was not a reason. It was just a feeling.

The house was dim and smelled of woodsmoke and the dried goosefoot hanging in bundles from the roof poles. Outside, the river made its low sound through the willows — it was early autumn, the water dropping back from its summer height, the mud banks exposed and dark. Waya sat up on her sleeping mat and felt the pendant shift against her collarbone, the cord worn soft as skin, and she reached up and unlooped it from her neck for the first time in years.

It sat in her palm and she looked at it in the grey light.

The copper had gone the color of old blood where it curved, and the crevices along the hammered edges had gone green, the verdigris worked deep into the metal's surface the way age works into a face — not damage, just time made visible. The shape was a broad crescent, the upper edge straight where the cord threaded through the perforation, the lower edge sweeping in a long arc that caught light differently depending on angle. Someone had made this with cold hammer-work, beating the copper thin and true without fire, and the marks of that work were still there if you looked, the faint ridges running across the surface like the lines on a palm.

She had looked at it so many times she had stopped seeing it. Now she looked again.

*Far away,* she thought. *Very far.*

She called for Suki.

---

Her daughter came in from outside, carrying a clay bowl still warm from the fire, the cord-marked surface rough under her thumb. She was thirty-one now. She had her father's jaw, broad and certain, and she had Waya's eyes, which were the kind of eyes that found the still point in a moving thing. She saw what was in Waya's hand and set the bowl down carefully on the packed earth floor.

"Sit," Waya said.

Suki sat.

Waya held the pendant out across the space between them. The cord dangled, the copper turned, catching a thin bar of morning light that had found its way through the smoke-hole above the hearth. For a moment it flashed — that reddish-gold the metal still held in its heart, beneath the tarnish, the color of a coal just before it goes dark.

"This came from far away," Waya said. "It means we are not alone."

The words were Ahanu's words. Ahanu's before that. She did not know how far back they went — she suspected they went back to the beginning of the pendant itself, to the first hands that had passed it on, though she could not know that and did not claim to.

Suki did not reach for it yet. She looked at it, and then she looked at Waya.

"Who made it?" she said.

Waya had expected the question. She had been asking it herself since she was Suki's age, sitting in this same kind of dim house, watching Ahanu hold out this same piece of copper.

"I don't know," she said.

"What people? What hands?"

"I don't know that either."

Suki's eyes moved back to the pendant. The cord turned slowly in Waya's grip, the copper swinging in a small arc, and Waya let it swing.

"A trader came," Waya said. "From the west. Along the great river routes, very far — Ahanu told me he spoke a language no one here understood well, only a few words of it. He stayed three days. He left this behind, and no one from his people came through again."

"Three days," Suki said.

"Three days."

"And we've carried it since."

"Yes."

Suki was quiet. Outside, a heron called once from the direction of the river — a flat, carrying sound, not musical, just a fact announced — and then was silent. The fire in the hearth had burned to embers and the house was cooling around them, the morning air coming in under the door-frame, smelling of mud and fallen leaves and the particular sharpness of oak-mast beginning to rot.

"It bothers you," Waya said. "Not knowing."

"Doesn't it bother you?"

Waya thought about it honestly. She had learned not to answer this question quickly.

"It used to," she said. "I wanted a name. A people. A face to put to the hands that made it." She looked at the pendant, the green in its crevices, the hammered ridges. "I thought not knowing was a kind of loss. Something missing."

"And now?"

"Now I think it's the point." She let the cord go still. "The people who made this — they had their own dead. Their own bad winters. Their own fires burning low in the dark. They were far away and they were not like us and I will never know their name." She looked at her daughter. "And still the copper came from their hands to mine. Across all that distance. Across everything we don't share." She paused. "That's what it means. Not that we know them. That we don't, and the copper came anyway."

Suki looked at her for a long moment. Then she held out her hand.

Waya placed the pendant in it.

The cord was warm from her palm. The copper was cool — it always ran cool, even in summer, the metal holding the temperature of the air rather than the body, which was part of what made it strange, part of what made it feel like something from outside the ordinary run of things. Suki turned it once, looking at the shape, the arc of it, the perforation worn smooth by decades of cord. Then she looped it over her head and settled it against her collarbone, where it lay in the hollow of her throat and caught the light from the smoke-hole and went still.

Waya watched it settle and felt something she had no word for — not loss, not relief, but the specific sensation of a weight redistributed, the way a load feels different when you shift it from one shoulder to the other, not lighter, just differently carried.

"Say it again," Suki said. "The words."

"I just said them."

"Say them again. So I have them right."

Waya said them again. *This came from far away. It means we are not alone.*

Suki repeated them back, quietly, not performing, just fixing them.

"And when I give it to mine," she said, "I say the same words."

"The same words."

"Even though I won't know any more than you do."

"Especially then," Waya said.

The fire had gone to grey ash. Suki picked up her clay bowl from the floor and stood, and the pendant moved with her, shifting against the deerskin at her throat. She was already wearing it the way Waya had worn it — not as decoration, not as display, but as a thing that had weight and history and demanded a certain quality of attention.

Waya watched her daughter walk to the door and duck through into the morning light, and for a moment the copper caught the sun full-on — a flash of reddish-gold, bright as a coal, bright as something just beginning — and then Suki was gone, and the house was dim and smokesmelling and quiet, and Waya's collarbone was bare.

She sat with that for a while.

Then she thought: *thirty years.* Suki would wear it for thirty years, or twenty, or forty — she would wear it until she was old and her own daughter was ready, and then she would sit in a dim house in the early morning and take it from her neck and hold it out and say the words. And whoever received it would ask the same question Suki had asked — *who made it, what hands, what people* — and would receive the same answer.

*I don't know. That's the point.*

The not-knowing would travel with the copper. It was part of what the copper carried.

Waya reached up and touched her collarbone where the pendant had rested for twenty-six years, the skin there slightly worn smooth, a small hollow she had made without meaning to. The absence had a shape. She pressed her fingers into it and held them there, and outside the river ran on through the willows, and somewhere in the oak-mast a squirrel worked at a nut with quick, certain teeth, and the morning went about its business without her.

She would not get it back. She had not expected to. She had not wanted to — or she had wanted to, and the wanting did not matter, and she had given it anyway.

That was what it meant to pass something on.

---

Suki wore it for thirty-one years.

Waya knew this because she counted. Not obsessively, not every day, but in the way you count the rings of a felled oak — after the fact, running your finger across what time has left. She was sixty-three when Suki fell ill in the cold month, the month when the river ran grey and the geese were already gone south and the bare oaks stood like old men with their arms out. Suki was forty-one. The fever came in three days and stayed.

Waya moved into her daughter's house and did not leave.

The house smelled of woodsmoke and the particular sweetness of illness — something vegetal and close, like green wood that won't burn clean. Suki's husband kept the fire fed without being asked. He was a quiet man who expressed everything through action and nothing through speech, which Waya had once found difficult and now found she was grateful for. He brought water. He brought broth made from dried deer meat and the last of the autumn squash. He sat on the far side of the fire and did not speak and did not leave.

Their daughter, Ama, was nine.

She was the one Waya watched. The child sat close to her mother's sleeping mat, cross-legged, her hands in her lap, her face doing the work of a much older person — holding itself still, keeping something in. She had her grandmother Waya's eyes, dark and slightly too direct, the kind of eyes that made adults uncomfortable because they looked at you as if they already knew what you were about to say and were deciding whether to believe it.

On the third day, Suki woke lucid.

She looked at the smoke-hole, then at her husband, then at Waya. She did not look surprised to see her mother there, which told Waya she had known it was serious before she had let herself know.

"The pendant," Suki said.

Her voice was rough from the fever. She had worn the copper through the illness — Waya had seen it at her throat each time she checked the fire — and now she reached up and touched it with two fingers, the gesture so familiar that Waya felt it in her own collarbone, the old hollow.

"I know," Waya said.

"Ama is nine."

"I know."

"She's not ready." Suki's fingers stayed on the copper. "I thought I'd have more time."

Waya pulled her stool closer. The fire between them was low, the coals breathing orange in the dim. Outside, sleet was beginning — she could hear it on the thatch, a sound like seed scattered on a clay pot.

"Tell me what you want," Waya said.

Suki was quiet for a moment. Her breath was shallow but even. The pendant lay in the hollow of her throat and caught the firelight, the reddish-gold surface throwing a small, trembling point of light onto the wattle above her head.

"I don't want it buried with me," she said.

Waya had not expected that.

"You don't."

"I've been thinking about it." Suki's hand dropped from the pendant to her chest. "I kept thinking — when I'm gone, it goes to Ama. But she's nine. Nine is too young to carry it. And if she's too young and something happens to her — " She stopped.

"Nothing will happen to her."

"You don't know that." Suki looked at her directly. "Neither do I."

Waya was quiet.

"If it's buried with me," Suki said, "it's mine. It stays mine. That's not what it is." She paused, choosing words carefully, the way she had always done — she had gotten that from Waya, who had gotten it from Ahanu, the habit of not speaking until the right word came. "It's not mine. It never was. I was just carrying it for a while."

Waya sat with that.

"Where, then," she said.

"The mound."

The word landed in the room and changed the quality of the air. Suki's husband looked up from across the fire. Even Ama, who had been staring at her mother's face with that too-still expression, shifted slightly.

The mound was half a morning's walk east, through the oak forest and down to the low ground near the river bend. It had been there longer than anyone's grandmother's grandmother could remember. It was where the community brought its dead and its important things — not all the dead, not all the things, but the ones that mattered in a particular way, the ones that needed to be kept rather than used up. Waya had been there many times in her life. She had helped carry her father Sokan up the slope. She had stood at the edge while the earth was opened and then closed again.

She had never thought of the pendant going there.

"It would be with the others," Suki said. "With all the things that have been kept there. That's — " She stopped again. She was looking for a word. "There's a word," she said. "The old word. The one Ahanu used."

Waya knew the word. It sat in her chest before it reached her mouth.

"*Nemi-teh*," she said.

Suki's face changed. "Yes."

The word meant both things at once — *remembered* and *held*. Ahanu had used it for objects placed with care, objects that were not lost but not in use, objects that existed in a third state between the living and the dead. The mound was a place of *nemi-teh*. The pendant would be *nemi-teh*. Not Suki's. Not Ama's. Not lost. Held.

"I don't want it in the ground with me alone," Suki said. "Alone is lost. The mound is different."

"Yes," Waya said.

"You understand."

"I understand."

Suki looked at her daughter then — Ama, who had been listening with her whole body, the way children listen when they know they are hearing something that will matter later, even if they cannot say why.

"Come here," Suki said.

Ama moved to her mother's side without hesitation. She was a compact child, all economy of motion, nothing wasted. She sat close enough that their knees touched.

Suki reached up and lifted the cord over her head. The pendant swung free, the copper catching the firelight one last time before she held it out — not to Ama, but to Waya.

"You keep it until it goes to the mound," she said. "You know what it is. You know the words."

Waya took it. The cord was warm from Suki's body. The copper was cool, as it had always been, as it would always be — the metal that held the temperature of the air rather than the person, that stayed itself no matter whose skin it rested against. She closed her fingers around it.

"Say them," Suki said. "So Ama hears them."

Waya looked at her granddaughter. Ama's face was doing that work again — holding itself, keeping something in. Her eyes were dry, which told Waya more than tears would have.

"*This came from far away*," Waya said. "*It means we are not alone.*"

Ama said nothing. But she was listening the way you listen when you are trying to memorize something, her lips moving faintly, repeating the words without sound.

"And when it goes to the mound," Suki said, her voice beginning to tire, "you tell whoever takes it there — you tell them the same thing. *Not lost. Kept.*"

"*Nemi-teh*," Waya said.

"Yes."

Suki closed her eyes. The fever was pulling her back down. Her husband moved quietly to her other side and adjusted the hide over her shoulders, and the sleet went on against the thatch, and the fire breathed low, and Ama sat very still with her hands in her lap and her lips moving silently around words she was fixing in place.

Waya held the pendant and looked at her daughter's face, the lines of it, the particular way Suki's mouth went when she was asleep — slightly open, slightly turned, as if she were about to say something. She had worn that expression since she was an infant. Waya had watched it across forty-one years of mornings.

She thought: *I have already lost her. I am just waiting for the rest of it.*

She did not say this. It was not the kind of thing you said.

Suki died four days later, in the early morning, with the sleet gone and the sky pale and the river audible through the walls — that low, continuous sound, the river talking to itself the way it always did, indifferent and steady and somehow, always, a comfort.

They buried her in the cold ground near the village, with her tools and her pots and the things that had been hers in the ordinary way of things. Her husband cut his hair. Ama sat through the burial with her hands folded and her face doing its work, and afterward she came to Waya and stood close without speaking, and Waya put her arm around the child's shoulders and held her, and they stood like that for a long time while the other women finished the burial songs.

The pendant stayed with Waya.

She wore it again for the winter — not because she had meant to, but because it needed to be somewhere, and her own collarbone knew its weight. She noticed that Ama watched it. Not covetously. Just watching, the way you watch something you know is going to matter.

In the spring, when the river flooded its low banks and the red-winged blackbirds came back to the marsh, Waya went to the mound.

She went alone, in the early morning, before the village was fully awake. The path through the oak forest was soft with last year's leaves, the mast turned black and wet underfoot, the air smelling of mud and new growth and the cold that still lived in the shadows. She walked the half morning without hurrying.

The mound rose from the low ground near the river bend, earthen and patient, its slopes covered in the same forest that covered everything else, as if the land had simply gathered itself up here and decided to be taller. It was not large — she could walk around it in the time it took to eat a meal — but it had a quality of attention, a sense of being looked at from the inside, that she had felt since childhood and never found words for.

She had spoken to the keeper the week before. The keeper was an old man named Paku, grey-haired and slow-moving and possessed of an absolute certainty about the proper way to do everything, which Waya found exhausting and, in this particular matter, reassuring. He had listened to what she wanted. He had asked three questions. He had said he would prepare a place.

He was there when she arrived, standing at the mound's eastern face where the ground had been opened — a small depression, carefully cut, the earth piled to one side. The smell of the opened soil was rich and dark, the smell of deep ground, the smell of what was already there.

Waya took the pendant from her neck.

She held it for a moment in the early light. The copper had gone slightly darker over the winter, the reddish-gold deepening toward brown at the edges, green working into the hammered crevices. The arc of it, the perforation worn smooth by decades of cord. The shape Ahanu had called a crescent, a word that came from watching the moon.

She thought of the woman far away, the woman she would never know, the woman whose dead child had worn this copper and whose grief had set it moving across the world toward this river, this mound, this morning.

She thought of her father Sokan, who lay in the earth behind her.

She thought of Suki's face, the mouth slightly open, about to say something.

She placed the pendant in Paku's hands. He wrapped it in a piece of bark, folded carefully, tied with a cord of twisted plant fiber. He did not speak. She did not speak. The river was audible through the trees, the same low conversation, the same indifference and steadiness.

He placed it in the prepared depression with the other objects — she caught a glimpse of them before the earth went back, a shell worked into a shape she didn't recognize, a small clay vessel — and then he covered them, and the mound was the mound again, patient and earthen, its slopes in the morning light.

Waya stood there after he was gone.

She thought about Ama, who was ten now and who had her grandmother's eyes and who had stood at Suki's burial with her lips moving around words she was fixing in place. She thought about what she would tell the child — not about the pendant, which was gone now, but about what the pendant had meant. The chain of it. The not-knowing that traveled with the copper. The word that meant both *remembered* and *held*.

She would tell her. When Ama was old enough. When the time came.

She turned and walked back through the oak forest, the leaves soft underfoot, the river running somewhere to her left, and the morning went about its business around her — a woodpecker working at a dead elm, a crow calling once and then going silent, the first warmth of the sun coming through the canopy in long, slanted shafts that lit the mast gold before they reached the ground.

The pendant was in the earth.

It was not lost. It was *nemi-teh* — held and remembered, the two things at once, the word that Ahanu had taught her and that Waya had taught Suki and that Suki had spoken on the last morning of her clear mind, getting it right, making sure.

Waya walked home through the trees, and behind her the mound held what it held, and the river ran on, and the ground kept its long silence, and the copper lay in the dark with the other objects, patient as the earth itself, waiting for the hands that would not come for a very long time.
