The river held its breath before dawn.

Yanu stood where the bank dropped to mud, her feet bare, the cold working up through her soles and into her ankles. The water was low for this time of year — she could see the pale ribs of the sandbar twenty paces out, the surface barely moving, flat as pounded copper in the grey light. On the far bank, the oaks were dark shapes against a sky that had not yet decided what color it would be.

She was watching the birds.

Not the herons — herons told you nothing useful, standing in their patient misery at the water's edge, waiting for whatever the river brought them. Not the ducks riding the current below the sandbar. She was watching the starlings, a mass of them lifting from the treeline to the northwest, maybe two hundred, maybe more, turning together the way a hand turns in water. They rose, banked left, and settled back into the hickories.

Then they rose again.

She counted. The flock turned, hesitated — that pause, that small stutter where the whole mass seemed to forget itself — and turned again. A third time. Three hesitations in the span of a slow breath.

Behind her, she heard the wet sound of reeds being pulled from the shallows.

"Sika."

Her daughter looked up. Fourteen years old, tall for it, with Ranu's broad forehead and a habit of squinting that made her look perpetually skeptical. She had a bundle of reeds under one arm and mud to her knees.

"Come here."

Sika waded out and came to stand beside her, dripping. She looked where Yanu was looking.

"They're going to settle again," she said.

"Watch the moment before they settle."

The starlings lifted a fourth time. Sika watched. The flock banked, and there it was — the hesitation, the half-second where the whole mass seemed to pull against itself before it chose a direction.

"They can't agree," Sika said.

"They can't agree because the wind can't agree." Yanu kept her eyes on the treeline. "The wind is arguing with itself from the northwest. When the wind argues with itself, it is deciding. And when it decides—"

"Rain."

"Not just rain. Two days. Maybe less." She turned to look at her daughter. "The shape of the flock doesn't tell you. The shape is just what you see first. What tells you is the hesitation between turns."

Sika looked back at the birds, then at her mother. "How many hesitations?"

"Three means two days. Two means one. If you see four—" Yanu paused. "Come home before dark."

Sika almost smiled. She was at an age where she tried not to smile at her mother's jokes, as though smiling meant she was still a child. Yanu found this both irritating and dear.

The starlings settled and did not rise again. The river exhaled — a small sound, water over the shallows, frogs going quiet in the reeds, and then the first real light breaking through the oaks on the far bank, turning the water from grey to brown to the color of wet clay.

This was enough. This morning, this teaching, this particular quality of light on a river she had known since before she could name it. Four children sleeping in the house behind her — she could picture each of them: Sika here beside her, Denu and Koto still tangled in their sleeping mats, the baby Oro with his fist pressed against his cheek. She had counted them when she woke, as she counted them every morning, a habit she could not break.

Five, she always started to count. Then four.

Her hand went to the pendant at her collarbone.

She did not decide to touch it. Her hand simply went there, as it always did, finding the cool weight of the copper against her skin — and then, as always, the copper was not cool at all. It was warm from her body, warm as a living thing, and she pressed her thumb against the edge of it and felt the tiny ridges where the trader's hammer had worked the metal flat. He had made it in a single afternoon, sitting cross-legged by the fire, working the copper with a smooth river stone, folding and striking and folding again. She had watched him. Taya had watched him too, sitting in Yanu's lap, reaching for the bright thing with both hands.

Yanu made herself stop touching it.

The light was fully up now. Somewhere in the village behind her she heard the thud of wood being split — Ranu, probably, or one of the older boys. The smell of woodsmoke reached her, and under it the thick, nutty smell of hickory meal simmering in the big cord-marked pot, the one with the cracked rim she kept meaning to replace. Ordinary smells. The smells of every morning.

"The shad will run before the storm," she said. "Tell your father."

Sika nodded, already turning back to her reeds.

Yanu stood a moment longer, watching the empty treeline where the starlings had been. The northwest sky was clear, pale blue, giving nothing away. You had to know what to look for. You had to have watched long enough to understand that the birds knew before the sky did, that the hesitation between turns was the storm's first word, spoken two days before the storm itself arrived.

She had learned this from her own mother. Her mother had learned it from someone else. She was teaching it now to Sika, and Sika would teach it to someone after her, and the storm would keep coming from the northwest, and the birds would keep knowing first, and the hesitation would always mean the same thing.

This was how knowledge lived. This was how it moved forward.

She pressed her thumb against the pendant one more time — she could not help it — and then she turned and walked back up the bank toward the smell of the fire and her children and the ordinary day that was already beginning without her.

Behind her, the river went on talking to itself over the shallows, saying nothing she didn't already know.

The fire ring sat at the center of the cluster of houses the way a heart sits in a chest — not the largest thing, but the thing everything else arranged itself around. Yanu saw it before she saw anything else when she came up from the river path: the orange pulse of coals under grey ash, the big cord-marked pot tilted slightly on its three stones, steam rising from the cracked rim in a thin thread.

She saw the spot before she saw Ranu.

She always saw the spot first.

It was nothing to look at — a stretch of packed earth between the fire ring and the nearest house, worn smooth by years of feet, no different from any other patch of ground in the village. But her eyes went there the way a tongue goes to a missing tooth. Three winters ago, Taya had lain there in the shade of the house wall on a hot afternoon, breathing in the shallow, rapid way that meant the fever had her fully. Yanu had sat beside her and kept the flies from her face with a strip of hide. The afternoon had lasted a very long time.

She walked past it without stopping. She had learned to do this.

Ranu was on the far side of the fire ring, cross-legged on a mat, with Denu pressed against his left side and Koto wedged under his right arm, both children still creased with sleep. He was in the middle of a story — she could tell by the way his hands were moving, fingers spread, palms out, shaping something large and improbable in the air between him and the children.

"—and the beaver said, *I have no argument with you, river spirit, I am only building my house*—" Ranu's voice dropped to the beaver's voice, a low, reasonable rumble, and Denu giggled. "—but the river spirit said, *Your house is blocking my argument with the stones downstream, and I have been making that argument for ten thousand years*—"

Koto, who was six and took everything seriously, frowned. "Can a river spirit argue with stones?"

"A river spirit can argue with anything," Ranu said. "That is what makes them difficult neighbors."

Denu laughed again, a clean, uncomplicated sound. Yanu stopped at the edge of the mat and watched.

Ranu had not yet seen her. He was fully inside the story — she could see it in his face, the slight loosening around his eyes that meant he was somewhere else, somewhere the story was more real than the village, the fire, the morning. He had always been able to do this. It was the thing she had loved first about him, before she loved anything else: the way he could step into a story and pull other people in after him, so that the story became the place they were all living for a little while.

He had been telling stories about Taya since the third day after she died.

Not to Yanu. To everyone else. To the children, to the other families, to the old men who came to sit by the fire in the evenings. Stories that had Taya in them — small stories, true ones at first, the real things she had done in her two years: the way she had eaten the hickory meal by pressing her whole fist into the bowl, the time she had tried to pick up a live coal because it was the same color as the copper pendant and she wanted it. Then, gradually, the stories had grown. Taya had become a child who laughed at ravens. A child who had once, in a story Yanu had not recognized at all, walked to the river by herself and stood at the water's edge and watched the herons for a long time without moving, as though she already understood something about patience.

Yanu had not said: *that didn't happen.*

She had not said anything.

She touched the pendant. The copper was warm from the river walk, warm from her skin, and she pressed her thumbnail against the edge of it until she felt the tiny hammered ridges bite back. The trader had made it for Taya in an afternoon — she could still see his hands, the river stone he used as a hammer, the way the copper changed shape under each blow, brightening at the edges where the metal compressed. Taya had worn it for six weeks. Six weeks and three days, if Yanu was counting, and she was always counting.

"—so the beaver said, *Then I will move my house downstream, out of your argument*—"

Koto: "But that's giving up."

"Is it?" Ranu looked at him seriously. "Or is it choosing which arguments are worth having?"

Koto thought about this with the full weight of his six years. Denu had stopped listening and was picking at a loose thread on the mat. Ranu glanced up and saw Yanu standing at the edge of the firelight.

His face changed. Not much — she would not have seen it if she didn't know his face as well as she knew the river's moods. A small softening, and under it a small careful thing, the look he got when he was trying to read her and could not.

"Sika is at the water," Yanu said. "The shad will run before the storm. Two days."

"I'll tell the men." He did not ask how she knew. He had stopped asking how she knew years ago; he simply told the men, and the men came in from the river before the storm, and the shad were there, and no one asked how Ranu had known either.

She moved past the fire ring toward the house. The big pot was simmering — hickory meal and something else, dried meat from the smell of it, the thick nutty smell she had been following up from the river. The pot's cord-marked exterior was black with soot from years of fires, rough as tree bark against her fingers when she touched the rim, but the inside was smooth, almost slick, smelling of fat and old meals and the mineral taste of the clay itself.

She should eat. She was not hungry.

"Yanu."

She turned. Ranu was looking at her over the children's heads, Denu now asleep again against his side, Koto still frowning at the philosophical problem of the beaver.

"Come sit," Ranu said.

She looked at the mat. She looked at the space beside him where there was room for her, where she had sat a thousand mornings, where the children would lean against her the way they leaned against him, warm and heavy with sleep, smelling of the sleeping mat and the night.

She looked at the spot on the packed earth between the fire ring and the house wall.

"I need to check the weir lines," she said.

It was not a lie. The weir lines did need checking before the shad run. But Ranu knew, and she knew he knew, and the knowing sat between them like a stone in the middle of a path that both of them had learned to walk around.

He did not press her. He never pressed her. He turned back to Koto and said, "Now, the river spirit was not finished—" and Koto leaned forward, and the story went on.

Yanu stood for one more moment at the edge of the firelight, watching them. Ranu's voice, low and easy, shaping the beaver's world in the air above the coals. Denu's sleeping weight against his ribs. Koto's frown of concentration, his small hands pressed flat on his knees.

She had not made Taya into anything yet.

She did not know how. She did not know if the thing she felt — the weight of it, the specific, daily, grinding weight — could be made into a story, into something you could hand to another person and say: *here, hold this for a moment, so I don't have to hold it alone.* Ranu had found a way to do that. He had found it quickly, or it had found him, and now Taya lived in his stories the way the river lived in the stories — always there, always moving, always the same river even when the water was different water.

Yanu pressed her thumbnail against the pendant's edge again.

The copper had been warm from Taya's skin too, in those six weeks. She had forgotten that until this moment — or not forgotten, but not let herself remember. Taya had worn it against her chest, and when Yanu picked her up the pendant would be warm, warm from the child's small body, and Taya would reach up and grab it with both hands and pull, and Yanu would say *gently, gently*, and Taya would look at her with the expression she had, the one that meant she understood the word but disagreed with the principle.

She was still standing at the edge of the mat.

She made herself walk.

Past the fire ring, past the spot, through the low doorway of the house where the baby Oro was still sleeping, his fist pressed against his cheek, his breathing the slow, even breathing of a child who had never yet woken to find the world smaller than he left it.

She stood over him for a moment in the dim interior, the bark walls holding the night's cool, the sleeping mat smelling of the dried sedge grass it was stuffed with. She could hear Ranu's voice through the wall, muffled now, still telling the story. The beaver was moving its house. The river spirit was reconsidering its argument.

Yanu crouched beside the baby and touched his cheek with the back of one finger, very lightly, so as not to wake him.

He was warm. His skin was warm and smooth and he breathed without any effort at all, the way the living breathe, not knowing it is something they are doing.

She stayed there until she heard Koto laugh at something outside — a real laugh, surprised out of him — and then she stood up and went to find the weir lines, carrying the pendant's weight against her collarbone, carrying the question she had not yet found an answer to, walking out into the ordinary morning that had no interest in waiting for her to be ready.

The women were already at work when Yanu reached the drying racks — four of them spread along the flat ground above the bank where the terrace dropped away to the river, their hands moving in the quick, practiced rhythm of women who had been doing this since before the sun was fully up.

The racks were simple: green poles lashed crosswise, the dried shad split and hanging in rows, their silver skins gone matte and stiff in the morning air. The smell was everywhere — salt and smoke and the particular sharp sweetness of fish that has been cured just long enough. Yanu's eyes watered when she walked into it, the way they always did, and Somi looked up from her work and grinned.

"You're late," Somi said.

"The weir lines needed checking."

"The weir lines always need checking when Ranu is telling a story."

The other women laughed — Pika, who was oldest and sat closest to the rack's best light; young Dassa, who was Pika's granddaughter and had Pika's same narrow hands; and Benna, who was Somi's sister and laughed at everything Somi said on principle. Yanu found her place at the far end of the rack, the place that was hers by the unspoken arrangement that governed who sat where, and picked up the bone awl she had left there yesterday, its surface smooth and faintly greasy from years of handling.

"The shad are running thick," she said. "The lines are full."

"We know," Pika said. "Leka's boys were at the river before dawn."

"Of course they were." Yanu worked the awl through a fish's tail fin, threading the cord that would hold it to the rack. "Leka's boys are always somewhere they shouldn't be before dawn."

"They're efficient," Pika said, with the careful neutrality of a woman who had strong opinions and had learned when to keep them.

"They're twelve," Somi said.

"Efficient twelve-year-olds."

Dassa laughed, and Benna laughed because Dassa laughed, and for a moment the work went on in the comfortable noise of women who know each other well enough to be quiet together and loud together and to know which is called for.

Down at the water's edge, two men were arguing about a canoe. Yanu could hear them without looking — Kael's voice, which carried, and the lower, more deliberate voice of old Tovan, who had built canoes for forty years and had opinions about wood that he expressed the way other men expressed opinions about enemies: with total conviction and no interest in being wrong.

"The crack runs with the grain," Kael said. "If you fill it, it will split again at the same place."

"If you fill it correctly," Tovan said, "it will not."

"You filled the last one correctly."

A pause. "That was different wood."

Somi caught Yanu's eye and said nothing, which was its own kind of comment.

The morning moved. The sun climbed above the tree line — oak and hickory mostly, the big canopy trees that had stood here longer than anyone's grandmother's grandmother could remember, their leaves just beginning to open, pale and new, the color of something not yet decided. A woodpecker worked at a dead trunk somewhere up the slope, its hammering coming down through the trees in irregular bursts. Yanu listened to it without meaning to, the way she always listened, the habit so deep it had stopped feeling like a habit.

Her daughter Suli came down from the house with the baby Oro on her hip, walking carefully the way she always walked when she was carrying him, as if she had decided that her own steadiness was the thing keeping him safe. She was nine. She walked like a woman twice that age.

"Oro wants to see the fish," Suli said.

"Oro wants to eat the fish," Somi said. "He's been staring at the rack since you got here."

Oro, who was not yet two and had no words for what he wanted, confirmed this by reaching toward the nearest hanging shad with both arms and making a sound of pure, uncomplicated longing.

"Not yet," Yanu said. "They need more time."

Oro looked at her with the expression that meant he had heard the word *no* and was considering his options.

"He has your face when he does that," Dassa said.

"He has his own face."

"Yes, but the *expression* is yours."

Yanu looked at her son. The expression was, she admitted privately, hers. She had made it herself at the council fire three nights ago when the men were arguing about whether to move the summer camp upstream, and Ranu had caught her eye and pressed his lips together to keep from laughing, and she had pressed hers together too, and for a moment they had been the same person looking at the same thing.

She looked away from Oro's face before the thought could go where thoughts went when she wasn't watching them.

"Suli," she said. "Do you see the swallows?"

Suli turned, still holding Oro, and looked at the sky above the river. A dozen swallows were working the air over the water, their flight low and fast, skimming the surface.

"They're flying low," Suli said.

"Yes. What does that mean?"

Suli thought. She had the same frown Koto had, the same pressing-in of the brows that meant she was taking the question seriously. "Rain," she said. "Coming before dark."

"Before midday," Yanu said. "Look at how fast they're working. They're feeding hard because the air is going to close down. When they feed like that — that tight, that quick — you have less time than you think."

Suli looked at the swallows a moment longer, committing it. Then she shifted Oro on her hip and said, "Should I tell Ranu to bring in the drying mats?"

"Tell him the swallows say so. He'll understand."

Suli nodded and turned back up toward the house, carrying Oro with his arms still stretched toward the fish, his protest fading as he got farther away. Yanu watched them go — her daughter's careful walk, the baby's weight, the pale new leaves of the hickory moving slightly in a wind that was not yet strong enough to feel on her skin but was there, was already there.

"She learns fast," Pika said.

"She pays attention."

"That's the same thing."

Yanu ran her thumb along the edge of the dried fish she was holding, checking the cure. Two more days, maybe three if the rain came in hard. She hung it back on the rack and reached for the next one.

"Yanu." Pika's voice had changed — not lower, exactly, but more careful. The voice she used when she was going to say something she had been holding for a while.

Yanu kept her hands moving.

"The pendant," Pika said.

The bone awl was still in Yanu's hand. She did not stop working. "What about it."

"I've been thinking about it." Pika paused. The other women were quiet — not the quiet of people pretending not to listen, but the quiet of people who understood that some conversations needed witnesses. "It's been almost a full turning of seasons."

"I know how long it's been."

"I know you know." Pika's voice was gentle, the way a hand is gentle when it touches something that might hurt. "I'm not counting for you. I'm asking."

Yanu looked at the fish on the rack. The smoke had darkened them to a deep amber, the flesh dense and dry, holding its shape. Things that had been preserved. Things that could last.

"Asking what," she said, though she knew.

"Whether you've decided." Pika set down the fish she was working on. "Whether you'll keep wearing it. Or give it to the earth with her."

The pendant was warm against Yanu's collarbone. It was always warm. She had stopped noticing that it was warm, which was itself a kind of answer, or the beginning of one.

"I don't know," she said.

Pika nodded, as if this were the answer she had expected. As if *I don't know* were a real answer and not the absence of one.

"There's no wrong choice," Pika said.

"I know that too."

"Do you?"

Yanu looked at her. Pika was watching her with the calm, steady look of a woman who had buried her own children — two of them, both before they could walk — and who had come out the other side of it carrying something that was not peace exactly but was the shape of peace, the place where peace would eventually live.

"If you keep it," Pika said, "she stays with you."

"She stays with me either way."

"Yes." Pika picked up the fish again. "But if you give it to the earth, she stays *there* too. In the ground. In the place. Not just in you."

Yanu said nothing. She threaded the cord through the next fish's tail and hung it on the rack and reached for the next one. Her hands knew what to do. Her hands had always known what to do. It was the rest of her that kept losing the thread.

Kael's voice rose again from the water's edge — something about pine pitch, about the specific grade of pine pitch required for a proper fill — and Tovan's voice came back with the patience of a man who had been right about wood for four decades and expected to continue being right. Somi's shoulders shook once, silently. Benna pressed her lips together.

The swallows were still working the river, their flight getting tighter, the sky behind them taking on the flat, white look that came before rain.

Yanu pressed her thumbnail against the pendant's edge, the gesture she made without thinking, the gesture her body made when her mind was somewhere else.

She did not know. She did not know if the thing she was carrying could be put into the ground, if *into the ground* was a place that could hold it, if the earth could take the weight of it the way Pika seemed to believe the earth could. She did not know if she was ready to find out. She did not know if she would ever be ready, or if readiness was even the thing that was required, or if the decision would come to her the way the weather came — not when she was watching for it, but when she had stopped watching, when she was doing something else entirely, when her hands were busy and her mind was somewhere ordinary, and then all at once the swallows would change their flight and she would know.

"We should bring in the outer mats," she said. "Before the rain."

Pika looked at the sky. "You're sure about midday?"

"I'm sure about the swallows."

Pika stood, her knees cracking, and called to Dassa, and the work shifted — the particular, efficient reorganization of women who have done this before, who know which mats come in first and which racks can take the rain and which cannot. Yanu worked with them, her hands moving, the pendant warm against her skin, the question still open, still waiting, patient as the ground beneath her feet.

The fire had been built up for the telling.

That was how you knew Ranu was going to speak — not the fire itself, which burned every evening, but the way it burned: higher than it needed to be for warmth, fed with dry hickory split fine, so the flames stood clean and yellow and threw light far enough to reach the outer ring of people settling in from the edges of the camp. Children came first, the way they always did, pulling their sleeping mats close to the heat. Then the older women, then the men who had been finishing work at the canoe frame, then Pika and Dassa and the others from the smoking racks, their hands still smelling of fish and woodsmoke.

Yanu came last. She sat where the firelight thinned — close enough to hear, far enough that her face would not be easy to read.

Ranu was already at the center. He had not announced himself. He never did. He simply sat where the fire was brightest and waited, and the community arranged itself around him the way water arranges itself around a stone, and then he began.

"There was a child," he said.

The camp went quiet. Not the quiet of surprise — everyone here knew which child — but the quiet of people who understood that this was a thing that needed to be said aloud, and who were grateful that Ranu was the one who could say it.

"She was very small. She had not yet learned to walk, but she had learned to watch. She would sit in the doorway of the house and watch everything — the river, the trees, the sky. Most of all the sky."

A child near the fire — Somi's youngest, barely three — shifted closer to her mother without looking away from Ranu. He had that quality: you looked at him and the world outside the firelight stopped mattering.

"The birds came to her," Ranu said. "Not all at once. First one, then another. A wren. Then a thrush. They would land near her hands and she would look at them with those eyes she had, those very serious eyes, and they would look back at her. And she would tell them things."

"What things?" said one of the older boys. He was seven, maybe eight, at the age when the story and the question were equally important.

"The things she knew," Ranu said. "Things she had learned from watching. She told the wren that the wind was going to shift before dark, and the wren flew away to tell its family. She told the thrush that the rain was three days off, and the thrush went to find higher ground. And they listened to her because she was a child, and children know things that the rest of us have forgotten."

Yanu's hand found the pendant under her robe.

The copper was warm. It was always warm.

"When she went to the spirit world," Ranu said — and his voice dropped here, not breaking, but softening, the way a river softens where it widens — "the birds did not know what to do. They had been taking their direction from her. They would circle over the camp looking for her, looking for that small face in the doorway, those serious eyes. And she was not there."

A girl near the fire made a sound — not quite a word, not quite a cry. Her mother put an arm around her.

"But she had not forgotten them," Ranu said. "She was in the spirit world, but she could still see the sky. She could see the whole sky at once, all of it, from the great water in the east to the mountains in the west. And she saw the birds circling, confused, and she felt sorry for them. So she began to call to them."

He paused. The fire crackled. A log shifted, sending a brief column of sparks up into the dark.

"She could not call the way we call. She was in the spirit world and her voice was different there — it was the size of the whole sky. So when she called, all the birds heard her at once. All of them. Every flock, every bird that had ever listened to her in the doorway. And she told them: *go south when the cold comes. Go north when the warmth comes. Watch the river. Watch the wind. I will tell you when to turn.*"

"And that is why," said the boy who had asked the question, because he had heard enough stories to know the shape of them.

"And that is why," Ranu agreed. "When you see a flock turn all at once — all of them, together, as if one mind moved them — that is her. That is the small child in the spirit world, calling out the direction. She has not forgotten. She is still watching the sky. She is still telling the birds what she knows."

The fire moved in the silence. Yanu could feel the warmth of it on her shins, on her hands. The pendant was a coal against her breastbone.

The children were asking questions now, the way children did — overlapping, eager, not sad at all, because Ranu had made it into something that was not sad. The boy wanted to know if you could hear her when the birds turned. A smaller girl wanted to know if she was cold in the spirit world. Ranu answered each one with the same patient attention, building the story around their questions, and the story grew in the way a good fire grows — fed by what was offered to it.

Yanu watched him.

He was good at this. He had always been good at this. His face in the firelight was open, warm, alive with the pleasure of the telling — not false pleasure, not performance, but the real thing, the joy of a man who had found the shape of his grief and could carry it in both hands and show it to people and say: *look, here it is, it is not too heavy, we can hold it together.* 

That was what he had done with Taya. He had made her into a story. He had taken the small, specific, irreplaceable fact of her — the weight of her, the smell of her hair, the way she had watched the wren from the doorway with those eyes that already knew things — and he had opened it out into something larger, something that explained the world, something that the whole community could carry.

And they were carrying it. She could see it in their faces — the relief of it, the gratitude. Pika was nodding slowly. Old Tovan had his eyes half-closed, listening. The children were leaning toward the fire, toward Ranu, toward the story, and their faces had the particular brightness of children who have been given a gift they did not know they needed.

The pendant's edge bit into Yanu's palm.

She had not realized she was pressing it. She loosened her fist, but kept the copper against her skin, hidden under the robe, where no one could see it.

*She told the birds what she knew.*

It was a beautiful story. It was the most beautiful story Ranu had ever told, and he had told many. She knew that. She could feel the beauty of it the way you feel the warmth of a fire you are standing too far from — present, real, not enough.

Because Taya had not told the birds anything. Taya had sat in the doorway and watched the wren with those eyes and the wren had done what wrens do, which was to find a grub in the leaf litter and eat it and fly away, and Taya had watched it go with the same serious attention she gave to everything, and that had been the whole of it. That had been enough. That had been everything.

The wren had not listened to her. The birds did not take their direction from her small voice in the spirit world. The flocks turned because of wind, because of cold, because of something in their blood that was older than any child, older than any story.

Taya had been two years old. She had not had time to become a reason for anything.

And yet.

And yet the children around the fire would carry this story. They would watch the birds turn and they would think of the small child calling out the direction, and the world would be larger for it, the sky more inhabited, the flocks more purposeful. Ranu had put Taya into the world in a way that would last. He had made her into something that could not be lost.

Yanu's throat was tight.

She pressed the pendant harder against her palm — the hammered copper edge, the slight roughness where the trader had worked the metal cold, the thin cord worn smooth with a year of her handling. It was the only thing she had that was just Taya's. Not Taya transformed, not Taya made into a lesson or a spirit or an explanation. Just the object that had been made for her, that had hung at her small throat for the three weeks she had been strong enough to wear it, that Yanu had taken back when she was gone.

Ranu's voice was warm and steady and the children were laughing now — something he had said, some small joke folded into the story — and the sound of their laughter rose up into the dark above the fire and disappeared.

Yanu sat at the edge of the light and did not move.

She understood what he was doing. She understood that it was good, that it was necessary, that the community needed this and he was giving it to them the only way he knew how. She understood that his grief was real and that the story was how he carried it, and that there was nothing wrong with how he carried it.

She just could not carry it that way herself.

She could not make Taya larger. She could only keep her exactly the size she had been: small enough to hold in two hands, heavy enough to change the weight of every day since. The pendant was that weight. The pendant was the only place where Taya stayed Taya — not a spirit, not a lesson, not the reason the birds turned. Just a child. Just hers.

A child near the fire yawned hugely, and her mother gathered her in, and the story was winding toward its end. Ranu was speaking more slowly now, his voice dropping back toward the ordinary register of a man sitting by a fire at the end of a day, and the community was settling, the way a flock settles when it lands — movement becoming stillness, the edges of the group drawing in.

Pika caught Yanu's eye across the fire. Not a question. Just a look that said: *I see you. I know where you are.*

Yanu looked back at her. Then she looked down at her hand, still closed around the pendant under the robe.

Ranu finished. People thanked him — the particular murmur of thanks that was not applause but was warmer than silence. He accepted it the way he always did, with a small nod and a slight smile, already coming back from wherever the story had taken him.

His eyes found Yanu across the fire.

She did not look away. She let him find her face, let him see whatever was there. He looked at her for a long moment — his husband's look, the one that knew her, the one that had known her for fifteen years — and she saw something shift in it. Not hurt. Not quite. Something more careful than hurt. The look of a man who has offered a gift and is not sure if it was the right gift, and is not sure how to ask.

She did not know how to answer him.

She opened her hand under the robe and let the pendant lie flat against her palm, its weight small and specific and exact. The fire burned down a little. Around her, the camp began the slow work of ending the day — mats being pulled in, children carried, the outer fire tamped to coals.

The question Pika had asked that afternoon was still open. *If you give it to the earth, she stays there too.*

Yanu looked at the dying fire and thought: *but what if I am not ready for her to stay anywhere yet. What if I am not ready for her to be in one place.*

Above the treeline, somewhere in the dark, a night heron called once and was silent.

The fire was coals now. Around her, the camp breathed in sleep — Ranu's slow exhale beside her, the children's smaller rhythms, the baby tucked against her side with one fist closed against his own cheek. The robe smelled of woodsmoke and the faint oil of rendered hickory, worked into the hide over many seasons until it had become the smell of warmth itself.

Yanu lay on her back and did not close her eyes.

She held the pendant against her sternum, not gripping it — just letting it rest there, feeling its weight, the hammered copper cool even against skin that had warmed it all day. With her thumb she traced the edge where the trader had worked the metal. Cold-hammered, he had shown her, pressing the copper flat with a smooth river stone, working it in small overlapping blows until it thinned and spread. She had watched his hands. She had not understood then why he was making it for a child he had never met, a child whose name he had not asked. He had simply seen the pendant take shape under his hands and offered it, and she had taken it, and three weeks later Taya had worn it at her small throat and looked up at Yanu with the expression she always had — alert, faintly puzzled, as though the world were a problem she was working on.

Three weeks. Then the fever. Then the pendant back in Yanu's hand, lighter than she remembered, lighter than it had any right to be.

She pressed it flat against her sternum and stared at the dark above her.

She did not want to sleep. Sleep was a kind of forgetting, and she was not ready to forget, even for a night, even for an hour. She knew this was foolish — she knew the body needed sleep the way it needed water, that she was wearing herself thin on grief the way a cord wears thin at the knot — but knowing it did not change the wanting. She lay awake and listened to her family breathe and held the pendant and waited for something she could not name.

Outside, the wind had dropped. The trees were still.

She had told the children the storm was coming in two days. She had said it with the certainty she always brought to these readings — the certainty that was not arrogance but experience, fifteen years of watching the river and the sky and the birds until their behavior had become a kind of language she read without effort. She had been right about weather more times than she could count. The community trusted her readings the way they trusted a well-set weir — not because it never failed, but because it failed rarely enough to be worth trusting.

But lying here in the stillness, she felt it: she had been wrong.

Not about the storm. The storm was coming. She had been wrong about when.

She turned the reading over in her mind the way she would turn a stone tool, checking the edge. The swallows this morning, skimming the water lower than usual — that was right, that was a storm sign, she had read it correctly. The way the herons had moved off the mudflats before midday — also right. But the geese. She had seen the geese flying low and fast along the river at dusk, and she had counted that as confirmation of two days, the way she always counted it, the way her own mother had taught her to count it. *Low and fast means the storm is still at a distance. Low and slow means it is close.*

They had been low and fast. She had read: two days.

But the night heron's single call, just before she had come inside — that was the sound a heron made when the air pressure was already changing, when the water was beginning to taste different to whatever sense herons used to taste the air. She had heard it and noted it and not revised her reading. She had been too settled in the two-day answer to let the heron move her off it.

The storm would come tomorrow.

She lay still and accepted this. She had been wrong by a day. It was not a large error. No one would suffer for it — the camp was well-sited on its terrace above the river, the food stores covered, the canoes pulled up and turned. A day's error in a storm reading was the kind of error that went unnoticed, corrected only by the storm itself arriving and everyone saying *earlier than you thought* and moving on.

But she noted it. She filed the correction — the heron's call, the pressure in the air, the way those two things together meant *sooner, not later* — and she would not make that error again.

This was the work. Not the being right. The correcting.

She had learned weather from her mother, who had learned it from hers, and the learning was not a body of fixed knowledge but a practice of continuous revision — watching, reading, being wrong, correcting, watching again. The birds did not lie. They responded to what was actually there, not to what you expected. If your reading disagreed with the birds, you were wrong. You revised.

She thought: *I do not know how to revise the grief.*

The thought arrived without warning and sat there, plain and exact, the way a correct reading sat — not comfortable, but true.

With weather, there was always a next reading. A next flock, a next shift in the wind, a next chance to correct the error and come closer to what was actually there. The practice had a direction. You got better. The knowledge accumulated. The errors grew smaller.

She did not know if grief worked that way. She did not know if she was getting better at it or simply getting used to it, which was not the same thing. She did not know if there was a next reading that would bring her closer to something true, or if this — the pendant, the weight, the lying awake — was simply what it was, without correction, without accumulation, without end.

She did not know what to do with the weight of the pendant.

She did not know what to do with the fact that Taya had been real — had been a specific, small, particular person with a specific way of looking at the world — and that this realness had nowhere to go now. Ranu put it into stories. Pika put it into questions. The community put it into the ordinary labor of grief, the food brought, the tasks shared, the careful not-mentioning that was its own kind of mention. All of it was true and none of it was enough and she did not know what enough would look like or if it existed.

She lay still and listened to the camp breathe.

Above her, through the smoke-hole in the bark, she could see nothing — the sky was already clouding over, the stars gone, the dark above her the flat dark of a sky that was filling with weather. She had been right about the storm. She had been wrong about the day.

Then she heard it.

A small sound. A shifting in the oaks above the camp — not wind, there was no wind, but the particular rustle of many birds adjusting at once, the way a roosting flock adjusts when something in the air changes, when the pressure drops or the temperature shifts in some way that the birds feel before any person does. Not alarm. Not flight. Just a small collective settling, a repositioning, a hundred small bodies turning slightly on their branches as the night changed around them.

She listened to it and read it.

*Tomorrow*, she thought. *Not in two days. Tomorrow, by midday.*

She had been wrong by a day. She corrected the reading. She noted the heron's call, the geese's speed, the way she had weighted them against each other, and she adjusted — the heron outweighed the geese when the heron called at dusk, not at dawn. She had not known that before. She knew it now.

She closed her eyes.

The pendant lay flat against her sternum, its weight small and exact, the copper edge smooth under her thumb. Around her the camp breathed. Ranu shifted in his sleep and his hand found her arm without waking, the way it always did, the long habit of a body that knew where she was even in the dark.

She lay still and did not yet know what she would do.

She did not know that in two years she would walk to the high knoll above the river and understand, all at once, what the mound was for. She did not know that the pendant would go into the earth with her daughter and with something else — not the grief itself, which could not be buried, but the part of the grief that needed a place to be, a fixed location, a ground that would hold it. She did not know that the earth would receive it the way the earth received everything: without judgment, without story, without making it into anything other than what it was.

She did not know that the pendant would stay there for two thousand years.

She did not know any of this. She only knew the storm was coming tomorrow, and she had been wrong by a day, and the birds above her had given her the correction she needed, and she was grateful to them for it, as she was always grateful — the birds who did not lie, who responded to what was actually there, who adjusted without grief or ceremony when the air changed and the night changed and everything that had been in one place shifted slightly and became something new.

Above the treeline, the oaks were quiet again. The flock had settled. The night held still around its answer.

Yanu breathed out slowly and let her thumb rest against the pendant's edge and did not yet know what she was waiting for, only that she was still waiting, and that the waiting itself was a kind of faithfulness — to Taya, to the weight, to the practice of watching until the thing you were watching finally told you what it was.
