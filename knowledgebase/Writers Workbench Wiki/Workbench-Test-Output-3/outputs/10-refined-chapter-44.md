The trowel caught something that wasn't soil.

It was a sound more than a sensation — a faint, dull ring, the kind a metal spoon makes when it taps the bottom of a clay pot, gone so fast Tayak thought she'd imagined it. She went still. The trench was two meters down now, the walls close on either side, and the late afternoon light came in at a low angle that turned everything the color of old honey. She was alone. Wren had gone to refill the water bottles twenty minutes ago, and the site felt the way it always felt at this hour — held, somehow. Waiting.

She set the trowel down.

The soil at Stratum Five was different from the layers above it: a darker band, almost charcoal at the edges, with the faint sweet-rot smell of very old organic matter. She'd been working it for three days, moving in centimeters, taking Munsell readings every pass — 10YR 3/2, very dark grayish brown, then shifting to 7.5YR 2.5/1 at the construction lens, a compacted layer that didn't belong to any natural accumulation. Someone had put this earth here deliberately. Someone had chosen it.

She picked up the fine brush — the one she'd bought herself, sable, because the university-issue brushes were too stiff — and began to clear the spot where the trowel had touched. The soil came away in small, dry puffs. The air smelled of clay and something underneath the clay, something mineral and faintly sweet, like a coin held in a closed fist.

Green.

She saw it before she understood it. A smear of color against the dark soil, the particular blue-green of old copper, malachite-bright at the edge and deeper where the earth had packed against it. Her stomach dropped the way it did at the top of a ladder.

*Don't rush it.* Her first field supervisor's voice, seven years dead, still lived in her hands. *The artifact has been there for a thousand years. It can wait five more minutes.*

She breathed out slowly and kept brushing.

The shape came clear in stages. First the curve — a crescent, or close to it, the arc of it emerging from the soil like a moon rising in slow motion. Then the surface: hammered flat, thin enough that she could see where the edges had softened with corrosion but still held their original line. The green was not uniform — there were patches where the copper had gone nearly black, and one place near what she was starting to think was the apex where the color deepened to a reddish brown, almost the color of dried blood, where the soil had sealed tight and the metal had not fully oxidized.

She sat back on her heels and looked at it without touching it.

*Hopewell-influenced.* The thought arrived fully formed, professional, certain. The shape, the gauge of the metal, the way it had been worked — hammered, not cast, native copper, probably from the Great Lakes by way of three or four centuries of trade hands. She had seen pendants like this in collections at the Smithsonian, behind glass, lit from below, labeled in small serif type. She had never seen one in the ground. She had never been the person whose trowel found it.

Above her, a crow landed in the sweetgum at the trench edge and said something sharp and brief, then left.

She reached for the calipers and the fine brush together, then stopped. Her hands were not quite steady. She put the calipers down and used only the brush, working the soil from the apex first, moving toward the suspension hole she expected to find there. It was there. A drilled circle, maybe four millimeters across, the edges of the drilling still faintly ridged where the original tool had worked. The inside of the hole was reddish-brown — protected from the soil, from the centuries, from everything. The copper there was almost the color it had been when someone made it.

When someone wore it.

She sat with that thought longer than she should have. The professional part of her was already composing the field log entry — *copper pendant, Hopewell-influenced, ca. 200–300 CE, hammered native copper, crescent form, single suspension hole at apex, malachite and azurite patina, reddish-brown metal retained at suspension hole interior, recovered from Stratum Five construction lens, Unit 7, Level 9* — and the rest of her was somewhere else entirely. She was thinking about the green halo she could now see in the soil around the pendant, copper ions that had leached out over eighteen hundred years and stained the earth, a ghost of the object visible in the ground around the object itself. The pendant had marked the soil. It had changed the chemistry of everything it touched.

She was thinking about the shell gorget from Stratum Three, the face carved into it — the woman's face, the one she'd told herself was generic, stylized, not a portrait. The one she'd been dreaming about since.

She was thinking about the fact that she had not written that in the field log either.

A mosquito found the back of her neck and she slapped it without looking up. The cicadas in the tree line had gone quiet in the last hour — the temperature was dropping, the shadows in the trench going blue — and in the silence she could hear, faintly, the long sigh of trucks on Route 301 half a mile away, the world continuing its business.

She photographed the pendant in situ: four shots from above, two from the side, one close-up of the suspension hole. She logged the coordinates on the tablet — Unit 7, Level 9, 1.94 meters below datum. She wrote the Munsell reading. She noted the green halo.

She did not note what she was feeling, because there was no field log category for it.

The pendant was perhaps seven centimeters at its widest. Small enough to sit in her palm. She did not lift it yet — it needed more clearing, and she was not going to rush the edges, where the copper was thinnest and most likely to flake. She worked the brush in small circles, gentle as she could make herself, and the crescent came fully clear in the last of the afternoon light.

It was beautiful. That was the word she'd been not-thinking for the past forty minutes, the word that had no place in a field log and every place in the trench. Beautiful, and old, and someone had put it here. Someone had chosen this ground, this depth, this darkness, and laid this thing down and covered it over and walked away.

The crow came back. Or a different crow. It watched her from the sweetgum with its head tilted, and then it was gone again.

Tayak sat in the trench for a long time after she'd finished the clearing, the pendant documented and bagged and labeled, the field log closed. The soil walls held the day's heat close. Somewhere above the tree line, a jet crossed toward Andrews, its contrail pink in the last light.

She had found six artifacts now. She was going deeper. The mound was not finished with her.

She picked up the field bag and climbed out of the trench, and the evening air hit her face like cool water, and she stood at the edge of the unit and looked down at where the pendant had been, at the small rectangular absence in the dark earth, and thought: *someone put that there for a reason, and the reason was not for me to find it, and I found it anyway, and now I have to decide what that means.*

She didn't decide anything. She covered the unit with the tarp, weighted the corners with river cobbles, and walked back toward the equipment shed.

Her phone was in her jacket pocket. She did not take it out.

Not yet.

The vision came for her in the equipment shed.

She had not sat down to have it. She had not closed her eyes or done anything that invited it. She was standing at the folding table with the pendant's evidence bag in front of her — sealed, labeled, tucked inside the padded transport case — and she was reaching for the zipper on the case when the shed went away.

Not dark. Not a blackout. The shed simply ceased to be the thing she was inside of, and something else was.

Firelight. Low and orange, the kind that comes from a small hearth banked for the night, not built up for cooking. The smoke rose in a slow column and the air around it was close and warm and smelled of pine resin and something else — rendered fat, maybe, or the particular sweetness of hot river clay. The floor was packed earth. The walls were close. She could not see them clearly, but she felt them the way you feel the walls of a familiar room in the dark: they were there, they were near, they were home.

The woman was already kneeling.

She was perhaps thirty-five, perhaps forty — the firelight made it hard to place, and in any case she wore her age the way people did who had worked outdoors their entire lives, the lines around her eyes and mouth not from years but from weather and attention. Her hair was loose, black, falling forward over one shoulder as she bent. Her hands were in front of her, holding something.

The pendant.

Tayak knew it was the pendant. The copper caught the firelight and threw it back warm and reddish, not green — not yet, not for centuries — and the crescent shape was unmistakable, the single suspension hole at the apex threaded with a cord of twisted sinew. The woman's grip was careful. Both thumbs under the curve, both forefingers along the back edge, the way you hold something you know is fragile.

In front of her, a child.

Perhaps two years old. Sitting upright with the slightly stunned gravity of a child who has been told to hold still and is trying very hard. The child's eyes were dark and very serious and fixed on the pendant with the total attention that small children give to things that catch light. She was wearing a small garment — hide, soft-looking, fringed at the hem — and her hair had been combed and oiled so that it lay flat and neat, the way you would prepare a child for something that mattered.

The woman lowered the pendant toward the child's neck.

Her hands were steady. Tayak noticed this the way she noticed things in the field — not emotionally, at first, just as data. The woman's hands were steady. She was not nervous. She was performing an act she had decided to perform, and the decision was already behind her, and what remained was only the doing of it.

The cord went over the child's head.

The child looked down at the pendant resting against her chest. She reached for it with one hand, that reflex of small children toward anything new and bright, and the woman caught the child's hand gently and held it, not pulling it away but just — holding it. *Not yet.* Or something that meant that. The child looked up at her mother's face and seemed to accept this.

Then the woman spoke.

The sound arrived the way sound arrives through deep water — present, but slowed, the consonants softened and the vowels stretched into something longer than speech. Tayak could not hear the language. She could not parse it into words. She could only feel it as a shape, the way you feel music in a room before you can make out the tune, and the shape of it was a name. A single name. The woman was bending down close to the child's face and speaking a name directly into the space between them, private and deliberate, the way you say a name when you want it to take root.

The acoustics of the vision did something then.

The sound shifted. As if the water above her had thinned, or the distance had collapsed, or something in the transmission had corrected itself — and for one clear instant, maybe half a second, the name came through.

*Tayak.*

She heard it in the woman's voice. She heard it the way you hear your own name called across a crowded room — not louder than everything else, but different in kind, cutting through, landing in the part of you that knows it belongs to you.

The child looked up.

Not at her mother. At Tayak.

The child's dark eyes found her across eighteen hundred years with the same total attention she'd given the pendant, and Tayak felt the ground tilt — not the ground of the vision, but the actual ground, the packed clay floor of the equipment shed, which had come back around her without her noticing when it came back. She was gripping the edge of the folding table with both hands. The padded transport case was open in front of her. The pendant's evidence bag sat in its foam cutout. The single bare bulb overhead threw its flat white light over everything and made it all look slightly unreal.

Her knees were not right. She sat down on the overturned bucket she used as a stool and put her hands flat on her thighs and breathed.

The child's eyes. The way they'd found her.

The woman's face — she had seen that face before. She had seen it in the shell gorget from Stratum Three, the carved face she'd told herself was stylized, generic, not a portrait. She had been wrong about that. She had known she was wrong about it the moment she said it. The face in the gorget and the face in the firelight were the same face, and the name the woman had spoken to the child was the name Tayak had carried her whole life, the name her grandmother had given her, the name no one outside the family had ever been able to explain.

*It's old,* Nora had always said, when Tayak asked. *It's just old.*

Outside, the tree crickets had started up in the dark — a wall of sound, steady and high, the way they got in late summer when the nights finally cooled below eighty. A truck downshifted on Route 301. The world was entirely normal and entirely impossible.

She looked at the field log on the table beside the transport case. The pen was there. She picked it up.

She put it down.

There was no entry she could write for this. There was no Munsell code for what she had seen, no provenience number, no way to record *child's eyes, eighteen hundred years, my name in a dead woman's mouth* in the format the university required. She had written nothing about the shell gorget vision either. She had two gaps in the log now, two places where the record simply moved on as if nothing had happened, and she was a scientist and she knew what gaps in a record meant.

She also knew what it meant that she couldn't close them.

She sat in the equipment shed for a long time. The crickets did not stop. The bulb hummed. Through the shed's single small window, the sky above the sweetgum and the oaks had gone fully dark, and she could see the faint orange wash of La Plata's lights on the low clouds to the north — the courthouse, the strip on Route 301, the housing developments pushing out into what had been farmland, the whole busy present pressing its light upward into the sky above the ground where someone had buried a pendant and spoken a name into a child's face.

She picked up her phone.

Her thumb found Nora's number before she'd decided to call it. She pressed the green circle and held the phone to her ear and listened to it ring once, twice — and then Nora picked up, and before Tayak could draw breath to speak, before she could find any word at all, her grandmother said:

"I know." A pause — not uncertainty, but the pause of someone who has been waiting a long time to say the next part. "I know what you found."

The phone was still warm against her ear. Nora had not said anything else.

Tayak sat on the overturned bucket and looked at the field log on the folding table and said, "What do you mean you know."

It came out flat. Not a question. She hadn't meant it to sound like an accusation and it had anyway.

"I mean I know," Nora said. Her voice was the same as always — low, a little rough at the edges, the voice of a woman who had spent thirty years in a classroom and never fully lost the habit of speaking to the back row. "I've been sitting here since supper waiting for you to call."

Outside the shed, the tree crickets filled every gap. Through the small window, the sweetgum stood black against the orange-washed sky, La Plata's light pressing upward into the low clouds the way it did every night now, the whole county's electricity lifting toward heaven whether anyone wanted it to or not.

"You've been waiting," Tayak said.

"Since you went down to that fifth level. Yes."

Tayak looked at her hands. They had stopped shaking while she was on the phone — the body doing what the mind could not, finding something to hold onto. She pressed her palm flat against the surface of the field log. The cover was black, spiral-bound, the same brand she had used since her first field season in graduate school, the kind that cost four dollars at any office supply store and lasted a summer. This one was nearly full. Eleven weeks of careful entries, Munsell codes and provenience numbers and artifact counts, the whole precise grammar of archaeological record-keeping — and two gaps. Two places where the pen had lifted and not come back down.

"Grandma." She stopped. Started again. "How long have you known what was down there."

A pause. Not the pause of someone deciding whether to answer. The pause of someone deciding where to begin.

"Sit down," Nora said.

"I'm already sitting."

"Good." Another pause, shorter. "Your name is old. I told you that."

"You told me it was just old."

"I told you what I could tell you when you were eight." A sound that might have been Nora shifting in her chair — the old wingback by the front window, the one with the armrest she'd reupholstered twice because she refused to replace it. Tayak put her free hand over her eyes. The bulb overhead threw its flat white light against the backs of her fingers and she could see the fine bones there, the tendons, the way the skin had thinned slightly in the last few years in a way she had not expected at thirty-eight.

"The ground told me," she said.

"I know."

"My name. She said my name."

"I know, baby."

The crickets. The hum of the bulb. Somewhere down the access road, a truck was working through its gears on Route 301, the sound rising and then dropping away into the tree line.

It was a small thing. Smaller than she'd expected when the trowel first caught its edge in the pale, compacted clay of the fifth stratum. The kind of object you could hold in a closed fist and no one would know you had it.

She had not held it. She had not touched it with her bare hands. She had used the brush and the wooden picks and the nitrile gloves, the way she always did, the way she had taught Wren to do, because the oils in human skin were acidic and the copper was already fragile, already thin in places where the corrosion had worked through, and you did not touch the things you were trying to save.

She had not held it and she had still heard her name.

"You need to tell me what you know," Tayak said. "Not what you can tell me. What you know."

Silence. Then: "Not on the phone."

"Grandma—"

"Not on the phone, Tayak." The way Nora said her name — not sharp, not evasive, but careful, the way you say a word you have been keeping safe for a long time. "Come home. Come home and I'll tell you everything. Write it all down when you get here — bring the log if you want. I want it written. I want it to last."

Tayak looked at the field log.

She had opened it after the vision ended, when the shed had reassembled itself around her and she'd found herself sitting on the bucket with no clear memory of sitting down. She had opened it to the next blank line — the line that should have read *Context 14: copper pendant, hammered native copper sheet, Hopewell-influenced, suspension hole, green malachite patina, dimensions to follow, condition fragile* — and she had held the pen above the page and felt the gap between what the log required and what had happened, and the gap had been too wide to cross.

She had sat there for a long time. Long enough for the sky outside to go from dark blue to black. Long enough for the crickets to reach full volume and then seem to get louder still, the way they did in September when the nights finally cooled and the insects played like they knew the season was ending.

She had closed the log without writing anything.

She had done this twice now. Once for the shell gorget, once for the pendant. Two gaps in eleven weeks of otherwise complete record. Two places where a future archaeologist, reading this log, would find the data simply resumed — artifact bagged, context number assigned, measurements taken — with nothing between the last entry and the next to indicate that the excavator had sat in the dark for an hour unable to hold a pen.

She knew what gaps in a record meant. She had written a paper about it once, in her second year of graduate school — a methodological piece about the epistemology of absence in field documentation, which her advisor had praised and which she had never entirely believed, because she had written it from the outside, from the position of someone who had never been the one who left the gap.

She believed it now.

"How long have you known," she said again. "Specifically."

"My grandmother told me." Nora's voice was steady. "The way her grandmother told her. It goes back, Tayak. It goes back a long way."

"How far."

"Come home."

She looked at the pendant in its bag. The green patina. The small drilled hole. The faint warping along one edge where eighteen hundred years of Maryland clay had pressed it slightly out of true.

Someone had worn this. Someone had put it around a child's neck and spoken a name. The same face she had seen in the shell gorget — the face she had told Wren was stylized, probably not a portrait, probably a conventional representation of a spiritual figure, the kind of thing you said when you needed a professional explanation and the professional explanation was not the true one.

She had been lying. Not to Wren. To herself.

"Okay," she said.

"Okay you'll come?"

"Tomorrow morning. I'll be there by eight."

"I'll have coffee."

"Grandma." She stopped. The thing she needed to ask was not a question she knew how to form yet. It was still a shape, not a sentence. She tried anyway. "Did you know it was this mound? Specifically this one?"

The pause was longer this time.

"Yes," Nora said.

The word landed the way a stone lands in still water — not loud, but with a weight that kept moving outward after the sound was gone.

"You knew," Tayak said. "Before I filed the permit. Before I started the survey. You knew what was there."

"I knew what the family always said was there."

"And you didn't tell me."

"No." No apology in it. No defensiveness either. Just the plain fact, offered steadily, the way Nora had always offered plain facts — as things that existed independently of anyone's feelings about them. "I needed you to find it yourself. There's a difference between being told a thing and finding it. You know that. You've always known that. It's why you became what you became."

Tayak sat with that for a moment.

Outside, something moved in the brush at the edge of the clearing — a deer, probably, slipping through the tree line the way they did at this hour, following the creek bed down toward the water. She heard the soft crash of something large and unhurried moving through the undergrowth and then nothing.

She picked up the field log. She held it in her lap without opening it.

She thought about the two gaps. She thought about what it would mean to go back and fill them — to write, in the precise required language, what she had actually experienced. She thought about what Voss would say if he read those entries. She thought about what the state's attorney would say. She thought about what a court would do with a field log that contained the word *vision* in the provenience notes.

She thought about the child's eyes. The way they had found her.

She set the log back on the table. She did not open it.

"Tomorrow," she said.

"Tomorrow," Nora said. "Drive careful. The deer are bad on 301 this time of year."

She hung up.

The bulb hummed. The crickets held their wall of sound against the dark. On the folding table, the field log sat closed, its two gaps sealed inside it like the spaces in a mound between one stratum and the next — the places where something had been buried, and the record had simply moved on, and the ground had kept what it kept.

She was not going to fill them tonight.

She turned off the light and walked out into the dark, and the sweetgum and the oaks closed over the path behind her, and the pendant stayed where she had left it, waiting in its labeled bag for the morning and whatever the morning required her to do with what she now knew.

She climbed out of the trench on her hands and knees, the way she always did at the end of a long unit — no dignity in it, just the practical business of getting her body from one level to another. Her knees left two dark prints in the spoil pile. She straightened slowly, one hand on her lower back, and stood for a moment in the late-afternoon light.

The site looked smaller from up here than it felt from inside it. The grid markers ran in their careful rows across the cleared ground, each orange flag snapping once in a brief wind and then going still. Wren's equipment cases sat stacked near the screen frame — the calipers, the battery scale, the tablet in its rubber field housing — all of it closed and latched, because Wren had gone back to the car an hour ago to download the day's GPS points. The screen frame itself held a thin skim of dried soil in its mesh, the last of the Stratum Five matrix she had sifted before the pendant appeared. The soil had dried to a pale, powdery gray-brown in the heat. It looked like nothing. It looked like dirt.

She did not look at the pendant bag on the folding table. She had already looked at it enough.

She walked. Not toward the car, not toward the water station, but toward the far edge of the cleared ground where the field gave way to the tree line — a ragged border of sweetgum and loblolly pine, with greenbrier vines looping through the lower branches and a stand of pokeweed gone to dark berry along the margin. The goldenrod was still up, yellow and heavy-headed, and it brushed against her pants as she passed through it. A crow called once from somewhere inside the trees and did not call again.

She stopped at the edge of the shade.

The air was cooler here by four or five degrees — she felt it on her forearms, on the back of her neck where the sweat had dried. From somewhere down the slope, in the wooded ravine she couldn't see, a creek was running. She could hear it: a low, continuous sound, underneath the cicadas, underneath the distant grind of a truck on 301. The creek had been there when the mound was built. It had been there when the pendant was placed. It was still running.

She stood there for a while and did not take out her phone.

Wren came back across the field from the direction of the car, the tablet under her arm, her boots raising small puffs of pale dust from the dry grass. She was reading something on the screen as she walked, the way she always did — not careless, just efficient, dividing her attention the way you learned to after two field seasons. She looked up when she got close enough to read Tayak's face.

"What happened?"

"Nothing." Tayak said it without deciding to. The word came out flat and certain, the way lies do when they are also, technically, true.

Wren looked at the pendant bag on the folding table. She looked at the field log, still closed. She looked at Tayak.

"The log—"

"I'll finish it tonight."

A pause. Wren's jaw shifted slightly — not a flinch, more the expression of someone absorbing a number that doesn't add up. She set the tablet on the table and began checking the latches on the equipment cases, one by one, the small deliberate sounds of someone giving another person time. But her eyes went back to the closed log once more before she looked away, and she did not say *okay* the way she would have said it if she'd actually accepted the answer. She said nothing, which was different.

"GPS synced clean," she said finally. "All forty-three points from today. I flagged the pendant's coordinates separately — I can run the LiDAR overlay tonight if you want the elevation profile before the morning briefing."

"Do that."

"Okay." Another latch. "You want me to bag the field log with the pendant documentation, or keep them separate?"

Tayak looked at the closed notebook. The cover was dark green, water-stained at one corner from the afternoon two weeks ago when the sky had opened up without warning and they'd run for the tarp with everything they could carry. The stain had dried in the shape of nothing in particular.

"Separate," she said.

Wren nodded and did not ask why. But Tayak watched her make a small notation on the tablet — a habit so ingrained Wren probably didn't know she was doing it — and understood that Wren was logging the fact of the closed log the way she logged everything else: quietly, completely, in a record that would not have gaps.

They packed the rest of the equipment in near-silence, the way the end of a field day usually went — the comfortable, tired quiet of two people who had been working the same ground for long enough that the work itself was a kind of conversation. The screen frame folded down. The calipers went into their case. The battery scale, still reading 0.00 on its small green display, went into the padded bag. The pendant, in its labeled plastic sleeve, went into the acid-free box, and the box went into the cooler they used for anything fragile, nestled between two cold packs that had long since gone soft and warm.

Tayak carried the cooler to the car herself.

The drive back to the motel took twenty minutes — two-lane county road, then a stretch of Route 301 with its gas stations and fast-food signs and the low flat light of late afternoon hitting the windshields of oncoming cars. Wren drove. Tayak watched the roadside: a church with a reader board, a fire station with its bay doors open, a stand of loblolly pine that had survived whatever had cleared the land on either side of it. The trees were tall and straight and very old, and then they were gone, and there was a housing development, and then another church.

She thought about what Nora had said.

*Come home and I'll tell you everything. Write it all down when you get here — bring the log if you want. I want it written. I want it to last.*

Not: don't write it. The opposite. Nora had spent decades carrying what Tayak's great-grandmother gave her, and the thing Nora wanted — the thing her whole private urgency had always been pointing at — was a record that would outlast her. A record that would hold up in a hearing room. The field log was exactly the kind of durable testimony Nora would want created. Tayak had understood this even as Nora said it, and the understanding had made the two gaps in the log feel different — not like choices she had made, but like absences she still needed to fill.

She would fill them. Not tonight. But she would fill them.

*Since before you were born,* Nora had said, on the phone in the shed. Tayak had not told Nora she'd called from the shed — she had not told Nora anything, had barely spoken — and yet Nora had known. Had been sitting in the wingback by the front window since supper, waiting. Which meant Nora had been tracking the excavation's progress the way you track a thing you have been waiting for your whole life, stratum by stratum, artifact by artifact, and when the pendant came up from the fifth level, something in whatever Nora was watching had shifted.

The question was not whether Nora had known. The question — the one that sat in Tayak's chest like a stone she had swallowed — was what else there was to know.

*She said there would be copper, and there would be a face you'd seen before, and the name would come to you the way names do.*

The face from the shell gorget vision. The same face, older now, in the longer vision she had not written down. A woman placing a pendant around a child's throat, her hands steady, her mouth moving. A name that had come through the strange acoustics of whatever the visions were — not heard exactly, more like recognized. The way you recognize a word in a language you were told you didn't speak.

Wren said something about the LiDAR profile and Tayak said yes, that's right, and Wren said something else about the morning's unit assignments and Tayak said fine, whatever you think, and Wren glanced at her once and then looked back at the road and said nothing more.

The motel was a two-story building set back from 301, with a parking lot that smelled of hot asphalt and a vending machine outside the ice room that hummed and rattled and never seemed to dispense anything. Tayak had been staying here for six weeks. She knew which step on the exterior staircase creaked. She knew the ice machine filled at two in the morning and woke her every time.

She carried the cooler up to her room and set it on the desk beside her laptop and her stack of county parcel maps — the GIS printouts she'd been marking up since the first week, the mound's position plotted against property lines and the LiDAR elevation contours, the creek drainage running south toward the Potomac just visible at the map's lower edge. She had looked at those maps so many times she could read the contours without the key. She knew this land the way you know a face you've been studying.

She had not known that Nora knew it too.

She sat on the edge of the bed and looked at the cooler.

The field log was in her bag. She took it out and set it on the desk next to the parcel maps, and she looked at the two objects side by side — the notebook with its water-stained cover, the maps with their careful lines — and she thought about what it meant that Nora had said *bring the log if you want, I want it written.*

Nora was eighty-two years old. She was losing words, sometimes — not often, not badly, but sometimes, a name misfiled, a date gone soft at the edges. She was racing something. She had been racing it for years, probably, carrying this thing that Tayak's great-grandmother gave her, waiting for the copper, waiting for the face, waiting for the name to come the way names do. Waiting for Tayak to be ready. And what Nora wanted, now that the moment had arrived, was not silence. It was the opposite of silence. She wanted the knowledge out, into a record, into something durable enough to survive both of them.

Tayak had been building an evidentiary case from one end. Nora had been building it from the other. The field log, with its Munsell codes and provenience numbers and artifact photographs, was exactly the kind of testimony Nora had been waiting for Tayak to produce. And the two gaps in that log — the shell gorget vision, the pendant vision — were the places where Tayak had flinched from her own evidence.

She opened the field log.

She found the first gap: the entry after the shell gorget, where the record resumed with measurements and bag numbers as if nothing had intervened. She found the second: the line that should have read *Context 14, copper pendant* and instead read nothing. She looked at both gaps for a long time, the pen in her hand.

Then she began to write.

Not *vision.* Not in those words. She wrote what she had observed — the quality of the light in the shed, the fact that she had lost track of time, the specific sensory details that had arrived with a clarity she could not account for through ordinary means. She wrote it the way she would write an anomalous reading in a soil column: here is the data, here is what it does not fit, here is the fact that it does not fit. She wrote it in the same hand she used for everything else, the same blue ink, the same careful spacing. She dated both entries correctly.

When she was done, the gaps were closed.

She sat for a moment with the pen still in her hand and the log open in front of her, and she looked at what she had written, and she thought: Nora will read this. Wren will read this. Voss will read this, eventually, and he will not know what to do with it, and that will be his problem, not hers. The record was complete. The record was true. Whatever happened in the hearing room, whatever Slade brought to the table, whatever the Maryland Historic Trust decided to do with a field log that described an excavator losing track of time in an equipment shed while holding a Hopewell-influenced copper pendant — the record would be there. Durable. Hers.

She closed the log.

She would bring it to La Plata in the morning. She would sit in Nora's kitchen and she would listen, and she would write down whatever Nora gave her, in whatever order Nora chose to give it, and she would add it to the record the way you add a new stratum to a site map: carefully, with full attention to what came before and after, leaving nothing out.

She set the log on the desk beside the cooler and looked at the two objects together — the notebook, the pendant in its acid-free box — and thought about the woman in the vision, her hands steady, the cord going over the child's head. *Not yet,* the woman had said, or something that meant that. But the child had looked up anyway. The child had found her.

She lay down on the bed in her clothes and looked at the ceiling and thought about what it meant to be the end of a line that had been pointing at this moment for two thousand years — and whether she was ready for whatever Nora was about to tell her.

Outside, a truck downshifted on 301, the sound rising and then fading south. Somewhere above the parking lot, a crow called twice — the same flat, declarative sound as the one at the site, or a different crow entirely, it was impossible to say. The vending machine hummed.

She did not sleep for a long time. But the log was closed and complete, and the pendant was in its box, and in the morning she would drive north to La Plata and Nora would have coffee and the record would be ready for whatever came next.
